# Universaali todennäköisyysraja

*Aihepiirit: [Suunnitteluteoria](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md).*


**Universaali todennäköisyysraja** on probabilistinen kynnysarvo, jonka on keksinyt ja määritellyt [William A. Dembski](william-dembski.md), joka soveltaa sitä [suunnitteluteoriaa](suunnitteluteoria.md) puolustavassa tuotannossaan. Dembski määrittelee sen "*epätodennäköisyyden asteeksi, jonka epätodennäköisempi [määrittynyt](../glossary/sanasto.md#määritys) tapahtuma ei enää järkevästi selity [satunnaisuudella](../glossary/sanasto.md#satunnaisuus), riippumatta siitä mitä tunnetun universumin probabilistisia resursseja otetaan huomioon.*"[^1]

<!-- Dembski asserts that one can effectively estimate a positive value which is a universal probability bound. The existence of such a bound would imply that the occurrence of certain kinds of events whose probability lies below this value can be rejected, given the resources available in the entire history of the universe. Contrapositively, Dembski uses the threshold to argue that the occurrence of certain events cannot be attributed to chance alone.

The idea that events with fantastically small, but positive probabilities, are *effectively* negligible[^2] was discussed by the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) <!-- red link: France --> [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician) <!-- red link: mathematician --> [Émile Borel](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Borel) <!-- red link: Émile Borel --> primarily in the context of [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology) <!-- red link: cosmology --> and [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics) <!-- red link: statistical mechanics -->.[^3]  However, there is no widely accepted scientific basis for claiming that certain positive values are *universal* cutoff points for effective negligibility of events. Borel, in particular, was careful to point out that negligibility was relative to a model of probability for a specific physical system.[^4][^5]

Dembski appeals to [cryptographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography) <!-- red link: cyptography --> practice in support of the concept of the universal probability bound, noting that cryptographers have sometimes compared the security of encryption algorithms against [brute force attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute-force_attack) <!-- red link: brute force attack -->  by the likelihood of success of an adversary utilizing computational resources bounded  by very large physical constraints. An example of such a constraint might be obtained  for example, by assuming that every atom in the known universe is a computer of a certain type and these computers are running through and testing every possible key. However, universal measures of security are used much less frequently than asymptotic ones.[^6] The fact that a keyspace is very large is useless if the cryptographic algorithm used has vulnerabilities which make it susceptible to other kinds of attacks.[^7]

Another objecton to Dembski's use of the universe probability is based on the existence of observed specific outcomes of an experiment with arbitrary potential outcomes.  For example, if a coin is tossed randomly 1000 times, the probability of any particular outcome is roughly one in 10<sup>300</sup>. For any particular specific outcome of the coin-tossing process, the *a priori* probability that this pattern occurred is thus one in 10<sup>300</sup>, which is astronomically smaller than Dembski's universal probability bound of one in 10<sup>150</sup>. Yet the *post hoc* probability of its happening is exactly one, since we observed it happening.
### Dembski's estimate

Dembski's original value for the universal probability bound is 1 in 10<sup>150</sup>, derived as the inverse of the product of the following approximate quantities:[^8]

* 10<sup>80</sup>, the number of elementary particles in the [observable universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe) <!-- red link: observable universe -->.
* 10<sup>45</sup>, the maximum rate per second at which transitions in physical states can occur (i.e., the inverse of the [Planck time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_units) <!-- red link: Planck time -->).
* 10<sup>25</sup>, a [billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scales) <!-- red link: Billion (word) --> times longer than the typical estimated age of the universe in seconds.

Thus, 10<sup>150</sup> = 10<sup>80</sup> × 10<sup>45</sup> × 10<sup>25</sup>.  Hence, this value corresponds to an upper limit on the number of physical events that could possibly have occurred since the [big bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang) <!-- red link: big bang -->.

Dembski has recently (as of 2005) refined his definition to be the inverse of the product of two different quantities:[^9]

* An upper bound on the computational resources of the universe in its entire history. This is estimated by [Seth Lloyd](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Lloyd) <!-- red link: Seth Lloyd --> as 10<sup>120</sup> elementary logic operations on a register of 10<sup>90</sup> bits[^10][^11]
* The (variable) rank complexity of the event under consideration.[^12]

If the latter quantity equals 10<sup>150</sup>, then the overall universal probability bound corresponds to the original value. -->
