# Agnosticism

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md).*


> There is enough light for those who only want to see, and enough dimness for those who do not.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^1]

Agnosticism is the view that no knowledge can be had of [supernatural beings](../articles/yliluonnollinen.md) (such as God or gods), or that the holder of the view does not consider himself to have such knowledge.

An *agnostic* does not claim that we know that no God exists. "Agnostic" can also mean a person who takes a position on the existence of God but does not consider himself to know the truth of the matter. In this sense, then, an agnostic can [believe in God](teismi.md) as a [fideist](fideismi.md) or be a [negative atheist](ateismin-määritelmä.md); agnosticism says nothing about a person's belief in God, since the agnostic denies only the possibility of knowledge.

Many religions, however, bear on a person's life, and one cannot live according to every worldview at once. Likewise, [natural knowledge of God](../articles/luonnollinen-jumalatieto.md) brings with it accountability to a moral authority higher than man. So in practice the agnostic has to choose: either he lives like an atheist, trusting that God (or other supernatural beings) does not exist, or at least that their existence has no bearing on his life, or he holds the existence of God, and some one religion, to be more convincing than the others.

The agnostic is thus forced to lean on one position about God more than on the others.

### Definition of the agnostic

On the proposal of **Thomas Henry Huxley** (1825–1895), an agnostic is a person who refrains from taking a position on the existence of gods for lack of sufficient grounds. T. H. Huxley coined the terms "agnosticism" and "agnostic" in the 1860s to describe his views: "I thought about it and invented what I thought a fitting name: 'agnostic'. It came into my head as suggestively antithetic to the 'gnostic' of church history, who wanted to know so much about the very things of which I know nothing. To my satisfaction, the name came into general use."[^2]

#### On the variants of agnosticism

**Agnosticism** is, in principle, the agnostics' position just described. There are, however, several different variants of agnosticism.

*Radical agnosticism* was represented by, for example, **Rudolf Carnap** (1891–1970) and **Alfred Ayer** (1910–1989). In their view, talk about God is meaningless and cannot be understood, so both theism and atheism must be rejected as meaningless claims.

*The weakest form of agnosticism* holds that no justified position can be taken on the question, at least for now, because the arguments are for the time being evenly balanced, so research on the subject should continue.

### Fideism versus negative atheism

Even if the agnostic is convinced that the question of God's existence cannot be settled in the light of empirical evidence, that by no means removes the question of God's existence from his life.

#### Natural knowledge of God

*Main article: [Children naturally believe in God](../articles/luonnollinen-jumalatieto.md)*

Since belief in God is a natural "factory setting" in human beings, the agnostic must at some point in his life have rejected this natural knowledge as a kind of childish illusion. People do not grow up to be agnostics without taking a conscious position on the existence of God and the knowledge connected with it. Remaining an agnostic requires holding on to this conscious position.

#### Moral realism and God

*Main article: [Moral argument](moraalinen-argumentti.md)*

The agnostic's skepticism toward knowledge about God is akin to skepticism toward moral knowledge.

Just as a person already as a child comes to form a concept of God even if no one teaches it to him, a person learns that murder is wrong as soon as he learns what the concepts "murder" and "wrong" mean. But just as a person can reject his natural knowledge of God as a subjective illusion, he can conclude that his moral intuitions tell more about his brain than about the ultimate moral nature of reality.

But if a person has no moral knowledge, he faces a moral risk analysis: if moral reality really exists and he lives without regard for moral norms, he will in practice be guilty of moral wrongdoing.

If, on the other hand, moral reality were an illusion but he strives to live according to its demands, he in fact does nothing either right or wrong.

This holds even for forming beliefs and avoiding false ones. Suppose a skeptic could show that we are not justified in believing in moral facts, and that believing in moral reality violates the norms that govern justifying beliefs. That conclusion itself, however, presupposes that moral reality binds human conduct, since on it forming false beliefs is morally blameworthy. If such an argument were sound, then by its own conclusion one would not need to care about it, since according to the argument there is nothing blameworthy in ignoring the conclusion.

If the agnostic is a moral realist (an Aristotelian or Platonist realist, for example), this raises the question of the ultimate ground of the unity of moral reality and material reality. If the ultimate nature of reality is a goodness beyond spacetime, on which spacetime itself depends for its continued existence, then this ultimate reality already has quite a few divine attributes.

#### Psychological pragmatism and worship

The agnostic has to decide by what moral principles he lives his life and by what values he orders the priorities of his reality, that is, what he exalts as highest in his life.

An intellectually honest agnostic may find it hard to avoid the conclusion that moral reality largely follows the path marked out by Christianity:
* Everyone has a duty to treat his neighbor as he would wish to be treated himself: otherwise he is a hypocrite.
* Everyone has a duty to tell the truth, or at least to avoid lying: otherwise he leads himself and others astray.
* Everyone has a duty to submit his conscience to correction by norms higher than himself: these include, for example, honesty, justice and love.
* Everyone who has grown to the age of moral awareness also knows that he has transgressed moral norms, and feels in his soul an accusation that he can at most muffle, but not arbitrarily dictate.
* One can be rid of this accusation of conscience only by abandoning one's wrong ways, confessing one's guilt and taking refuge in God's grace.
* If we hope to be forgiven our transgressions, then to avoid hypocrisy we must be ready to forgive those who have transgressed against us.

Christianity unites a message of raw honesty and of otherworldly hope.
* Raw honesty: honest humbling of oneself and confession of one's own moral failure
* Otherworldly hope: dwelling on one's own corruption in the wrong way leads to anxiety and depression, but in Christianity human moral corruption is always lit by the message of hope in God's otherworldly love and grace.

If a person acknowledges that these principles are good and wise, and means to live by them, he would do well to take seriously the fact that it is precisely Christianity that has given him this moral outlook.

If a person truly intends to order his life by these principles, he would do well to take Christianity as a whole seriously too. If one means to exalt truth and love as the highest values of one's life, how can that succeed without taking root in Christian life and tradition?

#### Risk analysis: Pascal's wager

*Main article: [Pascal's wager](pascalin-peliteoria.md)*

Since the agnostic cannot avoid orienting his life according to some position about God, and by his own admission has no sufficient evidential basis for making this choice, one essential perspective on the choice is risk analysis.

The mathematician Blaise Pascal was one of the founders of probability theory, and he framed the related wager as follows: by believing in God and seeking him with all his might, a person can attain eternal bliss and gain an infinite reward. If he happens to be wrong and God does not exist after all, he loses essentially nothing by trusting in God. If, on the other hand, a person lives indifferent to God, he gains nothing by being right, but loses infinitely by missing eternal happiness in communion with God, and must also pay for his wrong deeds with punishment after death.

Reason therefore says that such a person should rather take the risk of believing in God when God does not exist than believe that God does not exist when God does exist. At the very least, the agnostic should seek God with all his might.

### Agnosticism as withholding a position and as a position

Although agnosticism means withholding a position in the dispute between theism and atheism, it is itself a kind of position too.[^3]  The basic tenet of agnosticism can be stated as:

> It is not possible to know whether supernatural beings exist and whether the existence of the universe results from their benevolent or malevolent acts.

So this, too, is a proposition about supernatural beings, though not [ontological](../glossary/sanasto.md#ontologia) but [epistemological](../glossary/sanasto.md#epistemologia) in character. To avoid the burden of proof, the agnostic can moreover state the proposition in a subjective form: "I do not know whether supernatural beings exist and whether the existence of the universe results from their benevolent or malevolent acts."

Once a theist or an atheist has presented the agnostic with intelligible arguments concerning God's existence, the agnostic can no longer claim to know of no reasons to believe in God or in atheism unless he first takes some kind of position on the arguments presented.
