# Definition of atheism

*Topics: [Atheism](../topics/ateismi.md).*


Imagine being asked whether you believe in the existence of [Hattifatteners](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/hattivatti). You would probably answer "No!" So you do not believe that Hattifatteners exist. That is, you believe that Hattifatteners do not exist. According to some, the two previous sentences mean different things.

There is no consensus on the definition of atheism. Generally, what the different definitions have in common is either the view that gods do not exist or the rejection of belief in God.[^1] Atheism has appeared in history in different forms and on different grounds.[^2] This has contributed to the difficulty of defining atheism.[^3] If someone's [world picture](../glossary/sanasto.md#maailmankuva) is atheistic, he is called an *atheist*, and if it is theistic, he is called a *theist*.

It is fashionable to speak of [positive](../glossary/sanasto.md#positiivinen-ateismi) and [negative atheism](../glossary/sanasto.md#negatiivinen-ateismi). This distinction is used by, among others, [Michael Martin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Martin). To this formulation has been added Michael Martin's division into [positive atheists](../glossary/sanasto.md#positiivinen-ateismi) and [negative atheists](../glossary/sanasto.md#negatiivinen-ateismi).[^4] Positive atheists hold that the existence of gods can be regarded as demonstrably untrue. Negative atheists are those who think that no evidence at all has been presented for the existence of God or gods.[^5]

### Atheism is the denial of theism

A-theism, or non-theism, is literally the denial of theism.[^6] The definition of theism has hardly ever been disputed, so in this article atheism is defined through theism, that is, by denying theism.

#### Theism and atheism as beliefs

Atheism and theism can both be understood as beliefs with opposite contents.
* Theism is the belief that God or gods exist.

Atheism is the opposite of theism, and we can deny theism in two ways.
* Atheism is the belief that God or gods do not exist.
* Atheism is not the belief that God or gods exist.

On the latter definition, atheism can mean anything at all except theism, and it can be regarded as absurd and as not matching what the word means. Few speakers mean a shotgun by atheism, so the latter definition is a poor one.

#### Theism and atheism as propositions

Theism can also be understood as a proposition.
* Theism: "God or gods exist."

The theistic proposition can sensibly be denied in only one way, namely as follows:
* Atheism: "God or gods do not exist."

Other definitions have also been proposed. According to one definition[^7], atheism means the absence of belief in God, that is, the atheist does not believe in the existence of gods. The problem with this definition, however, is that it would make stones and computers, among other things, atheists.

### Ontological definition of atheism

As a proposition, belief in God, or theism, is the claim that a god or gods exist: *God exists*. The denial of belief in God is then the claim *God does not exist*. A person X who holds that God exists is a believer in God, a *theist*. A person X who holds that God does not exist is godless, an *atheist*.[^8]</br>For example, according to [George H. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._Smith) <!-- red link: George H. Smith -->, atheism is the proposition "there is no god or gods". [^9] This definition expresses an [ontological](../glossary/sanasto.md#ontologia) view, that is, a view about existence.[^10]

As positions on existence these are mutually exclusive: no rational person can be a theist and an atheist at the same time, because the claims would form an inconsistent set of beliefs.[^11] Some, on the other hand, think that one must refrain from taking a position for or against the existence of God. Person X then holds neither theism nor atheism. This position on the existence of God can be characterised as doubt. Even this view is nevertheless a position, one based on deliberation and the weighing of grounds. [Thomas Henry Huxley](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Henry_Huxley) <!-- red link: Thomas Henry Huxley --> proposed that this position be called [agnosticism](../articles/agnostisismi.md).[^8]

### Definition of the epistemological atheist

When atheism refers to an epistemic attitude (some person X holds that a god exists), belief in God can be denied in two ways. In Ilkka Niiniluoto's characterisation, the denial can happen "from the outside", in which case a-theism, or non-theism, is (as regards person X) *a lack of belief in the existence of God*. An "internal denial", by contrast, leads to a narrow definition, according to which atheism is *the view that gods do not exist*. Ilkka Niiniluoto considers the latter interpretation logically stronger in the sense that, assuming person X's beliefs are consistent, the latter claim implies the former, but not the other way round.[^12]

The philosopher [Michael Scriven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Scriven) <!-- red link: Michael Scriven --> has formulated the definitions of the atheist, the [agnostic](../articles/agnostisismi.md) and the theist as an [epistemological](../glossary/sanasto.md#tietoteoria) view, so that one's view on atheism, agnosticism and theism can come in degrees.[^13]

| View of the existence of God | Name |
|---|---|
| 1. Strong evidence against existence, no evidence at all for it | [Positive atheist](../glossary/sanasto.md#positiivinen-ateismi) |
| 2. No general or particular grounds or evidence at all for it | [Negative atheist](../glossary/sanasto.md#negatiivinen-ateismi) |
| 3. Some general or particular grounds for it, yet improbable | Sceptic, agnostic in the broad sense; although he considers the existence of God improbable, he considers it possible |
| 4. Evidence for and against in equal measure, probability 50 % | [Agnostic](../articles/agnostisismi.md) in the narrow sense, takes no position either way |
| 5. Strong evidence for it, yet inconsistencies and evidence against | Practical [theist](teismi.md), agnostic in the broad sense |
| 6. Strong evidence for it and no evidence at all against. Provable in the ordinary sense | Modern theist |
| 7. Directly provable by irrefutable facts | Old-fashioned theist |

Like Michael Martin, the philosopher [Paul Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Edwards_(philosopher)) <!-- red link: Paul Edwards --> has deliberately departed from the definition on which the atheist holds the proposition J about the existence of God to be untrue. He defines the atheist as a person who rejects belief in God. The rejection can then have grounds other than holding J untrue. Michael Martin notes that the concept of negative atheism is broader than Edwards' concept of atheism, since the concept of negative atheism also counts as atheists people who simply lack belief in God, and this lack of belief need not be the result of rejecting belief. According to Ilkka Niiniluoto, Kari Saari's definition of atheism as a lack of belief resulting from a lack of evidence is in this respect closer to Edwards than to Martin.

In Finland, defining the atheist as a denier of God has been opposed by, for example, [Kari Saari](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kari_Saari) <!-- red link: Kari Saari -->[^14] Reviewing in *Vapaa Ajattelija* 4/90[^15] the textbook by [Ilkka Niiniluoto](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkka_Niiniluoto) <!-- red link: Ilkka Niiniluoto --> and Anssi Kuusela for [secular ethics (elämänkatsomustieto)](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elämänkatsomustieto) <!-- red link: elämänkatsomustieto -->[^16], Kari Saari called this definition *"a very bad mistake"*; the atheist should instead be defined as *"a person who does not believe in the existence of God"*, so that atheism is *"a lack of belief resulting from a lack of evidence"*.[^17]

### Theistic criticism

As can be seen, what the attempts to define atheism have in common is the denial, rejection or absence of something. This matches the view of classical theology (handed down from antiquity) that sin is ultimately defined as the absence of good: only God is able to create, and Satan only to spoil what God first brought into being as good — first himself, then others and other things. According to another classical definition, *faith is open/opened eyes*; the atheist's spiritual eyes, by contrast, are shut as tightly as possible. In the light of classical Christian theology, the atheist understood in this way could therefore be defined as a person whom Satan has blinded so thoroughly that he lacks faith in God. This definition, however, presupposes Christian concepts, which the atheist of course also lacks.

Spiritual darkness also brings about a darkening of reason, which indeed comes out very clearly above. The whole idea that there is some "common atheism", whose definition just for some strange reason turns out to be terribly difficult, is clumsy. This is so obvious that it is almost admitted at the very start of the article: "Atheism has appeared in history in different forms and on different
grounds. This has contributed to the difficulty of defining atheism." The fault here is a kind of word magic: if, for some reason, views that were called by the same name in different historical situations but are otherwise different are to be bundled together without knowing beforehand what, strictly speaking, would unite them, then of course looking for a definition after the fact is a hopeless attempt; a further problem seems to be that no one could decide whether the purpose of the definition was mainly to classify only the views that have actually occurred in history or all conceivable views. In any case, it would have been better first to think about which ways of thinking one actually wants to talk about, then to try to find for each of them separately a linguistic expression that describes it as well as possible, and finally to give the meaning of each expression as clear and workable a definition as possible.

If, all the same, one wants some well-defined "atheism" to exist, and since it would have to be the denial, rejection or absence of something, it would first be necessary to define what the atheist denies or rejects, or what he lacks. "Belief in a god or gods" is not a well-defined concept: in antiquity it was precisely Christians who were accused of "atheism", since they lacked belief in the gods worshipped by the pagans living around them; these pagans, in turn, lacked belief in the God of the Bible, so Christians could just as well call them "godless", that is, "atheists". This really did happen, as documented at least in the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* (a detailed account of the martyrdom of Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna).

A concept that can mean either monotheists who do not believe in the polytheists' many gods, polytheists who do not believe in the monotheists' one god, or else people who in general have doubts, of varying degree, about the truth of some of their contemporaries' religious convictions can refer to more or less everyone, and therefore refers to no one in particular. So if someone wants to convert people into "atheists" although the concept itself has not even been defined meaningfully, the activity, seen from the outside, is as pointless as moving money from one pocket to another and back again on the grounds that one wants "to get money out of the pocket".

Even if there were no other evidence for the necessary existence of God than its deniers' evident inability even to state intelligibly what they actually consider themselves to be doing or not doing, or representing or not representing, the evidence would already be quite weighty: mathematics, too, uses indirect proof, where the idea is first, as a trial, to deny what one in fact wants to prove true, and then to show that this leads to an impossible situation. In this respect the theist apparently hardly needs to bother: the "atheists" do it themselves.
