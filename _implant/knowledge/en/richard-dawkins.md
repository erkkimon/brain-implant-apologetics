# Richard Dawkins

*Topics: [People](../topics/henkilöt.md), [Atheism](../topics/ateismi.md), [Richard Dawkins](../topics/richard-dawkins.md).*

**Clinton *Richard Dawkins*** (b. 26 March 1941, Nairobi, Kenya) is an English zoologist, [evolutionary biologist](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/evoluutiobiologia) and [populariser of the materialist view of evolution](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/yleistajuistaminen). He is also a [Fellow of the *Royal Society*](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Societyn_jäsenyys) (*FRS*).

### Career, achievements and assessments

After receiving his doctorate from the University of Oxford for a thesis on the behaviour of chicks[^1] in [1966](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966), Dawkins served from [1967](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967) to [1969](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969) as an assistant professor of zoology at the University of California, Berkeley. After returning to Oxford in [1970](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970) he lectured there on animal behaviour.[^2] He came to public attention in [1976](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976) with his book *The Selfish Gene* ([Finnish](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/suomen_kieli) *Geenin itsekkyys*), which popularised the [gene-centred view of evolution](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/geenikeskeinen_evoluutionäkemys) and introduced the concepts of the meme and memetics. In [1982](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982) he presented a theory of [phenotypes](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/fenotyyppi) in his book *The Extended Phenotype*.

In [1995](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995) Richard Dawkins was appointed the first holder of the University of Oxford's[^3] endowed "Charles Simonyi professorship of the public understanding of science" (*Charles Simonyi Chair for the Public Understanding of Science*). Because the condition of the endowment was precisely that Dawkins be chosen as the first holder of the post[^4] — that is, bypassing the normal academic peer-review procedure for filling a post — and because it is possible that he himself had influenced the endowment and its conditions, it is disputed whether he had a genuine academic right to the title of professor commonly used of him.[^5] Under the terms of the endowed post it is not to include any significant administrative or teaching duties at the University of Oxford; instead, its holder's main work is expressly meant to be wide-ranging participation in public debate in order to influence the general public, foreign travel included.[^6] Dawkins held this post until the end of [2008](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008),[^7] writing many well-known books on evolution and, among other things, appearing in numerous television programmes dealing with evolutionary biology, [creationism](../articles/kreationismi.md) and [religion](../articles/uskonnon-määritelmä.md). Despite the breadth of the subjects he has addressed, Dawkins holds no degree of any kind in [philosophy](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/filosofia) or [theology](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/teologia).<!--; professuurista luopumisen jälkeen hänen *CV*:nsakin on poistettu Charles Simonyi -professuurisivuilta.[^8]-->

Dawkins is a well-known [new atheist](uusateismi.md), an [opponent of theistic religion](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/antiteismi), a [humanist](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/humanismi), a [sceptic](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/skeptismi) and an influential commentator on science, religion and politics; indeed, his activity in these fields has prompted the comment that what he has really been doing is not popularising science but spreading atheistic materialism[^9]. His [official list of publications](http://www.simonyi.ox.ac.uk/dawkins/WorldOfDawkins-archive/Dawkins/Work/biblio.shtml) has, like his [CV](http://www.simonyi.ox.ac.uk/dawkins/CV.shtml), been removed from the Charles Simonyi professorship website, so the title of his doctoral thesis and official information about his other peer-reviewed scientific work are hard to find online, which has called into question his reputation as a significant scientist.[^10] His public activity has nonetheless made his name well known to the general public, so that in [2005](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005) he was voted third on *Prospect* magazine's list of a hundred well-known intellectuals[^11]; in the 2008 poll Dawkins was in 19th place[^12].

### The debate on evolution

Dawkins is known for his colourful and emotive way of speaking. One of his most quoted statements on the theory of evolution is the following:

> We can say with absolute confidence that if you meet someone who claims not to believe in evolution, that person is ignorant, stupid or insane (or wicked, but I'd rather not consider that).
>
> — Dawkins[^13]

**Phillip E. Johnson** criticised in particular the vague way this statement uses the concept of evolution:

> So we must either believe in evolution or go to a mental hospital. But what exactly are we required to believe? "Evolution" can mean anything from the generally accepted claim that bacteria "evolve" resistance to antibiotics to the grand metaphysical claims that the universe and mankind "evolved" through wholly purposeless mechanical forces. A word so elastic in meaning easily misleads by implying that we know as much about the grand claim as about the small one.
>
> — Johnson[^14]

### Dawkins uses the concept of evolution in a vague sense

*Main article: [Definition of evolution](evoluution-määritelmä.md)*

Dawkins's rhetoric often relies on his using the concept of evolution in a vague sense. To understand the scientific debate on evolution, then, one must always specify which of the many different meanings of the word "evolution" is intended in each case.

1. Evolution can simply mean "change over time". Evolution in this sense is not controversial if it is understood as microevolution, that is, organisms changing within certain limits. Darwin's studies of the finches of the Galápagos Islands, for example, belong to microevolution. The finch species are partly connected to one another through interbreeding, and they differ from one another in body size, colour, and the shape and size of the beak.
1. Second, evolution can mean the claim that all forms of life have a common ancestor. This is usually called macroevolution, especially when it is combined with the third meaning of evolution, the so-called "blind watchmaker theory".
1. According to the third claim, the so-called "blind watchmaker theory", evolutionary processes are without plan or purpose. Behind it is Dawkins's[^15] claim that "the only watchmaker in nature is the blind forces of physics – – natural selection itself is the blind watchmaker"[^16]. This claim takes a position on how the evolutionary processes mentioned in the first two points have taken place. They are supposed to have taken place entirely through natural processes such as random mutations, natural selection and gene flow. In other words, God has no part whatsoever in the origin or development of life.[^17]

As an atheist, Dawkins has no way of explaining the origin of life and the diversity of living things other than some variant of the theory of macroevolution. He is therefore forced to give it a higher probability than a theist would, who has other possible explanations as well. For the naturalist, the theory of macroevolution is the only possible explanation of the origin and development of life. So he must cling dogmatically to the theory of macroevolution, however weak its basis in research becomes as science advances. The naturalist's attitude to the theory of evolution thus easily becomes religious. It may be hard for him to approach the theory of evolution with scientific openness.

This lack of openness shows in Dawkins's comments on the theory of evolution. According to Dawkins, the theory of evolution is as certainly true as that the earth goes round the sun[^18].

One thing that hampers the debate is that Dawkins usually uses the concept of evolution in an undefined and vague sense: he does not distinguish between microevolution and macroevolution, any more than between guided and unguided evolution. Microevolution, that is, variation within a species or a basic group, can with good reason be regarded as a scientific fact, as certain as the fact that the earth goes round the sun. Dawkins, however, uses the concept of evolution more broadly, so that it covers both micro- and macroevolution. In addition, he reads into the theory of evolution his own atheist-religious conviction that all natural processes, evolution included, are unguided.

The evidence for variation within a species, that is, for microevolution, is so strong that microevolution can be regarded as a scientific fact that everyone acknowledges. The theory of macroevolution rests on extrapolation, inference and speculation, in which the facts of microevolution are extended to evolution proceeding from one basic group to another. The need for such an inference arises from the limits of a worldview starting point. For the atheist, development from one basic group to another is the only serious way to explain the present diversity of living things, so it has to be believed as firmly as microevolution, because otherwise there would be no explanation for that diversity at all.

But the need of an atheist like Dawkins to extend the scope of microevolutionary facts through speculation does not stop even there. Having first extended the facts about microevolution to macroevolution as well, he makes on that basis the further assumption that God has no part whatsoever in the life and development of creation. In this way he concludes that man is not a being created by God. This conclusion does not follow logically from belief in macroevolution, any more than macroevolution necessarily follows from the evidence for microevolution, but it is an essential part of the naturalistic approach and of methodological atheism.

Dawkins holds that evolutionary biology shows chance to play a significant part in human development, and that on this basis we can know that man is not a being created by God. Dawkins puts it like this:

> The facts tell quite the opposite, for the only watchmaker in nature is the blind forces of physics – – Natural selection, discovered by Darwin, is a blind, unconscious automatic process which, as far as we now know, explains the existence of life and the apparent purposefulness of the forms of life, but this automatic process has no conscious goal. – – If one wants to attach the image of a watchmaker to nature, natural selection itself is the blind watchmaker.
>
> — Dawkins[^19]

Here Dawkins makes five claims[^20]:

1. The forces of physics are the only watchmaker in nature.
1. The forces of physics are blind.
1. Natural selection is a blind, automatic process that has no purpose in mind.
1. Natural selection is the explanation of the existence of all life.
1. Natural selection is the explanation of all forms of life.

Natural selection is shorthand here for the whole neo-Darwinian mechanism, which includes natural selection, mutation, gene flow and so on.

First, note that Dawkins slides here, without anyone noticing, from the laws of physics that govern inanimate nature to natural selection, which operates within reproducing life, and treats natural selection as if it could be derived directly from the laws of physics. He gives the misleading impression that there were some scientific explanation of how the first reproducing cell came into being. Natural selection can of course act only once reproducing life already exists.

Second, Dawkins personifies the forces of physics and regards them as having creative powers. These forces are nature's watchmaker. The rhetoric of personification matters in this context, because it unobtrusively lends false credibility to an otherwise groundless claim: we find it easier to accept that a person has creative powers than that an impersonal force does. In addition, Dawkins says that these personified forces are blind. What does this mean?

In one respect there is nothing problematic about describing forces or mechanisms as blind. Quite obviously they are. The weak and strong nuclear forces, electromagnetism and gravity have no eyes to see with. Most mechanisms are blind. Think of a clock, a CD player or a computer's hard disk. They are not only blind; they also have no consciousness and no capacity to think. But although these mechanisms are blind in themselves, they have been built intelligently. This holds even for mechanisms whose working involves a certain amount of randomness.

The mechanism of a self-winding watch, for example, is blind and automatic and makes use of random processes: it uses the energy of the random movements of the wearer's arm to wind itself. But it would be foolish to claim that it was not designed. In fact a self-winding watch is more sophisticated than an ordinary watch, so its design has required more intelligence.[^21]

Dawkins's rhetoric is so skilful that he can pass over the fine-tuning of the physical forces, and the need for an intelligent design to explain it, without any arguments, simply by speaking of physical processes in personifying terms. He manages to create the illusion that the very existence of these fine-tuned physical forces makes it unnecessary to suppose that nature has an intelligent creator, although, reasonably considered, the matter is exactly the other way round: fine-tuned physical forces are hard to explain without an intelligent designer. Dawkins gives no intellectual reason for his own naturalistic claim; he does not even try to justify it. He relies entirely on the power of rhetorical figures of speech. In the phrase of the Australian philosopher David Stove[^22], he
tells "Darwinian fairy tales".

When scientific rhetoric describes evolutionary mechanisms as "blind", "automatic" and "purposeless", these terms are used vaguely, as if the question whether an intelligent designer is needed had been investigated and, on the basis of that investigation, rejected, although in fact nobody has managed to justify such a claim. Eloquence creates the impression that the question has been rationally dealt with and weighed, when it has simply been passed over and covered up with rhetorical figures of speech.

That there are evolutionary mechanisms that explain variation within a species does not give us the certainty Dawkins hopes for that God does not exist or that God did not create the world. On the contrary, the existence of these mechanisms suggests rather that nature has been intelligently designed. The capacity of organisms to vary is an important factor in their ability to survive when conditions change. It is hard to see how this would show that God did not create the world. The physicist John Houghton sums up this thought aptly:

> That we understand something of the mechanisms by which the universe and living systems work does not rule out the existence of a designer, any more than knowledge of the mechanisms of a clock, however automatic they may be, means that there was no clockmaker.
>
> — Houghton[^23]

In this case, however, Dawkins's need to see nature as detached from God leads him into circular reasoning. He thinks he can infer from the existence of evolutionary mechanisms that God does not exist only because he has first assumed that God did not create those evolutionary mechanisms.

If one adopts a theistic starting point, the same facts appear in a different light. The fine-tuning of nature fits better with the claim that God guides the processes of nature than with the claim that the processes are ultimately the product of chance. In Dawkins's case, however, the naturalistic worldview leads to the belief that the intricately fine-tuned ability of living things to adapt to their environment guarantees that God did not create the world. Such irrational reasoning is driven by the need to believe, and the need to convince others, that God does not rule reality. So at this point scientific reasoning is not religiously and ideologically neutral; it plainly depends on religious and worldview background assumptions.

Donald McKay is an expert on the brain's communication networks. He describes what has happened:

> "Evolution" began to be invoked in biology as a kind of substitute for God. And if in biology, why not elsewhere? Having first referred to a technical hypothesis – – the word was soon twisted to mean an atheistic principle about the ultimate nature of reality, an appeal to which could free one from all theological tremors before the grandeur of the universe. The word began to be written with a capital letter, and it was dishonestly decked out with the prestige of the scientific theory of evolution (which in fact gave it not a shred of epistemic warrant). "Evolutionism" became the name of a whole anti-religious philosophy, and "Evolution" came to be understood as a more or less personal deity, "the real power of the universe".
>
> — McKay[^24]

The biological theory of evolution does not necessarily lead to a naturalistic worldview. But what if we look at the matter the other way round? Suppose that the naturalistic worldview is true. Then it is logically necessary to give life some kind of evolutionary explanation, whatever evidence can be offered for it. What other possibility could there be on the basis of naturalism? Materialism is one variant of the naturalistic worldview. To make the point concrete, we can look at it from the standpoint of materialism. On the materialist assumption, matter/energy and the forces of physics are all that exists. So there is only one possibility: matter/energy together with the forces of nature has produced life. From naturalistic premises, some kind of evolution is the only possible explanation of the phenomena of life.[^25]

On his own premises the naturalist cannot make room for criticism of unguided macroevolution, because that would erode the basic certainty of faith on which naturalism rests. Something of the religious character of evolutionism shows in the way Dawkins regards the miracle-like events connected with the origin and development of life. In his book Sokea kelloseppä (*The Blind Watchmaker*), Dawkins openly states the religious purpose of the book:

> The purpose of this book is to show that no conscious designer is needed for the origin of life, just as none is needed for the other phenomena of the universe."
>
> — Dawkins[^26]

God is not needed because, according to Dawkins, the atheistic theory of the origin of life can postulate events so improbable that they can be called miracles: "our theory of the origin of life probably requires events that, seen from our point of view, are extremely improbable, even to be regarded as miracles"[^27]. Miracles "are just strokes of luck that happen to come our way"[^28]. The origin of life is so improbable an event that it can be regarded as almost a miracle. So there is no reason to expect it ever to happen in a laboratory.

> Obviously so small a probability gives us hardly any hope that the miracle of the origin of life would take place in our laboratory. If, however, we assume that life has arisen only once in the universe, our theory allows very large strokes of luck, because the universe has very many planets on which life could have arisen. Such an assumption is justified, because life demonstrably exists."
>
> — Dawkins[^29]

Because Dawkins has ruled out the possibility of God's work of creation, he can prove his own theory right simply by pointing out that life exists, so a stroke of luck that made the origin of life possible must have occurred. "A stroke of luck with a probability of one in a hundred billion billion is still acceptable."[^29] These events seem miraculous to us because "evolution has given our brains the ability to grasp the risks and probabilities that matter to a creature that lives for less than a hundred years."[^30]

To soften the implausibility of his own theory, Dawkins presents his final argument.

> I put forward a claim that is paradoxical, but for that very reason most interesting. I claim that as natural scientists we ought to be more worried than we are if the development of human consciousness did not seem to involve a miracle. Seen from the standpoint of our everyday life, an obvious miracle is precisely the part of the theory we are looking for when we explain the origin of life."
>
> — Dawkins[^31]

So Dawkins concludes that the apparently miraculous character of the origin of life is the strongest evidence that the origin of life is not the result of a miracle performed by God. The more miraculous things look, the less reason, according to Dawkins, we have to believe that we are dealing with a miracle of God. This can be seen as one of the showpieces of Dawkins's eloquence: he presents the very fact that the facts speak against the naturalistic theory of evolution as the real evidence for it.

### Is a possibility the same as a fact?

Dawkins seeks to show that there are no conclusive objections or evidence against the possibility that life could have developed solely as the result of natural processes, without God's guidance. On this basis Dawkins concludes that it is true that life has developed solely on the basis of natural processes, without God's guidance. We can put this reasoning in the following form:

Dawkins's starting assumption is the claim:

> 1. We know of no conclusive evidence against the possibility that biological life could have developed solely as the result of the natural processes put forward by Darwinian theory, without God's guidance.
On this basis Dawkins concludes:

> 2. It is true that biological life has developed solely as the result of the processes put forward by Darwinian theory, without God's guidance.
We can simplify this reasoning to the form:

We know of no conclusive evidence against the possibility that p.

> Therefore p is true.

We can make the problems of this reasoning concrete with the following example. You go to the payroll clerk at your workplace and tell her that the company's director has just raised your salary to 50,000 euros a month. The payroll clerk naturally asks for evidence for your claim. You can then offer her, as your justification, the fact that you know of no irrefutable evidence against the possibility that the director has raised your salary to this amount. Since there are no conclusive objections to this possibility, it is true.[^32]

Daniel Dennett, too, admits that the theory of evolution cannot show its claims to be true:

> The power of the theory of natural selection lies not in its ability to prove exactly how (pre)history went, but only in its ability to prove how things may have gone, taking as the starting point how things are.
>
> — Dennett[^33]

But in fact the theory of natural selection cannot show even what Dawkins and Dennett claim it can prove. They cannot give a satisfactory answer to John Locke's argument that it is impossible that "pure unthinking matter should produce a thinking intelligent being"[^34]. Locke held it to be logically impossible (in the broad sense) that intelligence could have developed from unthinking matter. In Locke's view there is no possible world in which there are intelligent beings but at some earlier moment there were none. Only intelligent beings can create intelligent beings. Like other theists, Locke thought that the ground of existence is an Intelligent Being, God, who has always existed. Many theists hold that God's existence is a necessary truth. The neo-Darwinian theory of evolution has not shown Locke to be wrong. It has not shown that God's existence is not necessary. It has not shown that it is logically possible for an intelligent being to arise from unthinking matter. It has not shown these things; it does not even discuss these questions. At best it merely assumes some answers to them.[^35]

The theory of natural selection has not shown that Locke is wrong. The theory of evolution of our time cannot show that it is possible for all the features of our world to have developed through unguided natural selection. But suppose for a moment, contrary to fact, that it were logically possible for an intelligent being to arise from unthinking matter. If so, is such a process of development biologically possible? The concept of biological possibility can be understood in many different senses. A state of affairs can be regarded as biologically possible if it is compatible with the laws of biology. Or, like Dawkins, one can regard as biologically possible a chance or "stroke of luck" that is not astronomically improbable. On the latter understanding, biological possibility means that a thinking being can arise from unthinking matter if the process in question is not astronomically improbable. Plantinga[^35], however, argues that the theory of natural selection cannot show, in any of these senses, that it is biologically possible for an intelligent being to arise from unthinking matter. This is because the theory of natural selection cannot show that God does not guide all the biological processes that take place in the world. If God guides biological processes, then the complex structures that result from biological processes, such as intelligent beings, are designed and created by him. So the theory of natural selection cannot show that the forms of life have arisen as the result of unguided natural processes.

So the theory of natural selection cannot show that macroevolution by unguided natural selection is biologically possible in the sense of not being astronomically improbable. Defenders of the theory of evolution can of course tell various stories about how various biological organisms could have developed by unguided natural selection. But they cannot show that these stories are not astronomically improbable. At most they can show that we do not know it to be astronomically improbable that biological processes of development could have proceeded in the way these stories tell. In other words, at most they can establish the following claim: if certain disputed assumptions about logical possibility are granted (that is, if it is assumed to be logically possible that intelligent beings have developed from unthinking matter), we do not know Darwin's theory to be astronomically improbable.[^35]

### Are there examples of [imperfect design](../articles/epätäydellinen-suunnittelu-argumentti.md) in nature?

Dawkins considers that he knows for certain that no intelligent designer would create such an "abomination" as the flatfish:

> The twisted and distorted shape of the flatfish's skull reveals its evolutionary history. So imperfect a skull is hardly the product of meticulous design; rather, its structure shows that it has arisen through gradual change. No sensible designer would end up with such an abomination on the drawing board if given a free hand to create a flatfish. Most competent designers would probably start sketching something like a ray.
>
> — Dawkins[^36]

Had Dawkins created the world, there would be no room in it for "abominations" like the flatfish. Dawkins is sure that God would not create an animal like the flatfish either, because it does not match his (Dawkins's) idea of elegant design.

According to Dawkins, the retina of the human eye, and of the vertebrate eye more generally, is badly designed.

> Any engineer would naturally assume that the photocells would point towards the light, with their wires leading backwards from the cells to the brain. He would burst out laughing if anyone suggested that the photocells should be turned away from the light and the wires placed on the side facing the light. Yet this is exactly how the vertebrate retina is built: every light-sensitive cell is wired in backwards, so that the nerve fibre leaving it is on the side of the light.
>
> — Dawkins [^37]

George Ayoub[^38], however, argues that in this case Dawkins's engineer looks at things too superficially. Placing the wires as Dawkins proposes would impair vision, among other reasons because it would not be optimal for the eye's oxygen supply. Oxygen is needed most in dim light.

### Is ultimate reality complex or simple?

Dawkins's naturalism includes a reductive aim of explaining everything from its simplest parts. He admits that he cannot in fact explain how the complex structures of the universe could have developed from their simplest parts, but he appeals to the fact that even very improbable events can happen if there are infinitely many opportunities:

> Some events are so improbable that they are almost miracles. But before we start talking about a miracle, we had better do a little calculating of the various possibilities. To make the calculations we need to know how much time has been available, or more generally, how many opportunities for the event to occur have been on offer. If there is infinite time or an infinite number of opportunities, anything can happen.[^39]

The Epicurean naturalists appealed to similar reasoning, but Dawkins's problem is that in the light of present knowledge there is no reason to suppose that the material universe is eternal. So, first of all, one has to be able to explain how the material universe came into being. Second, it is not at all clear that a living cell, which contains complex information and a system for copying information, could have arisen as the result of physical and chemical processes alone. Life even in its very simplest forms requires a complex copying machinery, which is a precondition of the ability of organisms to reproduce. "The problem now is that the copying needed for cumulative selection requires complex machinery, but as far as we know complex machinery can arise only through cumulative selection."[^40] On this basis Dawkins considers the claim that "in the beginning some designer was needed who was not blind but a far-sighted, supernatural watchmaker", one who "set going the original copying machinery that produces DNA and proteins".[^41]

Dawkins answers this claim as follows:

> This is an obviously shaky claim, and in fact it collapses on its own premises. Organised complexity is what we find hard to explain. Once we simply assume that some complex entity is available, for example DNA and proteins, the development of ever more complex entities is easily explained. – – To be intelligent enough to design a machinery that copies DNA and proteins, God must be at least as complex and as well organised as that machinery. – – Explaining the DNA and protein copying machinery by a supernatural Designer explains nothing in the end, because the explanation leaves the origin of the Designer open.
>
> — Dawkins[^41]

In his book *Darwin’s Dangerous Idea* Daniel Dennett quotes this statement of Dawkins's approvingly and calls it "an unrebuttable refutation, as devastating today as when Philo used it to trounce Cleanthes in Hume's Dialogues two centuries earlier"[^42]. Dawkins in turn[^43] later quotes approvingly this approving Dennett quotation of Dawkins, and adds that Dennett (that is to say, Dawkins) is entirely right.

In Dawkins's view, the strength of the theory of evolution is that it can explain how complex structures arise from very simple beginnings. He sees the problem of theism in its inability to explain complex structures except by postulating the existence of a being that is structurally even more complex, an intelligent Creator. The intelligence needed to create a living cell is even more complex than the structural complexity of the living cell, so, according to Dawkins, the problem of theism is that it does not explain the origin of intelligence.

It is not clear what Dawkins means by saying that God is complex. He surely does not mean that God is a complex physical thing or entity made up of different parts. He must mean that God's life requires complex intellectual structures, but these structures are presumably non-physical in nature. So suppose that the structure of God's intellectual life is complex. Does it follow that God needs something outside himself to explain him?[^44]

Since God is the origin of the universe, it would seem to follow from the very concept of God that God's existence is not the kind of fact that ought to be explained by appeal to some other fact. Most facts need such an explanation by appeal to external facts. The existence of an oak tree in a certain place, for example, is a fact that depends on where the acorn was planted.

Suppose that God exists and that he is, as Dawkins says, "a superhuman, supernatural intelligence who deliberately designed and created the universe and everything in it, including us"[^45]. If this is true, God laid down the laws of nature. They depend on how God laid them down. God himself is not subject to these laws. So on what could God's existence depend? If God exists, his existence is most sensibly understood as necessary. His existence depends on nothing outside himself.

The idea that God is a necessary being is not an artificial move invented to solve this problem, meant as a counter to a modern argument against God's existence. The history of thinking about God is almost unanimous in regarding God as a necessary being. It is hard to find a single exception to this, at least within the Christian theological and philosophical tradition.[^46]

Plantinga[^32] criticises Dawkins's reasoning with the following example. Suppose that space travellers land on a planet orbiting some distant star. On the planet's surface they find some machine-like objects that look like tractors and work like tractors. The leader of the expedition says: "This planet must be inhabited by intelligent beings who built those tractors." A first-year philosophy student in the expedition objects: "Wait a minute! You haven't explained anything at all!! The intelligent beings supposed to have built those tractors must be at least as complex as the tractors are." The other members of the expedition would probably tell the student that a little learning is a dangerous thing and advise him to travel back to Earth on the next spaceship and take a few more advanced courses in philosophy.

It is of course perfectly reasonable to explain the origin of the tractor-like machines on that planet by appeal to intelligent beings, even though those intelligent beings are more complex than the machines they built. In this case no attempt is made to give an ultimate explanation of complex structures in general. The aim is only to explain the existence of certain particular complex structures (those tractors). If one is not trying to explain the existence of complex structures in general, it is perfectly acceptable to explain the existence of one complex structure by appeal to an intelligent being who is, of course, more complex than the machine it built. When God is invoked as the explanation of the origin of life, no attempt is made to give an ultimate explanation of the existence of complex structures in general. The aim is only to explain the existence of one particular complex structure, the living cell. Even if God were structurally more complex than a living cell, he can legitimately be used as an explanation of the complex structures of the living cell.

Theists do not try to explain the existence of spirit or intelligence; it makes no sense to try to explain God's existence, because he is the ultimate reality from which everything gets its explanation. But according to Plantinga[^32], this cannot be used as an argument against theism. Intelligence needs an explanation if material particles are taken to be the ultimate reality and everything is to be explained from them. But if God, Spirit, the intelligent Creator is taken to be the ultimate reality, then intelligence needs no explanation; intelligence is the ultimate ground of explanation.

All explanations rest on some ultimate starting point. The materialist has no explanation for the existence of the ultimate particles: their existence simply has to be assumed. So to say that we need an explanation for the existence of intelligence is to dodge the real problem. All explanations have to rely on something that they can no longer explain.

Many theists regard the materialists' attempt to explain intelligence as the result of the gradual development of mere material particles as a complete failure. According to John Locke, for example, "it is as impossible to conceive that ever bare incogitative matter should produce a thinking intelligent being, as that nothing should of itself produce matter".[^34]

One problem in the debate on evolution has been that often no distinction is made between the biological theory of microevolution (an entity theory) and the naturalistic theory of macroevolution (a perspective theory). The biological theory of microevolution explains the variation found in biological organisms, and as such an entity theory it explains facts on which everyone agrees at the most basic level. At the level of immediate experience we can all observe that children differ in many respects from their parents and that gradual variation occurs from generation to generation. There is no dispute about this basic fact. When the theory of evolution is made part of a naturalistic perspective theory, one has moved on to viewing these facts from a particular worldview standpoint (perspective). It is then assumed that these gradual variations can explain the development of all forms of life from a common ancestor. The explanation of the facts of evolution from a naturalistic perspective can be summed up in the following quotations from Dawkins:

> Darwinism is indeed a very simple theory: it looks almost childishly simple compared with nearly any theory in physics or mathematics. In short, the essence of Darwinism is that hereditary changes appearing in offspring have far-reaching consequences if only there is enough time for these changes to accumulate. – – I also try to convince my readers that the Darwinian world view is not just accidentally correct, but that of the theories we know it is the only one that could even in principle solve the riddle of human existence. It is almost certain that Darwinism holds for all forms of life in the universe.
>
> — Dawkins[^47]

Everyone agrees that "hereditary changes appearing in offspring have far-reaching consequences if only there is enough time for these changes to accumulate". Children differ from their parents, and their children will probably differ even more from their grandparents. This is the domain of the biological entity theory. Different standpoints give different interpretations of what these far-reaching changes mean. Is it assumed that all phenomena of life reduce to the accumulation of microevolutionary changes, so that man has developed from a bacterium through gradual biological changes over billions of years, or is it assumed that, for example, consciousness and rationality are qualitatively different levels of life that cannot be reduced to mere biological changes? One moves into the naturalistic perspective theory at the point where it is claimed that all forms of life in the universe, including man with his rationality and morality, can be explained as the result of accumulated microevolutionary changes. The biological fact that children differ from their parents is then placed within the naturalistic perspective theory, according to which all phenomena of life must be explainable as the result of accumulated gradual natural changes, because no other naturalistic possibilities are known. One moves into naturalistic philosophy and religion at the point where it is claimed that the naturalistic theory is the only one that could even in principle explain the development of the forms of life. Naturalism accepts as explanations only those that appeal solely to natural causes. In this way creation theory has been ruled out from the start.

### The opposition of faith and knowledge

Dawkins is characterised by a highly simplistic opposition between "faith" and "science".

> "It is possible to argue that faith is one of the world's great evils, comparable to the smallpox virus but harder to eradicate. Faith, meaning belief that is not based on evidence, is the principal vice of any religion.
>
> — Dawkins[^48]

Science, on the other hand, is according to Dawkins "free from the principal vice of religion, which is faith".

Charles Taylor regards this view of Dawkins's as an expression of blind faith:

> The view that a scientist's work involves no assumptions that are not based on the factual evidence is surely an expression of a blind faith that cannot feel even the occasional unease of doubt. Few religious believers are so untroubled.
>
> — Taylor[^49]

### Is Dawkins guilty of telling fairy tales?

The atheist philosopher David Stove criticises Dawkins for using fairy-tale imagery. He takes as an example Dawkins's theory of "selfish genes".

Since, according to the basic idea of Darwinism, all living things strive to survive in a constant struggle for existence, altruistic behaviour has always been a problem for naturalistic thinking. Dawkins tries to solve this problem by defending two contradictory claims. On the one hand, he tries to explain the existence of altruism by the fact that every organism strives to secure the continuity of its own genes in later generations. So organisms behave altruistically towards their own offspring, because the parents' genes live on in the offspring. On the other hand, Dawkins tries to explain altruism as merely apparent, because altruistic behaviour is ultimately just a consequence of "the selfishness of genes".

One of the reasons Dawkins's writings are so interesting and attractive is precisely this unclear way of approaching the problem of altruism.

> If (for example) in reading [Dawkins's] book The Selfish Gene you cannot make out whether its author denies the existence of altruism between kin or offers a causal explanation of it (and in fact you cannot make it out), then the reason is probably that the author's thoughts on the subject are in exactly as inconsistent a state as his book. – – Moreover, if the book had said clearly and consistently either that altruism between kin does not exist or that it does, the book would have lost much of its fascination and sold fewer copies. Its inconsistency on this fundamental question — though it no doubt faithfully reflects its author's state of mind — is precisely one of the things that keep readers interested and guessing. It is at once the source of the readers' interest and of the author's income. Consistency would have cost the author money.
>
> — Stove[^50]

The vagueness of Dawkins's view lets him fend off criticism from whatever direction it comes. If Darwinian theory is criticised for being unable to explain altruism, one can always point out that Darwinian theory in fact explains the existence of altruism precisely by the drive to promote the spread of genes. If, on the other hand, Darwinian theory is criticised on the grounds that assuming altruism is incompatible with the basic idea of the struggle for existence, one can always reply that the theory of selfish genes in fact shows altruism to be ultimately an illusion.

According to Dawkins, "we are – – robots blindly programmed to preserve the selfish molecules known as genes". He says that "we are manipulated to ensure the survival of our genes". He takes it as a basic truth that "an organism is DNA's tool" and that "living organisms exist for the benefit of DNA". In these quotations Dawkins attributes to genes properties that make them in some sense divine. They are mightier than man, because they can manipulate man. Man exists for the sake of the genes. The genes are even in some sense eternal: "a gene – – does not grow senile; it is no nearer death when it is a million years old than when it is a hundred. It passes from body to body down the generations, manipulating body after body in its own way and for its own ends, abandoning the mortal bodies before they sink into senility and death. The genes are the immortals – – ." If Dawkins is right, "science has given us what the human heart has always longed for but never before attained: knowledge of beings so vastly superior to us that they deserve our reverence and worship"[^51].

According to David Stove, Dawkins has for his part helped create a religion in which genes are given many of the properties traditionally ascribed to God. Dawkins is a "polytheist" in that the gene-gods are very many. His religion differs from Christian theism in that his gods are not good but ruthlessly selfish. But they are like God in being mighty, purposeful, intelligent and immortal.[^52] Admittedly, Dawkins says a few times that genes are not purposeful agents. But for every such statement Dawkins has "a hundred statements that imply that genes are purposeful"[^53].

Throughout its history Darwinism has used purposive expressions to explain the adaptation of species, even though according to the basic beliefs of Darwinism one is not entitled to assume that any such purposiveness exists. Darwinists ought therefore to be able to explain how their purposive language should be translated and understood so that this purposiveness, illegitimate on Darwinian premises, is eliminated. Yet nobody has managed to offer such a clarifying interpretation. According to Stove, the representatives of the neo-Darwinian "gene religion" are in fact unwilling to clarify these basic concepts, because doing so would destroy the foundation of their religion.

> It is very easy to understand why the adherents of the gene religion are especially reluctant to produce a translation manual. For suppose the project could be carried through successfully: what would its effect on the new religion be? A simple consequence would be that Dawkins and people like him could no longer describe genes as selfish, as manipulating everything under the sun, as competing with their alleles for market share in future generations, and so on, while promising — but never delivering — a translation of those statements into "respectable language". There would no longer be any need, or any excuse, to describe genes in ways that imply purpose, intelligence and power. Everything Darwinists needed to say to explain adaptation could be said without expressions such as "selfish", "manipulation" or "competition". But this would, of course, mean that the new gene religion would vanish like a dream, leaving nothing behind.
>
> — Stove[^54]

According to Stove, this new gene religion is only a slightly refined variant of Darwin's original religion, according to which everything living is characterised by a purposeful striving to reproduce. Although Darwinism sought to remove purpose and design from nature by denying that nature is the creation of an intelligent Creator, it ended up bringing purpose back into nature by giving divine properties to material factors.

#### Sayings

* In an interview by Ben Stein, Dawkins admits that life could be intelligently designed, and that it would be possible to obtain evidence for this, but only if the designer were a humanoid civilisation that had itself arisen through a Darwinian process.[^55] Dawkins has also been upset that someone might conclude from the interview that he actually believes in designers from outer space.[^56]

* At a 2008 debate between Prof. John Lennox and Dawkins, Dawkins said that he considers a [deist](../glossary/sanasto.md#deismi) god rationally defensible, even though he himself does not find the claim convincing.[^57]
