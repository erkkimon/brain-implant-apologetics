# Miracle

*Topics: [God](../topics/jumala.md), [Miracles](../topics/ihmeet.md).*

A **miracle** is usually defined as an event that cannot be explained by the laws of nature.

### Do miracles happen?
If a miracle is defined as an event of a kind that does not happen, it is presumably entirely clear *[a priori](../glossary/sanasto.md#a-priori)* that miracles do not happen. If, on the other hand, it is defined as an event that departs from the laws of nature and is unpredictable, it (perhaps) cannot be tested experimentally. But most of history and of human action cannot be clearly predicted either, nor does it follow any clear laws.

Rope Kojonen summarizes research on miracles:

> The view that healing experiences are real has not been defended by theists alone. For example, the atheist **Jacalyn Duffin**, in her study *Medical Miracles* (2009), examines 1,400 miracle accounts confirmed by the Catholic Church. According to Duffin, the accounts rest on a strong basis, and in ruling out natural explanations and checking details the Church almost always followed the best scientific practice of its time. Also indispensable in the debate on miracles, whatever one thinks of the matter, is New Testament scholar **Craig Keener**'s detailed analysis of miracle accounts, [Miracles](https://www.amazon.com/Miracles-Credibility-New-Testament-Accounts/dp/0801039525). In the Finnish context one could of course also mention, for example, **Matti A. Miettinen**'s skeptical doctoral dissertation *Uskonnolliset ihmeparantumiset lääketieteellis-psykologisesta näkökulmasta* (1990) and **Ari Savuoja**'s dissertation in the philosophy of religion, [Mikä on ihme ja mitä ihmeistä voidaan tietää](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/14917059.pdf). At Areiopagi the theme has been examined by, among others, [**Vesa Palonen**](https://www.areiopagi.fi/2015/10/nain-lasket-ihmeen-todennakoisyyden/).
>
> — Rope Kojonen[^1]

### A critique of the critique of miracles
The criticism that atheists, materialists and naturalists level against miracles consists mostly of variations on a theme by [Hume](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume):
1. We have observed only natural processes.
1. The probability of a thing is roughly the same as the event [frequencies](../glossary/sanasto.md#frekvenssi) we have observed.
1. Hence the probability of a miracle is so small that it is not worth believing that one happened. It is much more probable that someone is lying.

This seemingly plausible reasoning, however, has serious problems.
In practice the argument claims that one should not believe that any rare event happened, but should instead assume that whoever reports it is lying. Yet critics of miracles usually use this 'argument' only against miracles. When you toss a coin, how many tails must come up before you are ready to claim that no coin can ever come up heads?

In fact every step of the materialists' argument is problematic. Below is a step-by-step critique:
1. There is no evidence that we have observed only natural processes. Materialists typically do not accept evidence for anything non-materialistic[^2]
1. This step is problematic because
  1. The things under consideration are usually a very mixed and heterogeneous set; they are not samples from one and the same distribution[^3], but each has very different underlying factors behind it. Changes in these underlying factors affect the probabilities very strongly, and a frequency derived from past events does not necessarily describe well the event now under consideration. The materialist would have to show that there are no underlying factors that would change the probability of the thing under consideration compared with earlier observations. For example, even if we had observed that no miracles occurred in the years 1–100, God might have a good reason to perform a miracle in the year 101, which greatly raises the probability that a miracle occurs in the year 101.
  1. Moreover, the division of past events into the classes from which the frequencies are calculated is rather arbitrary. Yet this classification substantially affects the outcome of the argument. For example, if we wanted to prove that Napoleon could not have been Emperor of France, we could point out that in hundreds of known realms over thousands of years there has never been a 168 cm tall ruler named Napoleon, so an Emperor Napoleon is extremely improbable and the people who tell of him are certainly lying. If, on the other hand, we wanted to make the existence of Emperor Napoleon credible, we could argue that in almost all known realms the rulers have had names longer than one letter and have been under three meters tall, so it is probable that the ruler of France would have been someone with a name of more than two letters and a height of under three meters. Napoleon is therefore a credible emperor.
1. Even if the probability that someone is lying were high, for example 0.1, the probability that someone is telling precisely the lie at hand is small [^4]. For example, let the probability of winning the lottery be one in ten million. Your friend tells you that Tauno Pentti has won the lottery. Do you conclude that your friend is lying because you know he lies in more than one story in ten million? You should believe your friend, because the probability that he is lying that precisely Tauno Pentti has won the lottery is much smaller[^5].

With respect to this argument, God's action is analogous to human action, even though God's mode of action is often unknown[^6]. If Hume's argument worked, it could be used to prove that it is not worth believing[^7] in the existence of a person A:
1. Clearly the great majority of all observed events in the universe have happened without any influence from person A; person A is not needed to explain them.
1. Hence the probability that person A has influenced the matter B under consideration is almost zero.
1. Hence the probability that person A has influenced anything at all, or that there are any observations of him, is so small that it is not worth believing. People who claim that person A influenced matter B are almost certainly lying. So we have no credible observation of person A, and therefore, in the absence of evidence, it is more reasonable to believe that he does not exist.

In short, Hume's much-used argument against miracles is a complete dud.

Miracles, then, are rare actions of God. If God exists, there is nothing odd about them: they are God's action just as human deeds are human action. Miracles are odd only if they happen without the help of God or some other supernatural person. Miracles are improbable only if God's existence is improbable, or if it is probable that He would not act in the situation under consideration.
An atheist who considers God's existence improbable will of course also consider miracles improbable, but this would seem to be merely a consequence of his belief that denies God's action.

### Miracles and the laws of nature

Many of the arguments against miracles ([Spinoza](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza), [Hume](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume)) rest on a conception in which a miracle is seen as a violation of a [law of nature](../articles/luonnonlaki.md). This conception is, however, mistaken, because laws of nature essentially involve [ceteris paribus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceteris_paribus) conditions, that is, conditions under which the law of nature holds. When God acts, 'all other things' are not equal, so the law of nature is not broken, because the conditions of its domain are not met when God acts.

The situation is analogous to the following: person A drops a ball to demonstrate free fall caused by gravity, but person B catches the ball. The ceteris paribus ('all other things being equal') condition of the regularity of free fall is not met, so the regularity of free fall does not hold. The event nevertheless does not break the law of nature governing free fall.

### Relevant details
