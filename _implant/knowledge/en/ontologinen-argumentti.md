# Ontological argument

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md), [Arguments](../topics/argumentit.md).*


The earliest and best-known form of the ontological argument was presented by [Anselm of Canterbury](../articles/anselm-canterburylainen.md) in the second chapter of his work Proslogion. Various versions of the argument were defended by John Duns Scotus (1266–1308), René Descartes, Baruch Spinoza and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.

Anselm sought to derive [the existence of God](jumalan-olemassaolo.md) from the concept of God and from certain necessary truths, such as "a thing is greater if it exists in reality and not merely in the human mind" and "the greatest conceivable thing is logically possible". According to Anselm, we understand God to be a perfect being than which nothing greater can be conceived. Since we have the concept "God", God exists at least in our minds, as an object of our thought. God exists either merely in the human mind, or both in the human mind and as a reality outside the human mind. But if God existed merely in the human mind, we could form in our minds the idea of a being greater than the being than which, supposedly, nothing greater can be conceived: namely, the idea of a God who also exists in the reality outside human consciousness. Since the concept of the greatest conceivable being, understood this way, would be internally contradictory, God cannot exist merely in the human mind. Therefore God exists not merely in the human mind but also in the reality outside the human mind.

### The course of the reasoning

**Stephen T. Davis**[^1] suggests that Anselm's argument can be stated systematically in the following form:

First, Anselm's argument rests on four premises:

> 1. Things can exist in two ways: in the human mind and in reality.
> 2. The greatest conceivable thing possibly exists in reality; in other words, its existence is not an impossibility.
> 3. The greatest conceivable thing exists in the human mind, that is, at least one person thinks of it.
> 4. Whatever exists in the human mind and could also exist in reality could be greater than it is.

Next, Anselm brings a fifth premise into the argument, which he seeks to refute by showing that it leads to absurd conclusions. He held that if the fifth premise is assumed together with the four earlier ones, a contradiction results.

> 5. The greatest conceivable thing exists only in the human mind.

The next three statements show how Anselm thought the fifth statement leads to a contradiction when combined with the four earlier premises.

> 6. The greatest conceivable thing could have been greater than it is.

The sixth statement is at least implicitly contradictory. Another way of saying the same thing is the seventh statement, which follows logically from the sixth.

> 7. It is possible to conceive of a thing greater than the greatest conceivable thing.

Since the seventh statement is internally contradictory, we must look among the earlier premises for the one that causes the contradiction. On this basis we can eliminate the fifth premise. So we can state the negation of the fifth premise:

> 8. It is false that the greatest conceivable thing exists only in the human mind.

And so we reach the conclusion:

> 9. Therefore the greatest conceivable thing exists both in the human mind and in reality.

### Criticism

#### Is existence a predicate?

Anselm's argument has been criticised in many different ways over the history of thought. One of the most common criticisms is that the verb "to exist" is not a predicate. Immanuel Kant sought to show that being is not a real property of things[^2]. Davis[^3], however, argues that being is a predicate. It is, admittedly, a predicate that in ordinary speech we often presuppose without stating it explicitly. The predicate character of being becomes evident when we begin to speak of things as possibly not existing. If I form a mental picture of a house I intend to build, the house of my imagination gains a new property when it is built in reality, when it really exists.

#### Does existence make a thing greater?

Anselm's argument assumes that being is a property that adds something to the greatness of things. Michael Martin argues against Anselm: "the assumption that God does not exist would not seem to take anything away from his perfection"[^4] The essential question here concerns Anselm's concept of "greatness". What did Anselm mean by the greatness of a thing? Anselm does not define the concept directly, but he gives some indications that by greatness he meant goodness. Davis[^5] suggests that Anselm's "greatness" can also be understood in the sense of power, ability or freedom of action. The greatest conceivable being would then mean an [omnipotent](jumalan-kaikkivoipuus.md) being. Applied to such a being, "being" is undoubtedly a property that adds to its greatness. An omnipotent being that exists in reality is undoubtedly far more powerful and capable than an omnipotent being that lives only in the imagination.

#### Can the argument be applied to other things?

A common way of trying to argue against Anselm's argument takes the form of a *reductio ad absurdum*: it is claimed that Anselm's argument could also be used to "prove" the existence of many things that we know for certain do not exist. Examples are "the greatest conceivable lost island" and "the worst conceivable being". Anselm himself answered this objection:

> I promise confidently that if anyone devises something, existing either in reality or in concept alone (except that than which a greater cannot be conceived), to which he can apply the logic of my reasoning, I shall find that thing and give him his lost island, never to be lost again.

How could Anselm show that his argument cannot be used to prove, for example, the existence of "the greatest conceivable lost island"? The essential point is premise 2 of the argument: "The greatest conceivable being can possibly exist in reality, that is, the greatest conceivable being is not an impossibility." Can the expression "the greatest conceivable island" be substituted into this statement? If greatness is understood in the sense of omnipotence, the statement becomes impossible. It is impossible to attach the property of omnipotence to an island. It is logically impossible for there to be "a most omnipotent conceivable island".

##### Why does the *reductio ad absurdum* fail?

It is not hard to explain why the *reductio ad absurdum* rebuttal is popular: it seems to offer an easy way of refuting the ontological argument. Using it, one does not in principle need to show exactly which detail of the argument goes wrong; it is enough to show that the reasoning as a whole goes wrong in any case, whatever the error hidden in it and wherever it may lie. In practice, however, building such a counter-argument successfully requires genuine fidelity to the structure of the original[^6] argument – otherwise the whole attempted rebuttal is watered down into merely constructing and mocking a caricature of the argument being opposed, that is, "burning a straw man".

Anselm [explains in detail](anselmin-jumalatodistus.md) how the idea "of that than which nothing greater can be conceived" can be formed. To validly show an error in his argument by the *reductio ad absurdum* method, one would have to be able to construct a line of reasoning that leads to a plainly false result and that, as an argument appealing to rational thought, would truly be of the same kind and the equal of the ontological argument. This requires understanding and reproducing the dynamics of the ontological argument, which means, among other things, that the concepts used in the reasoning must be clearly definable and, where necessary, also thoroughly explainable.

More precisely, one would have to find a concept X for which the following properties could be shown:

1. We know that X does not exist[^7].
1. X is nevertheless conceivable and could exist.[^8] This means that people[^9] in general are able to grasp X and talk about it, that is, to give an account of what X is (or would be) like.
1. In addition, X has some necessary[^10] property because of which it follows from X being conceivable[^11] that X also exists in reality.

If one now looks more closely at this list of requirements, one finds it contradictory: (2.) & (3.) => "X really exists[^12]", which contradicts (1.). That is why the *reductio ad absurdum* is in the end not a promising solution to the problem of refuting the ontological argument; rather, the error sought should be located and shown directly in the argumentation of Anselm and his followers.

##### Examples of failed *reductio ad absurdum* attempts

"The greatest conceivable lost island": the concept itself is rather vague – how great would it be, then? Why could an island greater than this not be conceived? Even more clearly, though, the requirement fails that such an island would have a property by which its existence in reality would follow from the possibility of conceiving it. One can of course try to justify this idea by pointing out that Anselm too used the **expression**[^13] "greatest conceivable", but on closer inspection it is easy to see that the [concept](../glossary/sanasto.md#käsite) Anselm meant here never referred to physical size at any point. Since in this case the whole alleged *reductio* counter-argument rests on changing the meaning of Anselm's words, the analogy between the two lines of reasoning is only apparent, and the ontological argument is not shaken this way.

"The worst conceivable being": there is something to this attempt, but on closer thought it is not a working counterexample either. For we do not know for certain that such a being does not exist, so condition (1.) is not met. Nor is it generally clear what "the worst conceivable being" would actually be like. No doubt it would want to do evil and nothing but evil, but would it want to do it to everyone equally[^14], or would it want to let some off more lightly so that others would be all the more miserable[^15]? And would it itself enjoy doing evil[^16], or would it do evil without getting any pleasure from it[^17]? So evil is not, in the conceptual sense, a simply maximisable property in the way the "greatness" or "excellence" used in the ontological proof is. The analogy limps at another point too: although it is easy to agree that any evil that exists both in the human mind and in reality is worse than if it were only in the human mind, this judgement comes from the concept of evil as such, not from its maximality: evil already contains, as a concept, the idea of something that ought not to exist. It is not indisputable, however, that a necessarily existing evil would be worse than one that exists only contingently: a necessarily existing evil would be in a position where it need not fear that its evil threatens the quantity or quality of its own existence[^18], so it is open to question whether it could ever be as ruthless in its evil as an evil that rushes into evil even at the risk of being held to account, whatever the cost. – All these ways of posing the question also illustrate that the concept of "evil"[^19] is not a simple symmetrical counterpart of the concept of "good" but is opposed to it in so many and such varied ways that it cannot, in the end, carry a line of thought as clear and convincing as the ontological argument.[^20]

#### The probative force of conceptual reasoning

A third counter-argument holds that it is not possible to prove the existence of any real thing merely by examining concepts or definitions. This objection is right insofar as no single human concept has a necessary connection with reality. Another, more fundamental question is whether human language and concepts can reach anything of reality at all. If one assumes that God does not exist, human rationality loses its foundation, and there is no reason to assume that human language and concepts are suited to describing reality. Anselm, however, makes the opposite assumption, according to which human concepts are suited to describing reality. In addition, he assumes that the concept of God has a necessary place in the human system of concepts and therefore also has a point of contact with reality. So the objection is uninteresting in that it merely assumes that Anselm's argument is invalid instead of trying to show why it is invalid. When existence is removed from the concept of God, the concept is given a content that takes away the ground of its meaningfulness. When God is given up, the ground for believing that human concepts and language can reach reality at all collapses with him.

### The argument and possible worlds

Contemporary philosophical discussion has paid attention to a somewhat different version of the ontological argument, which Anselm presented in the third chapter of his work Proslogion. There Anselm proposes that a being than which nothing greater can be conceived cannot be conceived as not existing. Therefore such a being necessarily exists. Building on Anselm's argument, Charles Hartshorne (1897–), Norman Malcolm (1911–1990) and [Alvin Plantinga](../articles/alvin-plantinga.md) have argued that God cannot be a contingent being who exists only in some possible worlds but not in other possible worlds. The existence of a perfect being is either necessary, in which case God exists in all possible worlds, or impossible, in which case God exists in no possible world. If it is possible at all that a perfect being exists, then God exists in all possible worlds and thus also in the actual world.

By Plantinga's[^21] definition, maximal greatness means maximal excellence in all possible worlds. He defines maximal excellence as omniscience, omnipotence and moral perfection in all possible worlds. Plantinga's argument can be systematised as follows:

> 12. There is a possible world in which maximal greatness is exemplified.
> 13. There is a possible world in which there is a being that is maximally great (derived from statement 12).
> 14. Necessarily, a maximally great being is maximally excellent in all possible worlds (by definition).
> 15. Necessarily, a being that is maximally excellent in all possible worlds is omniscient, omnipotent and morally perfect in all possible worlds (by definition).
> 16. Therefore, in our world and in all worlds there exists a being that is omniscient, omnipotent and morally perfect (derived from premises 13, 14 and 15).

The decisive premise of this argument is the assumption that the existence of a perfect being is possible. Insofar as this premise can be denied, the ontological argument does not prove the existence of God, although it does make belief in God's existence reasonable.[^22]

### On the nature of the argument

Stephen Davis[^23] reflects on the nature of the ontological argument as follows:

> The ontological argument aims at a complete knockout. If it is a formally and informally valid argument, and if its premises are true, then the being it proves really exists. The ontological argument is not a probabilistic argument resting on wide-ranging inferences from vast amounts of factual data. If the ontological argument succeeds, then God exists, and the matter is settled. Perhaps this is one reason why the ontological argument has always seemed so disturbing to many atheists, agnostics, or even theists (who reject the ontological argument but believe in God on other grounds). This connects with a phenomenon I have often noticed in students who study the ontological argument. I often meet students who say they are unable to refute the argument but are deeply suspicious of it. For one reason or another they cannot accept the conclusion of the ontological argument. So they say: "There must be something wrong with it."

The ontological argument rests on the intuition that human rationality and the ability of concepts to reach reality become incomprehensible if there is no perfectly rational and omnipotent being who created the universe. Human systems of concepts are left hanging in the air if they are not grounded in a rationality that has no human limitations. From the standpoint of theism it makes sense to assume that human concepts reach reality. On this basis some versions of the ontological argument seem promising. Within an atheistic frame of reference it becomes difficult in general to justify the connection between concepts and reality, and this scepticism is reflected in attitudes to the ontological argument as well.
