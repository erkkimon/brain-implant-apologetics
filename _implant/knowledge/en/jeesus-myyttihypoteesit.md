# Jesus myth hypotheses

*Topics: [History](../topics/historia.md), [Jesus](../topics/jeesus.md).*

According to the Jesus myth hypothesis, the Jesus described in the Bible was a [myth](../glossary/sanasto.md#myytti). There is no single Jesus myth hypothesis; rather, various views circulate around the world that meet the criteria of a Jesus myth hypothesis. What is essential to a Jesus myth hypothesis is the idea that the [Jesus of Nazareth](jeesus-nasaretilainen.md) described in the Bible is not a historical figure. According to some proponents of the Jesus myth hypothesis, a teacher called Jesus did live at the beginning of our era, but supernatural beliefs copied from older religions were later attached to his life.[^1] Others think the Jesus who appears in the Bible is an entirely invented character, and most often this camp, too, believes that Jesus' attributes and history were copied from other religions.[^1]

The credibility of myths among ordinary people rests on repetition. In popular sources, for example, similarities between Jesus and the leading figures of other religions are listed without any sources at all, as if the claims were so self-evident that no separate justification were needed.

The basic problem of the Jesus myth hypotheses is that, so far, hardly anyone has tried to defend them with evidence. Proponents of the various Jesus myth hypotheses mainly try to show that some of Jesus' attributes may have been copied, because those attributes already existed in religions before the beginning of our era. What is possible, however, is not thereby proven. The next stumbling block for the Jesus myth hypotheses is that the alleged similarities do not really exist at all. And the Jesus myth hypotheses fall at the latest on the fact that, in the light of historical research, Jesus of Nazareth does appear to have existed.

### History

Throughout the history of historical-critical Jesus research, mainstream scholarship has regarded Jesus as a historical figure, even though attempts have been made to mythologize, for example, his miracles and other supernatural deeds.

The roots of the Jesus myth hypothesis can be seen in the concept of the mythic hero archetype formulated in the early 20th century, on the basis of which it was possible to find loose similarities between Jesus and many mythical and historical figures.

#### History of historical-critical Jesus research

The study of Jesus from a purely historical interest in knowledge began with the work of Hermann Reimarus (1694-1768), who lived in the 18th century; it was published after Reimarus' death by Gotthold Lessing (1729-1781).[^2] The history of research is customarily divided into three "quests", during which the question of what kind of historical person Jesus of Nazareth was was examined:

* The 19th century (Life of Jesus research) [^3]
* The Second Quest [^4]
* The "Third Quest" [^5]

#### The mythic hero archetype

In the early 20th century, Otto Rank and Lord Raglan developed their views on the main points of the hero's life and plot arc that recur in mythical narratives; the typical hero who follows them is called the hero archetype or the [Rank–Raglan mythotype (*mythotype*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank%E2%80%93Raglan_mythotype). In his book *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909) Rank listed 12 typical traits in all, and Raglan listed 22 traits in *The Hero, A Study in Tradition, Myth and Drama* (1936), adding that the more traits a hero figure fulfils, the more likely it is that he is mythical, or that these biographical elements rest on unhistorical legends. The idea of the archetypal hero was popularized above all by the Jungian literary scholar [Joseph Campbell](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Campbell) in his work *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949).

#### Jesus and the Rank-Raglan hero typology

The Rank-Raglan traits attributed to Jesus are in particular:
1. an unusual birth,
1. a reputation as the son of God/a god,
1. an attempt to kill him soon after his birth,
1. flight because of the attempt on his life,
1. almost nothing is known of his childhood,
1. he loses favor with God/the gods,
1. he meets a "mysterious" (unusual) death,
1. any children he may have do not inherit his kingship (Jesus had no biological children), and
1. he has one or more tombs or tomb memorials

If the criteria are also stretched suitably, the following traits can be made to fit as well:
1. his mother is a *royal virgin* (vs. Jesus was **born of a virgin**)
1. his father is a king (vs. Jesus' earthly father **descends** from King David, and his heavenly Father is the almighty King)
1. he is raised by foster parents in a far country (vs. under the lead of Jesus' **foster father** the family moved to Egypt)
1. on reaching adulthood he returns to his kingdom (vs. Jesus *returns* with his family to live in Nazareth already in his childhood. On reaching adulthood Jesus began his public teaching ministry and **preached** the kingdom of God)
1. he defeats a king, a giant, a dragon or a wild beast (vs. Jesus defeated **death** and the power of Satan, who in the Book of Revelation is **depicted** as the Dragon)
1. he marries a princess, often the daughter of his predecessor (vs. Jesus receives a *bride*, that is, the **church**)
1. he becomes king (vs. on the cross Jesus is mockingly exalted as *King of the Jews*, which is in fact the true exaltation of Jesus as the King who has conquered death, but Jesus' kingship is not of this world: this **is not the same kind of crowning as king** as in a typical hero story)
1. he makes laws (vs. Jesus set out a new **interpretation of the law**, for example in the Sermon on the Mount, for "he taught as one who had been given authority, and not as the teachers of the law" ( ([Matt. 7:29](../books/mat.md))))
1. he is driven from the throne and the city (vs. an attempt is made to **forestall** Jesus' earthly kingship with a death sentence, for which Jesus is made to carry his cross out of the city)
1. he dies on top of a hill (vs. the Gospels do not actually say that Golgotha was on a hill, so the hill *is not part of the story*, though dying on a hill is possible. In itself, being *lifted up* on the cross is also dying **in a high place**.)
1. his body is not buried (vs. Jesus' **body is buried**, but on the third day **the tomb is found empty**)

With suitable interpretation a great many traits can be made to fit, but the more subjective judgment is allowed in applying the traits, the weaker the conclusions drawn from the type analysis become. If, unlike with other hero myths, symbolic and theological meanings are accepted here in addition to concrete matches, the usefulness of this set of criteria for telling a historical hero from a legendary one is radically weakened.

It is also easy to see that although, for example, Jesus' crucifixion fulfils the criterion of an unusual death, the same does not hold in the other direction: one cannot say that every mythical hero's unusual death took place by crucifixion. Likewise, it may be tempting to think that Jesus' virgin birth fulfils the *royal virgin* criterion well enough, even though Mary was not royal. But the same does not hold in the other direction, for in most hero myths this royal virgin by no means refers to a virgin birth, but simply to the royal maiden being a virgin when she marries! Similar problems arise at many other points as well.

#### The view of Jesus as a mythical figure

The term *Jesus myth* covers a wide range of ideas united by the view that the Jesus of Nazareth of the [Bible](raamattu.md) is not a historical person. The present theories arose in the 20th century in research that sought to establish a coherent biography of Jesus. [Max Müller](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Müller) argued that the roots of religions lie in mythical stories about the birth, death and rebirth of the [Sun](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurinko).[^6]

Among the first proponents of the hypothesis were the French Enlightenment thinkers [Constantin-François Volney](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantin_François_de_Chassebœuf) and [Charles François Dupuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-François_Dupuis) in 1970. The hypothesis has since been revived by, among others, [Earl Doherty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Doherty), [Robert M. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Price) and [George Albert Wells](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Albert_Wells), but the hypothesis carries no weight at all among historians and biblical scholars.[^7]

Jesus scholars working within science today are indifferent to the alleged similarities between ancient legends and the Gospels, because those similarities have been found to be groundless; this, however, has meant that laypeople who cherish the Jesus myth have been left free to spread their sensationalist propaganda.[^8]

In the 2010s the leading defender of the myth hypothesis has been [Richard Carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Carrier) <!-- red link: Richard Carrier -->, who received his doctorate in ancient history from Columbia University in 2008 and has formulated his myth hypothesis by means of [Bayesian reasoning](../articles/bayesin-teoreema.md)[^9]. Carrier argues that because Jesus meets so many Rank-Raglan criteria, the prior probability of his historicity is very low[^10].

This analysis of Carrier's has, however, been criticized as tendentious. The skeptic Johan Rönnblom compared the results when the Rank-Raglan typology is applied as literally as possible, and found that, looked at this way, Alexander the Great scores 13 points out of 22, whereas Jesus scores 8.5. He shows how Carrier, by conveniently altering the criteria, has obtained considerably higher scores for Jesus. Rönnblom illustrates how easily a creative interpretation of a measure of this kind opens the way to arbitrary results.[^11]

Daniel N. Gullotta reviewed Carrier's book in the *Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus* and also criticized the way Carrier uses the scoring of the Rank-Raglan typology:

"Per Carrier’s assessment of the Rank-Raglan hero-type applied to Jesus, Mark’s Jesus scores 14 and Matthew’s Jesus scores 20. But according to the traditional Raglan heroic archetype, Mark’s Jesus scores 7 or 8, and Matthew’s Jesus scores 8 or 9, producing a result that is less than 11"
[^12]

### Jesus as the fulfillment of an archetypal story

*Main article: [Myths of the dying and rising god](../articles/myytit-kuolevasta-ja-ylösnousevasta-jumalasta.md)*

The mythologies of several ancient pagan religions contain a story of descent into the underworld beyond ('[Katabasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katabasis)') and return from it. The Sumerian myth of [Inanna's descent into the underworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_of_Inanna_into_the_Underworld) is probably the oldest written version of this myth.

In the myth the "Maiden of Heaven" [Inanna](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inanna) descends into the underworld to seize power, but is taken captive, and once freed she has to send her husband, the shepherd [Dumuzid](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumuzid), in her place. Dumuzid too manages to escape, but in the end he may always spend half of the year in the land of the living and must return to the underworld for the other half. [^13]

The story offers a mythological explanation of why the growing season ruled by Dumuzid lasts only half the year. Dumuzid has counterparts in the mythologies of different peoples, for example Tammuz, Adon and Adonis, each of whom visits the underworld of the dead. The Egyptians, for their part, have an ancient myth of Osiris, who also dies, visits the underworld and comes back to life, and who has a corresponding role as the one who revives the harvest season. Some kind of [death and resurrection of a god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dying-and-rising_god) is thus a recurring theme in the world's mythologies already before Christianity, and similar myths are found in different parts of the world.

Especially in the early 20th century, the anthropologist and folklorist [James Frazer](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Frazer) and the psychologist [Carl Jung](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung) placed Jesus within this continuum of myths as some kind of demonstration that Jesus and his resurrection were unhistorical.

Rather, this is a narrative theme that belongs to universal human nature, the conquest of death and the longing for eternal life, rooted deep in the human psyche, and it is this longing that the message of the Bible answers. Jonathan Pageau points out that the presence of Christian plot patterns in the world's myths is a phenomenon that supports the message of Christianity rather than calling it into question:

> We find an example of what the story of Christ does to all stories. What he does to everything. That he at once transcends, he even abolishes the pattern. Yet at the same time he universalizes it, unifies it, grounds it, illuminates it. The pattern of descent into Hades and return from there is both transcended and fulfilled at the same time – and this is a transfiguration.
>
> And it is not only that, but suddenly all these old stories are seen implicitly through Christ.
>
> And there you see the hilarious irony of the anti-Christian 20th century: when scholars like Frazer use all the ancient myths you know of the so-called dying god to belittle Christianity, you notice that they are looking at all those myths through the lens of Christ. Christianity was such an earth-shaking event that many who oppose it cannot, even today, oppose it without looking through the glasses Christianity has given them.
>
> — Jonathan Pageau[^14]

### Alleged similarities

It is a common claim that significant figures of many religions rose from the dead. For example, a myth has been attached to [Mithras](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra) according to which he himself ascended to heaven to secure his followers' entry into the hereafter. Firm evidence that such a myth existed, however, is lacking. Oppositions associated with certain mystery religions – night and day, darkness and light, above and below – have been seen reflected in the writings of the New Testament. These, however, are vocabulary of everyday, ordinary language and as such prove nothing.

The mythical dying and rising gods differ from Jesus in at least three clear ways:
1. none of them died on behalf of others as Jesus did,
1. only Jesus died deliberately for sins, and
1. only he died voluntarily.
All of these attributes are essential to Jesus within the framework of Christian soteriology.

### Persons claimed as models for the figure of Jesus

#### Buddha

Many similarities have been claimed between the lives of the Buddha, that is, Siddhartha Gautama, and Jesus. The claim of the Buddha's virgin birth is plucked out of thin air. The Buddha had a father, whose name was Suddhodana.[^15] The historical sources give no indication that he was not also the biological father. The Buddha's virgin birth has sometimes been argued from the tradition that the Buddha's mother dreamed of a white elephant 10 months before the Buddha's birth. Putting an equals sign between the dream and a virgin birth, however, takes a rather lively imagination.[^16]

> Jesus is said to have been born on Christmas Day, like the Buddha

Jesus may well have been born on Christmas Day.[^17] The Buddha, by contrast, was born on the 8th day of the fourth month.[^18]

> The Buddha was visited by wise men who recognized him as a god.

The wise men did not recognize him as a god, but as a king or a holy man. One of them is even said to have foretold that he would become an enlightened one, that is, a Buddha. This should not be hugely surprising, considering that 8 Brahmins had been specially summoned to foretell the child's future.

> Jesus, as a babe in arms, told his mother that he was the Son of God.

The Gospels do not say that Jesus said anything of the kind. The Buddha may possibly have said so, but even so there is no similarity here.

> Resurrection

The Buddha's body was cremated after his death. The ashes were scattered here and there, from where they could of course rise into the sky on the wind, say. But this has nothing to do with an actual resurrection.

> Jesus went bodily to heaven.

The Buddha's spirit was released from the cycle of life into parinirvana. Here too one cannot really speak of a correspondence.

> Eternal, the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the end.

All the others, after all, are prisoners of the wheel of eternal rebirth, but the Buddha is precisely the one who was released from this eternal suffering.

> Jesus' second coming

Siddhartha did not intend to make any kind of return. Whether a next buddha is still awaited is another matter.

#### Dionysus

Dionysus was the god of wine and revelry. One myth about him tells that Zeus fell in love with a princess called Semele, approached her in human form and made Semele pregnant. Zeus' jealous wife, Hera, appeared in the guise of an old woman at the house where Zeus and Semele were, and so their doings were discovered. The disguised Hera persuaded Semele that Zeus was not really the king of the gods but only claimed to be so in order to get Semele into bed. When Zeus next came, Semele asked to be allowed to make a wish. Zeus swore to grant it. "Appear to me as you do to Hera when you make love to her," Semele asked. Hermes appeared in his heavenly glory and Semele was burnt to a cinder. The god Hermes saved her unborn child and took it to Zeus, who sewed it into his thigh. Three months later he removed the stitches, and Dionysus was born. Some interpret this to mean that Dionysus died and was born again. Read carefully, however, the myth says he was born only once.

Another version of the myth tells that Zeus lay with his daughter Persephone, who bore a son called Zagreus, also known as Dionysus. In her jealousy Hera incited the Titan monsters to attack the child. They lured the child to them, killed him, cut him to pieces and ate the body. The heart, however, was spared, and the goddess Athena brought it to Zeus. Zeus swallowed the heart and then begot a child on Semele, and so Dionysus was born again. The enraged Zeus then destroyed the Titans, from whose ashes mankind was born! This myth does not much resemble Jesus' resurrection either, nor does it seem credible that the New Testament authors drew on material of this kind.

#### Serapis

Under the Ptolemies a new cult arose in Egypt in which a deity called Serapis was worshipped. Serapis was a kind of composite god to whom attributes were attached from the Nile god Osiris, the god of medicine Asclepius, the supreme Olympian god Jupiter (Zeus), and the god of the underworld Pluto. Such a deity served the Hellenistic rulers' efforts at unification, because people of different backgrounds could see in it features of their own gods.

The Greeks also adopted elements of the widespread Egyptian cult of Isis, creatively transferring them to their own deities. Within the Roman Empire the cult changed and its original features disappeared almost completely. The Ptolemies shaped the Serapis cult out of it and other elements. As we saw earlier, the original Osiris died but did not rise. In the case of Serapis one cannot speak of death or of resurrection, because he was a blend of several gods. Nor does the Apis motif help much, because after its death the Apis bull became Osiris of the underworld, who did not rise from there
