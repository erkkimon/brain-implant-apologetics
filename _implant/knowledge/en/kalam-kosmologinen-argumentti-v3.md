# Kalam cosmological argument

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md), [Arguments](../topics/argumentit.md), [Cosmology](../topics/kosmologia.md).*

The various forms of the cosmological argument aim to show the existence of a sufficient Mind or First Cause (Latin: *prima causa*) based on the existence of the cosmos. The argument has been defended, among others, by **[Plato](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platon)**, **[Aristotle](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristoteles)**, **[Maimonides](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides)**, **[Anselm](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_Canterburylainen)**, **[Thomas Aquinas](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuomas_Akvinolainen)**, **[Descartes](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes)**, **[Spinoza](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinoza)**, **[Leibniz](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz)**, **[Locke](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)** and today, for example, by **[William Lane Craig](../articles/william-lane-craig.md)**, who wrote his doctoral dissertation on the subject.

*Image: According to the cosmological argument, everything that has a beginning also has a cause.*

In the Middle Ages, the Islamic theologian **[al-Ghazali](http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/al-ghazali/)** (1058-1111) condensed the claim for God's existence into a very simple form[^1]:
1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause of its existence.
1. The universe began to exist.
1. Therefore, the universe has a cause of its existence.

The first premise is quite convincing: it can be assumed to be true, for almost anything at all.[^2] The second premise, on the other hand, is a result of modern cosmology. According to the [*Big Bang* theory](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkuräjähdys), space and time have a beginning, before which there was no matter, space, or time. Speculative theories have been proposed that the known universe is merely one instance among several similar "bubbles". However, **Borde**, **Guth**, and **Vilenkin** have shown (BGV-[theorem](../glossary/sanasto.md#teoreema)) that in all such meta-cosmological models there is also a beginning, provided that space in them expands on average, as it almost invariably does.<!--lähde?--> Additionally, it can be [argued](../glossary/sanasto.md#argumentointi) that an actually infinite series of physical events is an impossibility,[^3] so on this basis as well, the universe must have a beginning.

So what would be a *sufficient cause outside of time* for all matter and space in the universe? A timeless, immaterial and transcendent, extremely powerful God.[^4]

### Everything [contingent](../glossary/sanasto.md#kontingentti) has a beginning and a cause

If some existing thing has a theoretical possibility of not existing, its existence can be considered to have resulted from something. According to common sense, everything that exists and has a beginning also has a cause. The entire [universe](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/maailmankaikkeus) is thus really a complex structure of various cause-and-effect relationships. So-called common sense is based largely on [analogies](../glossary/sanasto.md#analogia), i.e., comparisons made on the basis of the experiential world. Since in practical life we do not encounter things that would begin to exist without a cause, we assume that the same applies also at a more theoretical level.

Of particular interest to humans is precisely the beginning and cause of the universe surrounding us, because the reality we know is precisely this universe. Our thinking and the concepts we use in it are limited to the laws prevailing within this universe.

### The Beginning of the Universe

In the 1920s, astronomer **[Edwin Hubble](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble)** observed the [redshift](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/punasäteily) phenomenon, which, according to general understanding, indicates that the [universe is expanding](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universumi). This discovery led to the so-called [*Big Bang* –theory](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkuräjähdys)[^5]. This theory, currently prevailing in the scientific world, encompasses at least two basic theses:
1. The universe we know began with a great explosion approximately 15 billion years ago and has expanded since then.
1. According to the original theory[^6], before the *Big Bang*, all that exists was in a mathematical point that was infinitely dense. This point, located outside all [dimensions](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulottuvuus), contained all mass, energy, space, and time.

According to the second law of [thermodynamics](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Termodynamiikka), the so-called [law of entropy](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/entropia), the amount of available energy decreases in a closed system to which no additional energy is added from the outside. Consequently, even our universe most certainly faces a heat death (*heat death*). The cosmos therefore cannot be eternal, because otherwise it would already have met this heat death at some point.[^7] Thus, the fact that the universe is not by nature eternal but has had a beginning and has existed only for a finite period of time can be regarded as a scientific fact even independently of the Big Bang theory.[^8] The idea of an eternal cosmos goes against the [laws of nature](../articles/luonnonlaki.md) we know. A [theory](../glossary/sanasto.md#teoria), for example, of an eternally contracting and then again expanding[^9] cosmos is contrary to the laws of nature and thus impossible, at least if only naturalistic explanations are taken into account.[^10]

In our attempt to grasp the origin of the cosmos, we are inevitably forced to acknowledge both the limitations of our thinking capacity and, on the other hand, the incomprehensibility of the event itself. [Astronomer](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/astronomi) **Robert Jastrow** has interestingly noted the similarities between [creationist thinking](../articles/kreationismi.md) and the *Big Bang* theory:

> What is the ultimate solution regarding the origin of the universe? The answers given by astronomers are both divergent and significant. The most significant is the fact that in science, as in the Bible, the world begins with a creative act.
> Wiester, J.
>
> — (1983) The Genesis Connection, Nashville, Nelson, p. 24

If the Creator hypothesis is rejected, one must nevertheless accept the "[miracle](ihme.md)". As **[C. S. Lewis](../articles/c-s-lewis-v2.md)** states:

> An egg that does not come from a bird is no less natural than a bird that has always existed.
> Lewis, C.S.
>
> — [God in the Dock](../articles/god-in-the-dock-kirja.md), p. 211

God is the only entity whose existence has not begun, so He does not need a cause. If God had a cause, we would have to transfer God's "title" to that cause; thus, God's eternity is, in this sense, a quite [logical](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logiikka) attribute of the Creator. The problem of God's invisibility, which often arises in the surface-level philosophy of everyday life, is also related to this argument. God is not in our three-dimensional space, which we can perceive with our senses. After all, our senses are made to observe the phenomena of this universe. If God were perceivable by the senses, He would have to be part of the universe and explainable by the functioning of atoms. In that case, God would also be subject to the law of entropy, and it would be logical to assume that His existence began at some point and will end at some point. God would thus experience heat death!

Although fishing for scientific details in [the Bible](raamattu.md) is [hermeneutically](../articles/hermeneutiikka.md) problematic, many of God's attributes in the Bible are compatible with the [argument](../glossary/sanasto.md#argumentti) we have presented, which rests partly on the laws of physics. When the Bible speaks of God's imperishability and immutability, these descriptions can, of course, be understood relatively, for example, in relation to human lifespan. On the other hand, they also fit God's transcendence[^11], already mentioned, for which reason God cannot be subject to the same natural laws as creation. According to Hindu theology, by contrast, god is entirely identical with creation.[^12] Also in *[New Age](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_age) <!-- red link: New Age -->* philosophy, god is regularly defined as part of creation,[^13] so that every human being, too, becomes a god.[^14] This cannot be the definition of the Creator based on the presented cosmological argument. Cyclical conceptions of the universe's "rebirth" related to Eastern religions and *New Age* thinking are, as we stated earlier, problematic, among other things, due to the law of entropy.

### Criticism of the Cosmological Argument

#### Cause of the Cause of the Universe?

Perhaps one of the first objections to the cosmological argument is the question

> Does not the same apply to God as well – so what cause then brought Him into being?

But of course God does not have a beginning, but is a necessary being (see, e.g., [ontological argument](ontologinen-argumentti.md)) just as, for example, the laws of logic. God has always existed, so no cause needs to be presented for God's existence. Previously, atheists themselves argued that since the universe has no beginning, it does not need a cause.<!--lähde?-->[^15] Modern astronomy, however, showed this assumption of the atheists and thus the whole claim to be false.

#### Time also began

The renowned physicist **[Stephen Hawking](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking)** admits in his book *A Brief History of Time* that "as long as the universe has a beginning, we can assume that it had a designer". Indeed, Hawking seems to be trying in his book to get rid of a universe that would point to some kind of designer[^16].

> Although not much can be said with certainty about the initial state of the universe, it can be stated that as one approaches the initial moment, the entire universe was compressed into a singularity. According to general relativity, the universe was an infinitely dense and hot singularity before its expansion. Nothing can be said about the initial moment of this singularity, because at infinite temperature and density, the laws of physics we know probably do not apply. Since space and time emerged from the expansion of this singularity, it is not logical to speak of time before this singularity. Thus, it can also be concluded that this singularity is eternal, whereby the assumed eternity of the universe used as a counter-argument to the cosmological argument can be transformed into the form "energy has always existed".
> Hawking & Ellis
>
> — 1973[^17]

In this objection, *physics* is understood quite correctly, but the *concept of beginning* is understood incorrectly. The beginning of the existence of something does not require a preceding time period during which that thing was not in existence.[^18] In fact, the following holds true[^19]:
* The existence of thing <i>x</i> begins at moment <math>t</math> if and only if
  * <i>x</i> exists at time <math>t</math> and <i>x</i> does not exist in the world timelessly.
  * There is a time <math>t</math> when <i>x</i> exists, and there is no time <math>t^'< t</math> when <i>x</i> exists.
  * The existence of <i>x</i> is a temporal fact.

Furthermore, in the objection above, the concept of eternity is understood such that the thing has existed during all times of our universe. This is questionable, since normally eternity means infinite time. The objection perhaps owes much of its intuitive appeal to this shift in the content of the word "eternity", because infinite time would indeed remove the beginning.[^20] If one is content to speak of something having existed during all times of our universe, it is clear that since time itself also began[^21], the existence of that thing also has a beginning. The beginning of time thus, if anything, strengthens the Kalam cosmological argument.
