# Definition of evolution

*Topics: [Theory of evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*

This article deals with the different definitions of the term "evolution". The theory of how species developed and arose that rests on unguided processes alone is explained in the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md) article. The various known mechanisms of evolution are in turn presented in the [mechanisms of heritable variation](perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md) article.

Evolution is a [term](../glossary/sanasto.md#termi) that can be understood in many mutually contradictory ways. Especially in English-language texts, evolution can mean surprisingly different things. Even within biology alone, the word **"evolution"** can be used to refer to at least six different concepts,[^1] and biology dictionaries too usually define it in at least two ways that differ in content[^2]:

* Change over successive generations in the genetic composition of a population, which may be caused by natural selection, breeding, hybridization and mutations[^3]
* The sequence of events depicting the evolutionary development of a species – or of another group of related organisms; phylogeny[^4]

To minimize confusion, on ApoWiki evolution means exclusively "*changes brought about by [natural](../glossary/sanasto.md#luonnollinen) processes in a [population's](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/populaatio) [gene pool](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/geenipooli)*".

In this sense evolution is something hardly anyone denies. In this sense it must also be remembered that "evolution" is not the same thing as the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md) or [common descent](polveutumisoppi.md). In addition, the question of [the origin of life](../articles/abiogeneesi.md) is typically kept outside the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md), so that the theory of evolution seeks to explain only the development of life from the point when the first organism that can be classified as living appeared, one way or another.

### Problems caused by contradictory definitions

When evaluating the evidence for evolution, it is important to avoid committing a [false analogy](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virheellinen_analogia). If the evidence speaks for one kind of evolution, it may nonetheless at the same time speak against some other kind of evolution. Many well-known evolution skeptics, such as [Philip Johnson](../pages/phillip-johnson.md), [Paul Nelson](../pages/paul-nelson.md) and [Jonathan Wells](../pages/jonathan-wells.md), draw attention to how muddled the definition of the term is in debates about evolution.

> *"Evolution"* is a tricky word. If it means *"small changes over time"*, then even the most bone-headed fundamentalist knows that changes have taken place in the Earth's history. If evolution means the doctrine that *"all organisms descend from a common ancestor through unguided mutations and natural selection"* (this is the textbook definition of evolution), it raises difficult questions even among first-rate biologists.
>
> — Paul Nelson, Expelled: No Intelligence Allowed

Listed below are the four most commonly used definitions of evolution:[^1][^5]

|  |
|---|
| * **Evolution #1** *Change over time; natural history; any sequence of events in nature.*<br>> Natural history is not static. The branches of natural science that seek to understand natural history deal with evolution in sense #1 – as nature changing over time. Astronomers study the life cycles of stars, geologists changes in the Earth's surface, paleontologists examine changes in types of species over time in the rock strata, and biologists note ecological development over recorded history, for example a barren island becoming covered by lush woodland. Although the last example has little to do with the neo-Darwinian theory of evolution, it still fits definition #1 of evolution as a sequence of events in natural history.<br>* **Evolution #2**<br>> Evolution can mean that present-day organisms differ from organisms in the distant past. More precisely, evolution in this sense refers to [heritable variation](perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md) produced by natural processes. Even creationists accept that such "variation in the gene pool of a population" occurs.<br>* **Evolution #3**<br>> Some scientists associate the word "evolution" with the idea that all present-day organisms descend from one common ancestor. The claim is known today as [common descent](polveutumisoppi.md). This theory depicts the history of life as a branching tree.<br>* **Evolution #4**<br>> Other people use the term *evolution* for the cause or mechanism of development, that is, the biological process that leads to the development of species according to the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md), from a common ancestral form to today's diversity of species. The doctrine of universal common ancestry (evolution #3), together with the development (evolution #4) produced by heritable variation (evolution #2), forms the core of the neo-Darwinian theory of evolution. |

When readers meet the term "evolution", they should therefore ask themselves which of the definitions above is being used (or whether some other one is). Many critics of [neo-Darwinism](evoluutioteoria.md) focus on definitions #3 and #4. The discussion becomes muddled, however, when someone presents evidence for Evolution #2 in an attempt to prove Evolution #3 and #4. Conversely, others may discuss the problems of Evolution #3 and Evolution #4 and are therefore accused of rejecting Evolution #2. This is not true, since even those biologists who question the neo-Darwinian [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md) accept Evolution #2.[^5]

### The terms "microevolution" and "macroevolution"

Criticism of the theory of evolution has often made a conceptual division into micro- and macroevolution. In practice, however, using these concepts in discussion is problematic, because it is usually hard to reach agreement on the definitions of these [terms](../glossary/sanasto.md#termi). Microevolution means small variation, whereas macroevolution means something larger. The terms are meant to define the possibilities and limits of variation. For example, [Evoluutio – kriittinen analyysi](../articles/evoluutio-kriittinen-analyysi-kirja.md) (hereafter "E-ka") defines the terms as follows:

|  |
|---|
| * **Microevolution**: Evolution within existing structural characters; quantitative change in already existing organs, structures or body plans.<br>* **Macroevolution**: The origin of entirely new organs, structures and body plans and of the qualitatively new genetic material associated with them. The term "higher development" is used in this sense.[^6] |

As E-ka goes on to state, under these definitions the difference between micro- and macroevolution is not quantitative but qualitative. Usually, however, these terms are tied to taxonomic groups, so that microevolution means variation within a species[^7], whereas macroevolution means variation that crosses the species boundary and the origin of new taxonomic groups.[^8] E-ka in fact follows the taxonomic definitions of the terms by tying them to the concept of the [basic type](../pages/perusryhmä.md). Then "microevolution is all variation that stays within the bounds of the basic type. Macroevolution would be the origin of a new basic type from another."[^9]

A basic type consists of all the biological species that can be crossed with one another directly or indirectly (through other interbreeding species). These taxonomic definitions cannot, however, be tied without problems to the definitions of micro- and macroevolution given above. The known mechanisms of speciation always involve some kind of isolation of populations, not the origin of new structures, organs or body plans. Species diverge only once they have lived apart from each other for a longer time. Because of the definition, speciation at the level of the basic type does not differ radically from the speciation of biological species. By the definition, a basic type whose two internal parts interbreed with each other only through a single species can easily speciate into two new basic types. If this link joining the two parts of the basic type goes extinct, such an origin of new basic types would be macroevolution according to E-ka, even though it has nothing to do with the origin of new kinds of structures.[^10] Conversely, one can imagine a situation in which a new kind of structure arises within a basic type, but the biological species that gained the structure is still able to interbreed with some other species of the basic type. In this situation macroevolution would have taken place by the definition, yet it would still be evolution within the basic type.

So E-ka tries to tie the concepts of micro- and macroevolution to variation within a basic type and to the origin of new basic types. Because of how the basic type is defined, however, the attempt fails. With it fails the attempt to tie these definitions of micro- and macroevolution to the more traditional definitions, under which microevolution is evolution within a species, whereas macroevolution is the origin of new species or evolution above the species level.

In addition, the terminology E-ka uses carries an intuitive problem: the prefix micro- means something small, whereas macro- means something larger. If, for example, you have enough microscopic parts piled together, they can add up to a whole visible to the naked eye, that is, a macroscopic one. From the make-up of the terms it would thus be natural to claim that the difference between micro and macro is quantitative, not qualitative. Even so, E-ka uses these words to mean qualitatively different things.

To avoid terminological confusion, **ApoWiki tries not to use the terms micro- and macroevolution**. Instead of microevolution, one can simply speak of heritable changes that are merely quantitative changes in already existing organs, structures and genetic elements. Likewise, instead of macroevolution one can speak of heritable changes in which organs, structures or genetic elements arise that produce a new kind of function.

### Species and basic type

In discussing macroevolution, then, the rather vague concept of **species** also turns out to be essential. According to the **biological species concept**, organisms belong to the same species if they can produce fertile offspring with each other under natural conditions. By this definition, for example, the lion and the tiger, which interbreed successfully in a zoo, do not belong to the same species, because in nature geographical distance prevents them from breeding with each other. On the other hand, the horse and the donkey, for example, do interbreed, but the mule that results is almost always sterile, so they are regarded as different species. Questions of where to draw the line inevitably arise here: what kind of environment counts as natural conditions? Is a [nature park](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/luonnonpuisto) or a [wildlife reserve](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luonnonsuojelualue) still natural? What proportion of the offspring must be fertile? So the biological species concept is by no means self-evidently objective. To replace this vague species concept, evolution critics have proposed the so-called basic type model, under which all populations connected directly or indirectly through interbreeding belong to the same basic type. The hybrid does not have to be fertile; it is enough that it is viable.

### An example illustrating the problems of the terminology

By the most common definition, any change in a population's gene pool is evolution. So **every time an individual dies or a new one is born** and the genetic distribution of the population inevitably changes slightly as a result, this **proves by definition** that evolution is an empirical fact. A weakness of this definition is that it does not match the common picture in which evolution means development that makes organisms more complex. The definition is, however, established in the biological literature, so using it is justified.

This in itself would not yet be much of a problem,[^11] but problems start to appear when one and the same word "evolution" is seen to refer, very unclearly, now to this and now to that part of the theory of evolution. Sometimes evolution refers to the whole [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md), sometimes to [common descent](polveutumisoppi.md), sometimes to the "development" of a group of organisms[^12]. Then, as evidence for "evolution" in this unclear form, what is presented is again the first-mentioned evolution, that is, almost any [heritable variation](perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md).

Some supporters of the Darwinian theory of evolution hold that the division into micro- and macroevolution is artificial, because macroevolution is merely accumulated microevolution, so the two would differ in nothing but the amount of change. Macroevolution is then usually defined so that speciation, for example, falls within it. Under such a definition, macroevolution too is easily made an observed fact:
* If a [concept](../glossary/sanasto.md#käsite) is defined to cover 10 phenomena that have not been observed, then of course no phenomena falling under the concept have been observed, and the concept cannot be called an observed fact.
* Suppose, however, that as a result of terminological vagueness the scope of the concept changes as follows: the concept now also covers an 11th phenomenon, which has in fact been observed.
* Now the concept can be called an observed fact, since we have an observation of phenomenon 11, which falls under the concept.

This reasoning creates, confusingly easily, the illusion that everything included in macroevolution has been proven. So when some population of organisms, say after moving to a new area, merely changes its habits so that it no longer mates with the parent population in nature, it is declared a new biological "species" – and this is then supposed to prove that any change falling under "macroevolution" has been shown to be possible.

Above the species level, for example at the level of the order, it is in turn hard to get to discuss how plausible the supposed variation there is. For some reason evolutionary biologists have no term for it. In discussions of the theory of evolution it is often hard to get to the essential questions, because all the variation needed is in the end macroevolution, which has already been shown to be an observed fact.

The discussion simply states that all processes of variation, from the first "primitive" life to all the life forms that have appeared in the whole history of the Earth and the present, are in any case **the same** as microevolution, that is, nothing but the gradual accumulation of small microevolutionary changes over long periods of time. Once one believes this, it is easy to see that evolution above the species level needs no concepts of its own: after all, it "is" just "micro- and macroevolution" accumulated over the ages: change within species, speciation, changes within the separated species that arose, and new speciations again.

This way of thinking is encouraged by the image of the growing "tree of life": every sturdy branch visible today began as a delicate shoot from some tiny bud. When the branch arose it was quite unnoticeable; only over the ages did it gradually reach its present proportions, and it still grows and branches, but only slowly and imperceptibly, as always before. The direction in which the branches grow has no goal, and since it is wholly random, some grow "upward" and some downward. The branches that grew downward wither, that is, these branches go extinct. – Such a description is indeed an apt illustration of the character of the way of thinking that supports the theory of evolution, but it is no evidence that the theory is true. Yet it is used as "evidence" according to the following "logic": "Can you imagine the 'tree of life' growing like this? If you can, that shows that microevolution and speciation alone are enough to produce all the observed forms of life; if you cannot, that shows the fault is in you."

As a game of imagination, then, this juggling goes quite easily, but it does not survive closer inspection: it in no way follows from the slight variation that has been observed that the accumulation of slight variations could, as a matter of course, produce anything whatsoever! Although above we neatly managed to prove that all possible evolution falls under observed mechanisms, nothing at all was said anywhere about heritable changes in which organs, structures or genetic elements arise that produce a new kind of function. Not to mention ever getting to examine the organs, structures and genetic elements that living nature displays and that the theory of evolution ought to be able to explain. So these are mere [rhetorical](../glossary/sanasto.md#retoriikka) magic words, by which the reader is misled into believing the proof presented without critical evaluation.

There is, of course, better evidence for the changes the theory of evolution needs than this example may have suggested. Its purpose is mainly to illustrate the kind of unclarity ambiguous terminology can cause, and to give the reader a picture of the most typical misunderstandings that arise around the subject. For each piece of evidence it is of course worth assessing what it proves and what it does not prove, and often also what it was claimed to prove in the first place.

### Summary

The theory of evolution is surrounded by many unclear concepts, and using them in arguments easily causes confusion, even unintended confusion. Unclear and ambiguous terms also make the subject needlessly hard to understand, and they can hamper the analytical evaluation of the evidence when it is not clear what the observations presented were meant to prove. In general one may say that whoever controls the definitions controls all the thinking that takes place within them. A thinker who aims at precision should therefore avoid ambiguous terms and try to get round unclarities with less ambiguous expressions.
