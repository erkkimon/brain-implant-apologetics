# Methodological naturalism

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md), [Naturalism](../topics/naturalismi.md).*


**Methodological naturalism** is a methodological approach that has often gained the status of something self-evident in science, yet is committed to a particular worldview: it holds that everything that happens must be explained by natural causes. When this is combined with the view that the scientific method is the only reliable way to gain knowledge, the result is a view that entirely ignores even the possibility that God acts in the world or that anything could be known about him.

Methodological naturalism has been the prevailing view in science for the last hundred years, and it has also been a common view among Christian scientists.

### History

Great pioneers of science such as **[Newton](../articles/isaac-newton.md)**, **[Maxwell](../articles/james-clerk-maxwell.md)**, **[Faraday](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday)** and **[Boyle](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle)** were not proponents of methodological naturalism. Since then, however, such a restriction has been accepted into the mainstream of the philosophy of science. Yet the collapse of demarcationism in recent philosophy of science heralds a change to the prevailing situation.

### The most common justifications

The following justifications are usually given for methodological naturalism:
1. The success of naturalistic explanations in the history of science.
1. A common playing field for researchers who start from different worldviews.

### Problems

Methodological naturalism seems to be more a cultural myth and a gut feeling than a view supported by arguments.
The boundaries between [science](../articles/tiede.md), philosophy and theology are merely human demarcations for dividing up the descriptions of one single reality. No clear division can probably be found, since that would require reality itself to be divided.

The biggest problem with methodological naturalism is that when [science](../articles/tiede.md) is bound to such a strong presupposition, science ceases to be a search for truth and its explanations are steered to fit the presupposition. As the domain of science expands, reality too comes to be seen as conforming to the naturalistic presupposition that was made. All presuppositions are therefore quite dangerous in science, even though they are often made, and they ought to be justified especially strongly to be credible.[^1]

#### The problem of definition

In reality it is very hard to define what a naturalistic, or "natural", explanation is. This depends a great deal on a person's worldview. For a Christian, for example, a special intervention by God may in some cases[^2] be the "natural"[^3] explanation of the observations made.

In the current situation, a naturalistic explanation seems in practice to be equated with a material explanation or with a law of nature. Unfortunately, both the concept of "materiality" and that of a "law of nature" are also open to interpretation. In practice, "material" means those entities whose existence present-day physics presupposes. It follows that anything on which physics is built, or could be built, can be natural. Today it is fashionable in physics to believe in dark energy and dark matter, for which there are only disputed[^4], indirect observations. There can be indirect observations of the supernatural too. The best definition of a "law of nature" so far is that a law of nature is apparently something that makes observed events recur in a certain way when certain other things are sufficiently uniform. Almost anything fits under this definition.

#### The success of naturalistic explanations

The ideological tailwind methodological naturalism has enjoyed so far would seem to reflect mainly just this: in most natural phenomena already regarded as satisfactorily explained, such as the effects of gravity, chemical reactions and so on, a design explanation has not been needed for predictions, so it has been sensible to leave it out of the calculations.[^5] But in these contexts, refraining from seeking non-naturalistic explanations has not been based on the demands of methodological naturalism but on the criterion of predictive power (which is not an absolute criterion either).

There are, however, already cases for which no satisfactory naturalistic explanation exists or is even in sight. It is therefore a crude oversimplification when the advocates of methodological naturalism still infer, from the fact that appeals to God's direct action have usually not been needed in scientific explanation before, that appealing to God's action is not admissible at all in the scientific explanation of anything. After all, quarks were not used as an explanation either before quantum chromodynamics was devised.

God's action, or in general that of some otherwise unknown but sufficiently powerful intelligent designer, should therefore be an acceptable scientific explanation when, according to the best examination available in an open space of discussion, it really does predict something better than other explanations. It should also be accepted that even though a considerable share of today's researchers, being personally committed to methodological naturalism, could not even imagine ever publishing a research result of this kind, such ideological commitment should be voluntary for every researcher, not imposed from outside by "professional group discipline".

#### Repeatability

An essential difference between the ways a designer and the laws of nature act is at least repeatability: a designer does not necessarily, and probably does not, slavishly repeat the same action in the same situation. Insofar as psychology, archaeology, history and in general the disciplines that do not use repeated experiments can be classified as science, [ID](suunnitteluteoria.md) and the God explanation are acceptable science all the same.

Giving up the requirement of repeatability is nothing new in natural-history research either, since even within methodological naturalism it is taken as axiomatic that the past phenomena under study, which have essentially shaped the present situation, would not recur in the same way in an imagined rerun of the experiment. Rejecting purposive explanations by appealing to their unpredictability therefore looks more like an intellectually dishonest rhetorical trick than a genuinely scientific critique of design explanations: after all, no methodological problem has been seen in appealing to the effects of unrepeatable, "astronomically improbable" purposeless chance.

#### A common field of discussion

The claim that methodological naturalism gives science the common field of discussion it needs, on which researchers starting from different worldviews can discuss matters, is misleading. There is no reason why one worldview, naturalism, should be privileged as the common field of discussion for everyone. Rather, the common field of discussion should be made up of shared rules of inference, probability calculus and other criteria of sound reasoning. What unites the practice of science should thus be a "common language" rather than a "common mind" hammered into shape along the educational pipeline by means of a collective compulsory ideology.

In this way researchers who represent different worldviews and draw inspiration from them could examine more neutrally even their own and one another's worldviews, the influence of these on interpretation and, conversely, which worldview yields the theory that in each case ultimately gives the most natural interpretation of the observations.

### Social effects

Methodological naturalism, then, as a demarcation criterion both for reality and for the science that studies it, is ultimately an entirely unfounded simplification. By applying it, a Christian can admittedly, on the surface, concentrate on doing science without disruptive, or even career-destroying, conflicts with atheist colleagues. At the same time, however, whoever applies it hands over the public forum that was within reach, and all the resources society has allocated to their own research, in an area of knowledge significant to them, to a worldview alien to them, in this case naturalism, completely free of charge.[^6]

The biggest problem with methodological naturalism is that when science is bound to such a strong presupposition, science ceases to be a search for truth and its explanations are steered to fit the presupposition. As the domain of science expands and its results become the starting point for both school teaching and political decision-making, the reality it describes (subject to its own methodological restrictions) also comes to be seen, in other than research contexts too, as quietly conforming to the naturalistic presupposition that was at work in the background. Methodological naturalism can thus be seen as a major, if not the major, cause of the secularization of Western thought.[^7]

### The theistic alternative

The Christian alternative for doing science is **Christian scientific realism**, in which God and design are used without inhibition as an explanation when that is a good explanation, that is, when it has explanatory power, makes sufficiently good predictions, or the existing alternatives are implausible. This is ordinary comparison of hypotheses, in which the probability of hypothesis A increases when A's own prediction of the observations improves or when a competing hypothesis's prediction of the observations worsens.

As always in science, a direct God explanation of a particular thing is provisional too, and if better explanations appear, the direct God explanation can be given up for that thing. That is how science works. At the same time, the theistic line of research develops the predictions of the God explanation and exposes further problems in naturalistic explanations. A Christian realist can be honest about the problems of naturalistic explanations, and can expose the arbitrary assumptions behind them, which in turn lower the probability of those explanations. Contrary to naturalistic suspicions, then, the legitimacy of theistic explanations in science by no means implies abandoning critical thinking and thus a fatal decline in the standard of science; on the contrary, it implies greater scientific rigor and a resulting rise in the standard of science. An ideological monopoly does no good to anything, especially in the long run, and least of all to science.

A [theistic realist](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teistinen_realismi), then, does not use God's direct action as the explanation for everything, but only for those things that are currently best explained by God's direct action. The laws of nature can be seen (and historically have been seen, e.g. by Newton) as God's indirect action through economical natural law. That is why the search for laws of nature remains a self-evident part of the research program of Christian realism as well. But within Christian realism the laws of nature are not ultimate absolutes, merely beautiful regularities that hold when certain conditions are met. God can override the laws he has established, or add something new on top of them if he wishes.

Theistic realism also allows the researcher not to absolutize current conceptions of the laws of nature.[^8] The theistic realist is thus, by his starting point, more intuitive and more open to new ideas than the naturalist, for whom the "absolute law of nature" represents the ultimate foundation of all of reality[^9], and who therefore finds it more painful to give up.

It is also worth noting that since the intelligibility and lawfulness of the universe follow more naturally from Christianity than from naturalism, and since the scientific method was developed within a Christian framework, the success of science, the discovery of laws of nature and the [great order of the cosmos](kosminen-hienosäätö.md) also increase the credibility of Christianity relative to naturalism.

### The cultural future of metaphysical naturalism

By the nature of the matter, the social and metaphysical contest between theism and naturalism is ultimately not comparable to a game of football in which finding a law of nature would count as a goal for the naturalist team and failing to find one as a goal for the theist team. Instead, the "naturalist team" could, logically speaking, win only in a situation where the opposite goal takes plenty of hits while its "own goal" remains *completely*, or at least *almost entirely*, *untouched*[^10]. Since the starting position is thus so critical for the naturalists, it is humanly speaking no great wonder that many of them have wanted to "secure the win" by bribing or blackmailing the "referee" in advance into disallowing every hit aimed at "the naturalists' own goal". From the standpoint of metaphysics and cultural history, this kind of "refereeing game" is ultimately what methodological naturalism is about.[^11]

Rather, the situation is this: if God explanations really are needed for *something* in honest science, then the theistic view also gains scientific confirmation *in interpreting the laws of nature as God's indirect action*, rather than as the purposeless, independent ultimacy that naturalism takes them to be. In that case it is precisely the cultural credibility of **[metaphysical naturalism](../glossary/sanasto.md#metafyysinen-naturalismi)**[^12] that would be in danger of collapsing, as it turned out to be at bottom nothing but a modern reheating of the circular wishful-thinking world of ancient Epicureanism, an idea long since gone stale in the history of ideas.[^13] Methodological naturalism is thus, in part, also a kind of Trojan horse of metaphysical naturalism, by which the latter, under a guise of neutrality, seeks to maintain its cultural dominance both in science and in the whole society around it.
