# Historicity of Jesus

*Topics: [History](../topics/historia.md), [Jesus](../topics/jeesus.md).*

Denying the existence of Jesus of Nazareth is a common and fashionable way to brand Christianity as ridiculous. A vanishingly small minority of scholars[^1] deny that Jesus ever existed and claim that he was a mythical or symbolic figure created by combining features of the gods and heroes of non-Abrahamic religions.[^2] Modern Jesus research does not assume that the first Christians aimed at impartial history-writing, but it nevertheless considers the denial of Jesus' historicity entirely groundless.[^3] [Tieteen kuvalehti](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tieteen_kuvalehti) (a Finnish popular science magazine) sums up the general opinion of the scholarly community as follows:

> Hardly any scholar who is to be taken seriously dares to claim that Jesus is an invented figure. In the light of the historical sources it seems clear that a human being called Jesus lived about 2000 years ago. Mentions supporting this view are found in several texts. Their authors are known, and not all of the writings are connected with the Bible.[^4]

According to a study made in 1994, 67% of Finns thought that Jesus was a real person. The most convinced of this were university graduates and upper-level white-collar employees (80%). 10% did not consider Jesus a real person. 66% believed that Jesus is the Son of God, and 12% were of the opposite opinion.[^5] Those who question the historicity of Jesus have generally not been historians.[^6]

The denial of Jesus' historicity is often justified by the contradictions in the [canonical gospels](../articles/evankeliumit.md). However, contradictions and gaps in the historical evidence do not necessarily mean that the event behind the information did not happen.[^7] For example, the two complete biographies of [Alexander the Great](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksanteri_Suuri), written by [Arrian](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrianos) and [Plutarch](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarkhos), were composed more than four hundred years after Alexander's death, and yet these writings are considered reliable. Then again, no one has questioned Alexander's existence either, since he left physical evidence behind, such as coins.

It can be considered likely that Alexander the Great died. Among the suggested causes of his death are [malaria](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/malaria), poisoning, pancreatitis and [encephalitis](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/aivokuume). Behind the poisoning theory is, for example, [Curtius'](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Curtius_Rufus) claim that Antipater's son [Cassander](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassandros) brought the poison with him from Europe and that Alexander's [cupbearer](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/juomanlaskija) poisoned the king. Other historians, such as Arrian, claim, however, that Alexander was not poisoned.[^8] Even though the accounts of how Alexander the Great died differ from one another, he presumably died nonetheless. Likewise, there are two completely different versions of [Hannibal's](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal) crossing of the Alps, yet no one has questioned the historicity of the event itself.[^9]

Although the reliability of the [canonical gospels](../articles/evankeliumit.md) can be questioned to some degree, it is difficult to explain how the Jesus tradition they contain arose without assuming a real person behind the tradition. Mainstream [secular](../glossary/sanasto.md#sekulaari) [historical research](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/historiantutkimus) and liberal [theological](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/teologia) research (such as the [Jesus Seminar](../articles/jeesus-seminaari.md)) also accept the historicity of Jesus, among other reasons because it is the most plausible explanation of how the Jesus tradition arose.[^10]

### Research material on the historicity of Jesus
The general opinion of scholars does not, of course, prove the historicity of Jesus. The article now goes briefly over the most central research material on the historicity of Jesus.

References to Jesus of Nazareth outside the canonical gospels <!-- red link: kanoniset evankeliumit --> are so few that it is difficult to build a comprehensive picture of Jesus' life from them. There are, however, enough of them that we can be almost certain of his existence. The small number of references is explained by the fact that, according to many scholars, before his death Jesus was less well known than [John the Baptist](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kastaja).[^11]

Information from the beginning of our era has been found only in the works of four Roman historians. These historians are [Suetonius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius), [Tacitus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus), [Velleius Paterculus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velleius_Paterculus) and [Dio Cassius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dio_Cassius).[^12] According to [Matti Kankaanniemi](matti-kankaanniemi.md), from the perspective of the Roman Empire of his time Jesus' life was not so significant that one could expect many written mentions of it.[^13] The Jews can be assumed to have been considerably more interested in Jesus' life. However, when the Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed in AD 70, a large number of Jewish documents were destroyed as well, and this may partly explain why hardly any information about Jesus can be found in Jewish sources either.[^14]

#### Christian writings

Jesus is the main character of several [New Testament](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uusi_testamentti) books and of other early Christian writings. The most detailed accounts of Jesus' life are found in the four canonical gospels, which according to the current consensus were written roughly between the years 60 and 100. Some hold that the New Testament letters too, especially those the [apostle](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/apostoli) [Paul](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paavali) wrote after the middle of the first century, tell something about Jesus. The oldest known papyrus copies of New Testament books date from the first half of the 2nd century.

Jesus is also a central figure in the New Testament [apocrypha](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/apokryfiset_kirjat), which have never been widely counted in the canon of the books of the Bible.

##### Gospels

The New Testament gospels are the most central texts about Jesus and thus also hold a key position in research on the historicity of Jesus. The gospels are known in complete form from the 4th century. Papyrus fragments of various lengths survive from the 2nd and 3rd centuries.[^15] The central problems in gospel research and in assessing the gospels' reliability are their dating, their authors' connections to Jesus, the different layers of the texts and their dependence on one another. The last of these involves the so-called [synoptic problem](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/synoptinen_ongelma).[^16]

The gospels were not written primarily as historical works; their aim was to make readers believe that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of God.[^15] All of the tradition described in the gospels has already been interpreted from the standpoint of this faith. The view of mainstream research, however, is that the tradition itself originated from a concrete historical person.[^17] The gospels of the Bible are the most credible biographical works about Jesus, and for this reason they cannot be set aside as somehow inferior to other historical sources. All historical sources start out on the same footing, and their present-day use as such should not affect the assessment of their credibility.

##### New Testament letters

The authentic letters of the apostle [Paul](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paavali) <!-- red link: Paavali --> are among the oldest texts of the New Testament. According to neither his own letters nor the [Acts of the Apostles](apostolien-teot.md) did Paul ever meet Jesus in person. He knew Jesus only through the visions he saw and through his conversations with Jesus' disciples. Paul refers to the historical Jesus when he speaks of him as "born of a woman"[^18] and as a descendant of [David](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daavid) <!-- red link: Daavid -->[^19], and when he mentions his brother [James](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaakob_(Jeesuksen_veli))[^20]. Paul's letters also contain references both to sayings of Jesus that are not found in the canonical gospels and to tradition about Jesus.[^21]

##### Writings outside the canon

The so-called apocryphal books outside the canon are mostly late, from the 2nd–3rd centuries or even later, and they cannot be considered historically reliable accounts of Jesus. Some of the writings show signs of tradition independent of the canonical gospels, but this does not yet mean that they are historically more reliable.[^22]

Of the gospels outside the New Testament, the [Gospel of Thomas](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuomaan_evankeliumi) has provoked the most discussion. Many of its sayings appear early in form, predating later editorial work. One notable group of New Testament apocrypha is the so-called [infancy gospels](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/lapsuusevankeliumi). They have influenced, among other things, the church art of the Eastern church, whereas in the West they have not been accepted to the same extent as accounts of Jesus.[^22]

#### Non-Christian writings

Of non-Christian historians, only a few are known to have written anything about Jesus or the early Christians. Apparently nothing was written about Jesus during the time when he is generally believed to have lived. Nor is any archaeological material on Jesus known.

Most of the writers whose writings have survived, however, paid little attention to events in the [Near East](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lähi-itä) at all, let alone to [Judea](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juudea) in particular. So they would not necessarily have had much reason to mention a local preacher who, by the estimate of modern Jesus research, was publicly active for about three years. Jesus does not appear, for example, in the writings of [Philo of Alexandria](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filon_Aleksandrialainen), [Seneca the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Elder) or [Plutarch](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarkhos), which may suggest that, if Jesus existed, he did not attract much attention, since these writers mention several persons of much smaller historical importance.

The writings of four non-Christian historians do, however, contain passages that may possibly refer to Jesus or to Christ. These are the Romans [Tacitus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus), [Pliny the Younger](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plinius_nuorempi) and [Suetonius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius)[^23] and the Jew [Josephus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus)[^24]. These passages, however, usually refer to the early Christians in general rather than to Jesus. Tacitus (*[Annals](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annaalit)*) mentions "Christus" and summarizes the common opinions about Jesus, but gives no sign of having had independent sources at his disposal. Pliny the Younger mentions Christ as an object of cultic worship. Suetonius makes an unclear reference to a person called "Chrestus".[^23] The most significant non-Christian testimony comes from Josephus, who writes among other things about James, the brother of Jesus.

##### Josephus

The most important Jewish source is [Flavius Josephus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus) (AD 37–100), the most important Jewish historian of the first century. One of his best-known works is the 21-part history of the Jews, *Antiquitates Iudaicae* or *Jewish Antiquities*, which he completed between the years 93 and 94. This work contains two clear references to Jesus.[^24] In book 20 of the work, Josephus mentions James as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ. The mention does not appear to be Christian-motivated, and it fits its context. Although some scholars do doubt the authenticity of this mention,[^25] the great majority of scholars consider this mention of Jesus authentic.[^26]

> He convened the Sanhedrin and brought before them a man named James, the brother of that Jesus who was called Christ, and some others. He accused them of breaking the law and handed them over to be stoned.
>
> — Josephus *[Jewish Antiquities 20:9.1](http://www.ccel.org/ccel/josephus/works/files/ant-20.htm)*

More discussion in the scholarly community has been provoked by the passage known as the [Testimonium Flavianum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus_on_Jesus#The_Testimonium_Flavianum):

> At that time there lived Jesus, a man filled with wisdom, if one may call him a man at all. For he did quite incredible things and was the teacher of all who gladly hear the truth. Many Jews and Greeks followed him. He was the Christ. At the instigation of our influential men Pilate did condemn him to death on the cross. Yet those who had loved him before remained faithful to him. For on the third day he appeared to them alive, as the prophets sent by God had proclaimed of him in thousands of wonderful prophecies. Even now there is a sect whose members call themselves Christians after him.[^27]
>
> — Josephus: [*Jewish Antiquities* 18:3.3.](http://www.ccel.org/ccel/josephus/works/files/ant-18.htm)

The Testimonium Flavianum is notoriously contradictory and questionable.[^24] [John Dominic Crossan](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dominic_Crossan) and [K. H. Rengstorff](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._H._Rengstorff), among others, have pointed out that on internal evidence these passages appear to be incompatible with Josephus' other writings and with what is known about Josephus, which has led them to think that these passages were added later, either entirely or in part.
Between 1937 and 1980 [Louis H. Feldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Feldman) went through 87 articles, most of which questioned the authenticity of the Testimonium Flavianum.[^28] Based on a study made by Alice Whealey in 2003, however, it would seem that most present-day scholars hold that Josephus wrote something about Jesus but that additions were made to Josephus' original text.[^29]

It is indeed quite possible that early Christian copyists altered Josephus' original text, since its content is generally considered too Christian to have been written by a Jew:[^24] the text implies that Jesus was more than a man, that he was the Christ and that he rose from the dead on the third day. In addition, the statement "he was the Christ" breaks the flow of the narrative in its context and thus appears detached from it.[^24] In his *Contra Celsum*, the church father [Origen](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origenes) mentions that Josephus did not believe Jesus to be the Messiah ([Heb.](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/heprea) מָשִׁיחַ, *anointed*), that is, the Christ ([Gk.](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kreikan_kieli) Χριστός, *anointed*).[^30] Origen's claim is logical, and therefore credible, in that acknowledging Jesus as the Messiah would in practice have made Josephus a Christian in the context of his time. Josephus himself says in the *Jewish War* that he is a Jewish priest.[^31]

Origen's *Contra Celsum* (AD 248[^32]) is the most significant material for assessing the reliability of Josephus' mentions of Jesus, since it is the earliest reference to Josephus' mentions of Jesus. It is strong evidence that the *Testimonium Flavianum* has been polarized. In it Origen summarizes the content of the *Jewish Antiquities*, referring to that work by name, so he clearly knew the work. In Origen's understanding, however, Josephus did not believe Jesus to be the Christ. From this it can be concluded that the *Testimonium Flavianum* Origen knew was not like the version that has come down to us.

An Arabic version of the *Testimonium Flavianum* has also survived. It is found in a historical work written by Bishop Agapius, in which Agapius quotes Josephus as follows:

> At this time there lived a wise man called Jesus. His conduct was good and he was known to be virtuous. Many people from among the Jews and the other nations became his disciples. Pilate condemned him to death by crucifixion. But those who had become his disciples did not abandon their discipleship. They reported that he had appeared to them three days after the crucifixion and that he was alive; accordingly he was perhaps the Messiah, of whom the prophets have foretold wonders.[^33]
> Agapius
>
> — [Kitab al-'Unwan](http://www.tertullian.org/fathers/agapius_history_02_part2.htm)

This Arabic version contains nothing that could not be considered Josephus' own writing.

##### Pliny the Younger

[Pliny the Younger](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plinius_nuorempi) (c. 63–133) refers to Christians around the year 112 in correspondence with Emperor [Trajan](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajanus) and the governor of [Pontus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontos) and [Bithynia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bithynia). In it Pliny asks for advice on how he should deal with Christians who refuse to worship the emperor as a god and instead worship "Christ" as a god. Pliny, however, only reports what the arrested people believed and does not mention the name Jesus.[^23]

Among other things, Pliny wrote:

> I thought it right to release those who denied that they were or had been Christians and who, as I dictated, called upon the gods and offered incense and wine to your image, which I had ordered to be brought in for this purpose together with the images of the gods, and who moreover cursed Christ ­­­– none of which, it is said, a true Christian can be forced to do. Many of those charged with this accusation (of being a Christian) at once admitted that they had been Christians but had already abandoned it; they had indeed been Christians but had given it up, some three years ago, some earlier, some as long as twenty-five years ago. All of these worshipped your image and the statues of the gods, and cursed Christ.
>
> — Pliny the Younger: Letter to Trajan 10.96.

##### Suetonius

[Gaius Suetonius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius) (c. 69–140) wrote the following in the year 112 in his biography of Emperor [Claudius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius) in the work *Kahdentoista keisarin elämä* [The Lives of the Twelve Caesars] (published in Finnish as *Rooman keisarien elämäkertoja*):[^23]

> *Iudaeos, impulsore Chresto, assidue tumultuantes Roma expulit* ("He expelled the Jews from Rome, since they were constantly rioting at the instigation of Chrestus")

He had already mentioned the Christians earlier, in his biography of Emperor [Nero](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero):

> Punishment was inflicted on the Christians, people belonging to a new and wicked cult.

Some have taken the "Chrestus" mentioned by Suetonius to be a misspelling of "Christus" ("Christ") and thus a possible reference to Jesus. Suetonius, however, implies that the person in question was in Rome in the year 54, in which case he could not refer to Jesus.[^23] Yet the [Acts of the Apostles](apostolien-teot.md) also mentions that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome[^34].

Suetonius may, however, have misunderstood the person of Christ and his significance to Christians. The term "Chrestus" also appears in other texts referring to Jesus, which shows that the idea of a spelling error is not impossible. Chrestus was, however, also a common name in Rome; it meant "good" or "useful" and was an especially common name among [slaves](../articles/orjuus.md), and the unrest Suetonius describes concerns precisely a revolt of slaves. For this reason some scholars do not think the passage refers to Jesus. In any case, the value of Suetonius' texts as a historical source on the historical Jesus remains very slight[^23].

##### Tacitus

The Roman historian [Tacitus](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus) (c. 55–120) wrote in the early second century and also mentioned Jesus in his famous writings. Of Tacitus' works, the parts covering the years 29–32 have been lost, that is, precisely the books that could reasonably be expected to have dealt with Jesus' trial and death sentence.[^35] Tacitus is known to have written two passages about Jesus and Christianity in the year 116. The first says that there were Christians in Rome in the time of Emperor [Nero](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero) (54–68). The second says that Christianity arose in Rome and Judea and that the procurator [Pontius Pilate](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontius_Pilatus) had sent "Christus" to his death:

> A persistent rumor held Emperor Nero to be the one who started this fire. To suppress the rumor he blamed the crime on a sect that was generally shunned for its ways of worship and whose members were called Christians. The name had been given them after a certain Christus, whom the procurator Pontius Pilate condemned and nailed to the cross in the reign of Tiberius. This dangerous sect, which I have described earlier, has taken root not only in Judea, where it comes from, but also in Rome itself, where all things fearful and shameful gather and find their home.[^36]
> Tacitus
>
> — Annals 15, 44

Tacitus portrays Christianity in a very negative light, which suggests that Christians have not altered the passage. It appears in all known copies of the work and is written in Tacitus' style.[^37] He calls Christianity a "dangerous superstition" and "something hideous and shameful", and so the text is probably not a later Christian addition.

In his writing Tacitus refers only to "Christus", the Greek translation of the Hebrew word "[messiah](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/messias)", and not to the name "Jesus". He refers to Pontius Pilate as "[procurator](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/prokuraattori)". According to an inscription, in Pilate's time the governor's title was *praefectus*, which suggests that he also had military authority, even though there was no legion in the province. By Tacitus' time, however, the governor's title had been changed to procurator.[^38]

Some scholars hold that the second passage merely describes Christian beliefs that were not disputed (namely, that the cult's leader had been sentenced to death), and that Tacitus therefore had no reason to check his information, even though he used only religious sources. Other scholars, such as [Karl Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Adam_(theologian)), have held that, as a historian and an enemy of Christianity, Tacitus would have investigated the claims about Jesus' execution before writing about them. *[citation needed]*

##### Rabbinic records

Rabbinic sources contain a few references to Jesus, mostly polemical. The sources are aimed against the messianic faith directed at Jesus, but they do not try to deny Jesus' historicity. As historical sources, the rabbinic texts are questionable. First, it is not certain whether the information they contain is independent of the New Testament. Second, the process by which the texts came into being is complex. Third, it is not certain whether the references concern Jesus of Nazareth or some other Jesus.[^24]

Only a few Jewish historical documents have survived from the last period of the [Second Temple in Jerusalem](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalemin_toinen_temppeli). Apart from Josephus' works, the earliest source from that time is the [Mishnah](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishnah), which is a Jewish collection of law, not a court record or a general history. The Jewish records of that time, both oral and written, were gathered into the [Talmud](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud), a collection of legal debates and stories. It is a large work consisting of more than thirty parts.

The Babylonian [Talmud](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud) mentions a person called "Yeshu" and five of his disciples, Matthai, Nakai, Nezer, Buni and Todah, in the tractate [Sanhedrin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhedrin_(Talmud)) 43a.[^39][^40]. The Talmud also says that Jesus was the illegitimate child of a Jewish woman and a Roman soldier named Pantera. This was meant to refute the idea of the virgin birth.[^24]

### Archaeological evidence

#### Pilate inscription

Just as there has been debate about the historicity of Jesus, some have likewise questioned the historicity of many persons connected with Jesus, such as Jesus' mother Mary, his disciples and many other persons mentioned in the New Testament. *[citation needed]* For centuries there was debate about, among others, the historicity of [Pontius Pilate](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontius_Pilatus), who ordered Jesus' crucifixion. He is indeed mentioned in the works of Josephus, but not in the official Roman documents of the period. From this some have concluded that his existence is pure fiction or even a forged detail. There has likewise been debate about the alleged Pilate's title: was he a [prefect](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/prefekti) (governor) or a [procurator](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/prokuraattori)? *[citation needed]*

In 1961 the situation changed when a limestone block bearing Pilate's dedicatory inscription of a *Tiberieum* was found in the ruins of the Roman theater in [Caesarea Palaestina](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesarea_Palaestina), the capital of Judea. According to the inscription he was *prefectus* (usually written *praefectus*), that is, the governor of Judea. The word *Tiberieum* is unknown. Some scholars have suggested that it was a building, possibly a temple, dedicated to Emperor [Tiberius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius). The inscription is now kept in the [Israel Museum](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelin_kansallismuseo) in Jerusalem under the number AE 1963 no. 104. It is known as the Pilate inscription. Many advocates of the historicity of Jesus point to this inscription as one part of the historical evidence that supports the New Testament's account of the historical Jesus.

#### Shroud of Turin

*Main article: [Shroud of Turin](../articles/torinon-käärinliina.md)*

The Shroud of Turin is a very well-known [relic](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/pyhäinjäännös), discussed and disputed internationally. It is a burial cloth kept in the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist in [Turin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torino) that bears the image of a [crucified](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ristiinnaulitseminen) <!-- red link: ristiinnaulitseminen --> man. Some Christians and some people who have studied the shroud believe that it is the image of [Jesus](jeesus-nasaretilainen.md), transferred onto the cloth at the moment of his [resurrection](jeesuksen-ylösnousemus.md).[^41] In the view of skeptics it is a medieval forgery.[^42]

### Other evidence

#### A boat from the time of Jesus

Fishing was common in Jesus' time, and the gospels mention it more than fifty times. A boat that was found, dated to around AD 40, possibly represents a fishing boat from the time of Jesus; it was found in 1986 in the [Sea of Galilee](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesaretinjärvi). The find was made by the brothers Moshe and Yuval Lufan. The brothers reported that after they had found the boat in the mud, a double rainbow appeared in the sky.[^43] The find is also called the *Jesus boat*, although it would not directly prove anything about the Jesus mentioned in the Bible.[^44]

#### The manner of Jesus' death

According to Ismo Dunderberg, the manner of Jesus' death is also evidence of his historicity, because as an invention it would not have been a good "selling point" at the time when Christianity began to spread through the Roman Empire.[^45] According to Tom Holmén, "execution on a cross is considered in research one of the most certain things known about Jesus". In his view the reason for this certainty is simple: "the message of a crucified Messiah and Son of God appeared, in the light of the thinking of the time, so foolish, repulsive and offensive that the crucifixion cannot possibly be imagined as something made up".[^46]

##### Criticism

According to [Robert M. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Price), many different versions of Jesus' death have arisen, differing from one another so significantly that they are probably fiction. He claims that the very oldest crucifixion account in the gospels borrows its entire short text from an Old Testament book, [Psalm](../books/psa.md) 22, without citing the source, however. Price supposes that Matthew supplements Mark's account with new borrowings from the Old Testament [Book of Zechariah](../books/zec.md) and from the [Book of Wisdom](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viisauden_kirja) <!-- red link: Viisauden kirja -->.[^47]

#### The tomb of Jesus

There are several places that are regarded as Jesus' tomb or have been claimed as his burial place. The [Church of the Holy Sepulchre](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyhän_haudan_kirkko) is Jesus' burial place according to tradition, and some Christians and scholars accept that the church was built over Jesus' burial place. Another is the [Garden Tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_Tomb), a tomb found in the 19th century. According to some Christians, the Garden Tomb is Jesus' real burial place. Arguments for the Garden Tomb have been put forward based on how its location, surroundings and shape match what the gospels report about Jesus' tomb. Non-Christian accounts of Jesus' burial place, or accounts that depart from what the Bible reports, include: [Roza Bal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roza_Bal), [Shingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingō,_Aomori), [Talpiot Tomb](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talpiotin_hauta) <!-- red link: Talpiotin hauta -->

### Archaeological hoaxes

#### James Ossuary

*Main article: [James Ossuary](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaakobin_luuarkku) <!-- red link: Jaakobin luuarkku -->*

The James Ossuary is a box used for burying a dead person's bones, found in Israel in 2002. On the basis of its inscription and dating it was claimed to be the ossuary of Jesus' brother [James](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaakob_(Jeesuksen_veli)) <!-- red link: Jaakob (Jeesuksen veli) -->, and some held that it proved that around the beginning of our era there lived a notable Jesus whose father was Joseph and whose brother was James. The authenticity of the ossuary is, however, now strongly questioned in academic circles, and it is considered a modern forgery — the part of the text referring to Jesus appears to have been added in modern times.[^48]

#### Talpiot Tomb

*Main article: [Talpiot Tomb](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talpiotin_hauta) <!-- red link: Talpiotin hauta -->*

The Talpiot Tomb is a tomb found in 1980 in Talpiot, near Jerusalem in Israel. When it was found, the tomb contained ten ossuaries, one of which has since disappeared. The tomb came to wider public attention in February 2007, when the documentary *The Lost Tomb of Jesus*, produced by [James Cameron](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cameron) and [Simcha Jacobovici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simcha_Jacobovici), and the book *The Jesus Family Tomb*, written by Jacobovici, put forward the theory that the tomb was the burial place of Jesus and of some other persons connected with him who are mentioned in the New Testament.

#### Jesus as a myth

*Main article: [Jesus myth hypothesis](jeesus-myyttihypoteesit.md)*

Some scholars have questioned the historicity of Jesus. According to them, Jesus never existed as a real historical person; he is some kind of [myth](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/myytti). For example, [Timothy Freke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jesus_Mysteries) and [Peter Gandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jesus_Mysteries) have cited as evidence the *Second Letter of John*, which says: "Many deceivers are now abroad in the world, those who do not confess Jesus Christ as a man, come in the flesh" (*2 John* 1:7 [http://raamattu.uskonkirjat.net/servlet/biblesite.Bible?formname=search&formrnd=1148324605587&search=&rng=0&submit1=Hae&ref=2.+Joh.+1%3A7&ctx=0&mod1=FinRaam&mod2=FinPR&mod3=WHNU](http://raamattu.uskonkirjat.net/servlet/biblesite.Bible?formname=search&formrnd=1148324605587&search=&rng=0&submit1=Hae&ref=2.+Joh.+1%3A7&ctx=0&mod1=FinRaam&mod2=FinPR&mod3=WHNU)). According to them, this indicates that there were doubts about the historicity of Jesus already at a very early stage. Mainstream scholars hold that the passage refers to [docetism](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/doketismi), which has nothing to do with the question of Jesus' existence.

Scholars who deny the historicity of Jesus usually appeal to points such as the following:
* the almost complete silence of the Christian sources regarded as the earliest (e.g. the letters of Paul and others) on the details of Jesus' earthly life, and the ambiguity of even the few mentions there are
* the lack of eyewitness testimony, contemporary testimony and near-contemporary testimony;
* the large number of contemporaries and near-contemporaries who should have mentioned, or could have mentioned, Jesus but did not;
* the lack of detailed accounts of Jesus' life in non-Christian sources as well;
* the lack of physical evidence, and
* the similarity between early Christian writings and the common myths of the period.

Perhaps the most prolific biblical scholar to deny the historicity of Jesus is [George Albert Wells](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Albert_Wells). Later the view has been represented by, among others, [Earl Doherty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Doherty), [Robert M. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Price) and the former head of the [American Atheists](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Atheists) organization, [Ellen Johnson](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Johnson). [Bertrand Russell](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell) also questioned the historicity of Jesus.[^49] Few historians, however, can entirely deny the historicity of Jesus in the light of the documents that have survived from the period.[^50]

[Luigi Cascioli](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Cascioli) in Italy went so far as to try to sue the Catholic Church for inventing Jesus and for deceiving people on a large scale.
