# The Edge of Evolution (book)

*Topics: [Books](../topics/kirjat.md), [Design Theory](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md), [Theory of Evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*

**The Edge of Evolution: The Search for the Limits of Darwinism** (EOE) is a book written by biochemistry professor [Michael Behe](../articles/michael-behe.md), in which he deals with the empirically observed limits of [evolutionary mechanisms](../articles/perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md). The observations are based on studies of mutations in malaria, HIV, and human populations. Since in these cases the selective pressures are particularly massive, and the HIV and malaria parasite populations are large and rapidly mutating, this is likely the best available evidence for the capacity of mutation and natural selection, as well as their limits.

### Antibiotic resistance in malaria

When malaria parasites are studied on a global scale, the probability of a single specific point mutation can be calculated when the population size, reproduction rate, and average mutation frequency are known. In each location of the malaria parasite's genome, a mutation occurs a hundred times within a year, and even more frequently with HIV. Nevertheless, these pathogens have not (fortunately) achieved any essentially new tricks.

Behe's analysis is based on empiricism and probability calculations in a way that is best illustrated with case studies: A single point mutation produces resistance to the drug [atovaquone](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/atovakoni) in *P. falciparum* malaria parasites. The probability of this event is 10<sup>-12</sup> (one divided by a million million) for a single parasite. Resistance to the drug [chloroquine](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klorokiini) requires two point mutations, whose combined probability becomes 10<sup>-20</sup>. Malaria is able to achieve these resistances because its population is so large: a single patient's body contains about 10<sup>12</sup> parasites, and about a billion people have malaria. Spontaneous resistance to atovaquone is observed in about one in three individuals, while resistance to chloroquine develops in parasites in about one in a billion people.

### An arms race, or trench warfare and scorched earth?

Behe concludes that the mutation-natural selection mechanism is often capable of giving an organism better short-term chances of survival, but mainly only by destroying ready-made wholes that are harmful to it at that particular moment. Evolution thus resembles a rear-guard battle, where one's own villages, bridges, and industry are burned to achieve a temporary military advantage. Measured by economy (getting by without extra assumptions), the design explanation apparently works best on many levels of analysis at which research committed to materialism is in trouble or is forced to invent separate, often improbable explanations.

### The edge of evolution
#### CCC
If some combination of point mutations yields a selective advantage in some situation, the probability of that advantage arising by chance can in principle be calculated. In practice, however, such an advantage requires a sufficiently large population of organisms, and the more improbable the combination, the larger the population required. A suitable unit for comparing the complexity of the advantages that point-mutation combinations can in principle reach is the population size that theoretical calculations and practical observations show is needed to acquire chloroquine resistance, namely 10<sup>20</sup> (*chloroquine-complexity cluster*, CCC). Since this is a unit of complexity, the CCC figure grows the more intricate the case: 1 CCC is thus one chance in 10<sup>20</sup>, and other values are obtained logarithmically: when **n** = the number of cases of which on average one is favourable, the complexity of the situation is log<sub>10<sup>20</sup></sub> **n** CCC (taking the logarithm to base 10<sup>20</sup>). So, for example, the atovaquone resistance mentioned above requires log<sub>10<sup>20</sup></sub> 10<sup>12</sup> CCC = 12/20 CCC = 0.6 CCC.

On the basis of the above, a 1-CCC point-mutation combination develops in malaria only about once every five years. Because in most species populations and selection pressure are much smaller than in malaria, and the time between generations much longer (the generation time of the malaria parasite *Plasmodium falciparum* is 48 hours), in mammals evolution can less often cross changes even the size of one CCC.

#### Double CCC
10<sup>30</sup> bacteria are born on Earth every year[^1], and if we assume the number has stayed roughly constant, then over a few billion years there would have been a little under 10<sup>40</sup> cells on Earth.[^2] This is a little less than what is needed on average to produce a double CCC once. Roughly here, then, at a mutation combination whose probability is below 10<sup>-40</sup>, lies the edge of Darwinian evolution. So if organisms have structures whose evolutionary origin would have required a mutation cluster at least the size of a double CCC, we can conclude that such a structure probably did not arise as a result of Darwinian evolution.

### Structures that break the edge

Next Behe goes through the biochemical machinery of the cell, showing that most of it requires more than 2 CCC to develop. For proteins, the 2-CCC level of difficulty corresponds to two binding sites between proteins. Most of the cell's functions use six or many more proteins, so most of the cell's machinery would not seem able to develop by means of random mutations and natural selection. In the shape space of proteins, finding the shape of a particular binding site requires over 10<sup>7</sup> trials. As a number, this corresponds to changing at least five amino acids in a protein. Even if only three of these were needed to gain a selective advantage, at a point-mutation probability of 10<sup>-8</sup> the difficulty is 10<sup>24</sup>, that is 24/20 CCC = 1.2 CCC, which is still over 1 CCC.

### Clarifications
#### The number of mutations needed
The limit calculated by Behe applies only in situations where intermediate stages are harmful or less fit. Moreover, the average mutation rate does not indicate the probability of a specific single mutation; rather, mutations may occur with higher probability in certain locations[^3], as observed when comparing cases of atovaquone resistance. Atovaquone resistance is approximately 10<sup>-12</sup>, whereas based on the average mutation frequency, a specific single point mutation should occur with a probability of approximately 10<sup>-8</sup>. For this reason, the probability of two mutations is not always the same as CCC, and a double-CCC is not necessarily just a cluster of four mutations. By definition, a double-CCC refers to a change that is just as improbable as double chloroquine resistance, i.e., 10<sup>-40</sup>.

#### Neutral intermediate stages
The limit defined by Behe does not concern the probability of pre-adaptation occurring through neutral evolution, but rather the limit is defined for situations where mutations would be harmful if occurring alone. Due to genetic drift, the probability of two mutations may take the form <math>u\times\sqrt{u}</math> (where u is the mutation rate). This does not, however, fundamentally change Behe's argument: instead of two binding sites between proteins, the limit of evolution becomes about three binding sites, i.e., four proteins, which is still far too few for most cellular functions.

#### P. Z. Myers and the Lenski experiments

P. Z. Myers, who critiques [Design theory](../articles/suunnitteluteoria.md), criticized Behe's writings in his blog post[^4] concerning L. E. Lenski's famous bacterial experiments. In these experiments, bacteria were cultivated in a laboratory for 20 years so that a portion of the population was frozen for later use at specific intervals. At approximately the 31,500th generation, a certain bacterial population developed a new trait that enabled them to use citrate as a nutrient source under aerobic conditions. If acquiring this trait had required only a single mutation, it would have appeared much more quickly and frequently. However, it was not a simultaneous combination of two mutations, as it turned out that the ancestral forms of that population were able to acquire said mutation more easily at around the 20,000th generation than before. Thus, it was a neutral intermediate stage. Additionally, a third mutation occurred in the population at around the 33,000th generation, which enhanced citrate utilization.

Myers accused Behe of distorting and misunderstanding Lenski's research. Myers's argument does not, however, seem entirely watertight.

In his first response to Behe's comment, Myers wrote:

> This is an experiment that revealed a trait that required at least three mutations.

However, the new trait providing a selective advantage appeared already after the second mutation. The third was thus merely some kind of optimization. Furthermore, Myers apparently missed Behe's point here. At least two mutations are required to acquire the trait from the ancestral population, so in practice it does not appear when populations are as small as this compared to, for example, malaria or HIV. At least one of these mutations was, however, neutral when occurring alone, so it acted as a pre-adaptive mutation, after which the second mutation was also found relatively quickly. Behe's point: gaining the selective advantage required two mutations independent of each other, so the probability of the change was significantly smaller than if only one mutation had been sufficient to achieve it. Of course, the probability of the change was greater than if both mutations, when occurring alone, had been harmful and not neutral as in this case.[^5]

In his second response, Myers's interpretations and conclusions drift even further off:

> Behe claims that he has shown in his book that the result observed by Lenski and colleagues could not occur without intelligent intervention

Behe does not, however, claim this. Behe draws the probability threshold at the probability of the double-CCC, which corresponds approximately to four simultaneous point mutations of the right kind, and this applies only in a case where the intermediate stages are specifically harmful. Even if we were to accept Myers' interpretation of the three mutations, the change would nevertheless not exceed the limit of evolution. First, the intermediate stages were not harmful; rather, the first intermediate stage was evidently neutral and the second intermediate stage was already beneficial, so Behe has not defined a precise probability threshold for that evolutionary process.[^6] Second, even if the intermediate stages had been harmful, and the combination of mutations extremely rare, three mutations would still fall well within the double-CCC limit, so the change would not in this case either exceed the limit defined by Behe.[^7]

So Behe clearly holds that this trait arose through an evolutionary process. He does not claim that such a change would need intelligent intervention, contrary to what P. Z. Myers says.
