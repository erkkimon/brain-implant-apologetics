# Pascal's wager

*Topics: [Worldview](../topics/maailmankatsomus.md), [Atheism](../topics/ateismi.md), [Theism](../topics/teismi.md).*

**Pascal's wager**, in Finnish *Pascalin peliteoria* ("Pascal's game theory"; also *Pascal's scale*, *Pascal's betting*, *Pascal's argument*, *the game-theoretic argument*, *the gambler's proof*), is one reason to believe in God. It is not a proof of God's existence – that is, it does not try to make a person more convinced than before that God really exists – but a kind of "proof of the reasonableness of practical theism", which tries to show that even when starting from uncertainty, believing in God is nonetheless the most worthwhile option from the standpoint of [game theory](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/peliteoria), and to that extent at least the most sensible one.

The starting point of Pascal's wager is this: if the existence of God is treated as an open question on which each of us must nevertheless, by logical necessity, take some position,[^1] human life can be seen as resembling a game of chance in which the stakes are placed on one of the possible options, namely views of reality, but the outcome of the game itself is not for the person to decide – each person's final destiny is settled solely and only *according to the objectively correct view of reality*, regardless of what that person had believed about reality.[^2]

It follows from the nature of the matter that the result of this game is settled, and any payout made, only after our earthly life has ended. According to Pascal's argument, on the basis of the information available now, one can in any case say that the consistent believer in God "plays his life" more skilfully – takes the available information into account more carefully and therefore reaches a "better on average" outcome – than the atheist.

### "Life at stake"

Behind Pascal's wager lies the [Christian](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kristinusko) conception of God, especially from the point of view expressed in the Bible, for example in  ([Heb. 11:6](../books/heb.md)).

The gambler's proof says that even if we do not know whether God exists or not, it does not pay to refrain from believing in God, because even if God did not exist, one could never be certain of it, and such a belief would bring no benefit. Believing in God, on the other hand, does pay, because the reward gained in this way, eternal life, is infinitely great, and the punishment that follows from unbelief, eternal suffering, is terrible.

The [expected value](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/odotusarvo) of the Christian's anticipated gain – the [probability](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/todennäköisyys)[^3] of winning times the net value of the win (the surplus of the win[^4] after deducting the costs[^5]) plus the probability of each possible loss[^6] times the net price of that loss (taking into account the damage suffered[^7] and any temporary advantages gained[^8]) – is therefore infinitely great.

#### The logic of the proof

In a nutshell:
* If God exists and a person acts accordingly, that is the only chance of winning anything lasting at all. The gain is as great as it can be.

* If God does not exist but a person believes in him, then according to Pascal this person gains the protection of commandments that protect the family and so on, and a more exciting course of life, even if he loses out on momentary pleasures.

* If God exists but a person does not believe in him, this person suffers the greatest loss imaginable.

* And above all: if God does not exist and a person does not believe in him, this person admittedly loses nothing – but does not win anything either.

### Blaise Pascal's formulation

The line of thought was put forward in the 17th century[^9] by the French mathematician and physicist [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md), known also as one of the key developers of probability theory, and that is why it still bears his name.

Pascal writes about the wager in his book Mietteitä (*Pensées*), whose third section is titled *Vedonlyönnin välttämättömyydestä* ("Of the necessity of the wager"). He first sets out the place the wager has in engaging with a person who regards God with suspicion. In Pascal's thinking the wager goes hand in hand in many ways with the idea that for those who sincerely want to believe in God there is evidence enough, and for those who do not want to believe it is obscure enough.[^10]

He sets out the following order for engaging with the doubter:

1. First he must be shown that religion is by no means wholly contrary to reason
1. Next he is shown how religion is admirable and even desirable
1. Only once the person regards the possibility of faith positively is it time to present evidence

> People despise religion; they hate it and fear that it is true. To remedy this, one must begin by showing that religion is not contrary to reason; that it is worthy of esteem, by awakening respect for it; then we must make it lovable, so that good people would hope it is true; finally, we must prove that it is true.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^11]

According to Pascal, on the basis of the Bible it is entirely natural to expect that God does not show himself to us so brightly that a person could not reject this light, since the God of the Bible is a hidden God:
"Truly, you are a hidden God, O God of Israel, the Saviour." ( ([Isa. 45:15](../books/isa.md)))

> If this religion boasted of seeing God clearly, and that this view is open and unveiled, one could attack it by saying that there is nothing in the world that shows it so clearly. But instead it says, on the contrary, that people are in darkness and estranged from God, that He has hidden himself from their knowledge, that this is in fact the name He gives himself in Scripture, *Deus absconditus*;
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^12]

Pascal further holds that if a person who does not believe in God at least strives to gain knowledge of God, he is in an entirely different position from a person who is indifferent to the question of God's existence:

> Therefore, among those who do not believe, I make a great distinction between those who strive with all their might to gain knowledge of the matter and those who live without worrying or thinking about it.
> I can feel only compassion for those who sincerely lament their doubts, who regard it as the greatest misfortune, and who, sparing no effort, make this inquiry their chief and most serious pastime.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^13]

Pascal's argument should therefore be seen in a context where the doubter has no real reason to consider belief in God irrational, but may be indifferent to it and think that he does not even need to take a position on the matter:

> Let us then examine this matter and say: "God is, or He is not." But to which side shall we lean? Reason cannot decide here. An infinite chaos separates us. At the far end of this infinite distance a game is being played in which heads or tails will come up. How will you wager? By reason you can choose neither the one nor the other; by appealing to reason you can defend neither option.
>
> Do not then reproach for their errors those who have made a choice; for you know nothing about the matter. "No, but I blame them not for having made this particular choice, but for making a choice at all; for again, both he who chooses heads and he who chooses tails are equally at fault, they are both in the wrong. The true course is not to wager at all."
>
> Quite so; but you must choose your stake. Choosing is not optional. You have already made your investment. Which will you choose, then? Let us see. Since you must choose, let us see what interests you least. You have two things to lose, the true and the good; and two things at stake: your reason and your will, your knowledge and your happiness; and your nature has two things to avoid, error and misery. Your reason is no more shocked by choosing one rather than the other, since you must choose. This point is now settled. But what about your happiness? Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is. Let us assess these two chances. If you win, you win everything; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^14]

Pascal also considers the value of an infinite gain in relation to the uncertainty of winning it:

> But at stake is an eternity of life and happiness. And if this is so, if there were an infinite number of possible outcomes, only one of which were favorable to you, you would still be right to stake one to win two, and since you are obliged to play, you would act foolishly if you refused to stake one life against three in a game where, of infinitely many outcomes, only one is favorable to you, if there were an infinity of infinitely happy life to be won. But here there is an infinitely happy life to be won, and only a finite number of chances of loss, and your stake is finite. Let us do the division: where [what can be won] is infinite, and the ratio of the chances of loss to the chances of gain is not infinite, there is no time to waste, you must give everything. And so, when one is obliged to play, he must sacrifice reason to save his life, rather than risk his life, to gain an infinite benefit, accepting at the same time that he loses nothing.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^15]

> So it is no use saying that the gain is uncertain but that we certainly take a risk. That the infinite distance between the certainty of what is staked and the uncertainty of the gain is as great as the ratio of the finite good certainly staked to the uncertain infinite gain.
>
> That is not so, for in every wager the player sacrifices a certain stake to obtain an uncertain gain, and yet he stakes a finite certainty to obtain a finite uncertainty without offending reason. There is no infinite distance between the certainty of the stake and the uncertainty of the gain; so that is not true.
>
> In reality there is an infinite difference between the certainty of gain and the certainty of loss. But the uncertainty of the gain is proportional to the certainty of the stake according to the ratio of the chances of gain and loss. It follows that if there are as many risks on one side as on the other, it is reasonable to play even; and then the certainty of the stake is equal to the uncertainty of the gain, so far from certainty that there is an infinite distance between them. And so our proposition is of infinite force when a finite stake is in play, in a game with equal chances of gain and loss and infinite risks of gain. This is demonstrable; and if people are capable of finding any truths, this is one.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^16]

> So it was not right that He should appear in a manifestly divine way and completely convince all people; but neither would it be right that He should come so hidden that those who sincerely seek Him could not know Him. To them he has willed to make himself quite recognisable; and so, wishing to appear openly to those who seek Him with all their heart and to be hidden from those who flee Him with all their heart, He so regulates the knowledge of himself that he has given signs of himself which are visible to those who seek Him but not to those who do not seek him. There is light enough for those who only want to see, and dimness enough for those who do not want to.
>
> — [Blaise Pascal](../articles/blaise-pascal.md)[^17]

### Further considerations

#### Scope

A weakness of the argument can be seen in the fact that it does not directly say which theistic religion one should choose, although it does rule out certain options. In particular, the universalist "everyone goes to heaven" model is uninteresting from this point of view, because the payout it leads one to expect after death is, as with atheism, independent of what kind of life each person has lived.

It is possible to approach many different worldview options from the point of view of expected value and to picture what their weight looks like "weighed on Pascal's scale"; this opens up the possibility of "reusing" Pascal's scale. All in all, practice in applying such a "metaphysical stake-and-return theory" would indeed be well suited to opening up the concrete meaning of the perspective of eternity to the modern Western person, who is used to clinging convulsively to this life.

#### Personal preconditions

The argument is aimed mainly at an [agnostic](../articles/agnostisismi.md) of some degree, who admits that he cannot be completely sure which worldview is true. This type of person is common today too, and many of them have grown used to lulling themselves with the idea that, lacking knowledge about ultimate questions that they would consider certain, they "should concentrate on enjoying this life". It is precisely this "to be safe, I take no position" philosophy of life that Pascal's wager shows to be thoroughly mistaken.

According to the argument, an agnostic should not take the risk of living like an atheist and turning out to be wrong, because he could end up in suffering after death. Instead, he should take the risk of living and believing like a Christian even if atheism should turn out to be true. After all, he would not have to wake up after death to grieve over having been wrong, and before that he could still live a good and interesting life. Even then there is a risk that some other religion is true and that, according to it, the Christian would have to suffer after death. However, many religions do not allow adherence to other religions – or the whole idea of "adhering" to mutually exclusive religions at the same time is internally contradictory – so some risk always remains. To minimize the risk, one should in any case reject atheism, because the atheist at least cannot win. If atheism did turn out to be true, what would it matter – how could any atheist ever, in any ultimate sense, get to "enjoy being right"?

### Responses to criticisms

#### "But which god?"

Pascal originally argued from the assumption of a single god. He thus set only atheism and Christianity against each other. It is generally admitted that in such a situation the argument would work. If, however, one has to choose among several gods, there is a risk of believing in the wrong god.[^18] This risk simply has to be accepted, because there is no choice at all that would play it safe in every respect. To reduce this risk, however, it is justified to refrain from believing in atheism.

#### The argument's "moral dubiousness"

The game-theoretic argument has been criticized on the grounds that fundamental choices of belief about reality should not be made by calculating payoffs, and that God would probably not look kindly on those who believe on the basis of such reasoning.[^19] In practice, however, such reasoning is highly justified in many fundamental situations.

It is of course possible that your whole life is just an illusion and that your brain is really in a laboratory run by humanoids, where you are constantly fed false perceptions as electrical impulses. What if this is true? Would this possibility oblige you to take some kind of action? What kind of action would be good or bad in such a situation? If you cannot do anything about it, you may as well live as if your perceptions were reality. If you were wrong, what would it matter?

It is also logically possible that no conscious beings other than you exist. Other people would be like virtual characters in a computer game. Then killing others would not be morally wrong, just as killing a person in a computer game cannot be considered morally wrong. Although you are now aware that such a possibility exists, you still do not believe in it, because you do not want to take the risk of being wrong. Although you probably have no desire to harm other people directly, you nevertheless also take care not to hurt others by accident. So even if this were true, you should live as if it were not.

#### "Surely some god could reward atheists too"

According to some atheists, one could just as well claim that the [atheist](ateismi.md) is rewarded with an infinite reward and the believer is condemned to infinite suffering after death.[^20] In that case the gambler should strive not to believe in God.

This is, in principle, entirely possible. Then again, it is also possible that a meteorite is about to fall on your head. Even if you would like to eliminate the risk of being hit by a meteorite, it is not worth dodging. It is in practice just as unlikely that the meteorite would fall on your head precisely where you have dodged to, because the fall of a meteorite is in practice completely unpredictable and in this respect arbitrary. Likewise, a god of the kind described can with good reason be considered arbitrary. In such a reality, after all, those who believe wrongly would receive the reward, because this god would reward atheists precisely for not having believed in him. Arbitrary gods can of course be invented completely arbitrarily, so protecting oneself against one god exposes one, with equal probability, to the punishment of another. The possibility of arbitrary gods can therefore be left out of account in the wager, because they are by definition completely unpredictable.

### Why convert to atheism?

The argument probably works best of all as a deterrent against converting to atheism. Why would a person who has lived, for example, as a Christian ever, in any situation, find it worthwhile to convert to atheism?

Following the truth unconditionally could be considered an understandable reason for converting to atheism. If, then, the evidence showed irrefutably that no gods exist, this could be considered a good reason to convert to atheism. It is, however, quite easy to see that no actual proofs of godlessness exist. Many arguments have indeed been presented against individual theistic beliefs, but as the basis for believing in atheism specifically, what is usually offered is an [appeal to ignorance](../articles/argumentum-ad-ignorantiam.md).[^21] The atheist may thus say that the theist should prove the existence of God before it would be reasonable for him to believe in it. If the theist then offers some [ground](jumalan-olemassaolo.md) for the existence of God, the atheist can accuse the theist of appealing to a [God of the gaps](../articles/aukkojen-jumala-v2.md) and say that the evidence is not good enough. In practice the atheist can always come up with an [alternative explanation](../articles/naturalistien-paniikkiratkaisut.md) for the evidence, or else he can say that the matter is not yet known, for as long as God would be the only solution to the problem. If one then asks the atheist what kind of evidence would suffice for God's existence to be considered credible, the atheist may answer that God would surely know [what would be sufficient evidence](ateismin-falsifioitavuus.md) and would certainly present it if he existed. On top of this one can still ask: ["Why doesn't God reveal himself clearly?"](../articles/miksei-jumala-ilmoita-itseään-selvästi-v2.md) When this rhetoric is over, however, it should be noted that not a single rational proof of atheism has still been presented. An appeal to ignorance is, after all, a fallacy.

It is therefore naive to imagine that atheism is believed because it is known to be true. Indeed, most atheists admit that, as far as their knowledge goes, they are agnostics[^22]: in their view they do not have enough knowledge to believe in God – nor, on the other hand, enough knowledge to prove that there are no gods. If the matter cannot be settled by purely epistemic arguments, then the game-theoretic approach is highly justified, and atheism can be rejected for purely practical reasons.[^23]

For this reason, a person who cannot be sure that atheism is true would not, on any reasonable assessment, find it worthwhile in any situation to convert to atheism.[^24]
