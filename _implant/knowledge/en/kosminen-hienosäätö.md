# Cosmic fine-tuning

*Topics: [Design theory](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md), [Cosmology](../topics/kosmologia.md).*

Cosmic fine-tuning refers to the observed fact that the structure of the universe and the structure of the laws of physics, and especially the constants of nature, must have been set very precisely for life to be possible.
Physicist Stephen Hawking describes this in his book *A Brief History of Time* as follows:

> The laws of nature that we know contain fundamental constants, such as the magnitude of the electron's electric charge and the ratio of the masses of the proton and the electron... It is a startling fact that the values of these physical constants appear to be very precisely adjusted to allow for the development of life. For example, if the electron's charge differed by even a tiny amount from the current value, stars would not be able to burn hydrogen and helium, or else they would not be able to explode. It clearly appears that these constants must be strictly bounded for the development of any intelligent life to be possible. Most different combinations of constants would lead to universes that might be very beautiful, but in which no one would be there to admire that beauty.
> Stephen Hawking
>
> — A Brief History of Time (1988)[^1]

In his more recent book Hawking does not deny that fine-tuning exists but tries to explain it with a multiverse; we respond to this below.
(The separate article [Our cosmic neighbourhood](../articles/kosminen-lähiömme.md) describes how special Earth's location is.) Signs of design seem to be found everywhere, from very large things to very small ones. Examples include

* The universe's just-right expansion, appropriate mass uniformity, and entropy
* The properties of our galaxy, our sun, and our planet
* Life's multi-part systems that would not offer a selective advantage until the entire system was complete
* Life's program code as well as the properties of DNA and proteins
* The properties of elements and molecules, especially carbon and oxygen
* The laws of particle physics and quantum physics, which make the existence of elements possible.

We go through some of these below.

## Fine-tuned parameters
Astronomer and cosmologist Edward Harrison describes the fine-tuning of natural constants as follows:

> We observe that deviations in the values of such constants as *c* (speed of light), *h* (Planck's constant), and *e* (elementary charge) cause large changes in atoms and atomic nuclei. Even if the changes were only small, most atomic nuclei would be unstable and thus could not exist...
> We also notice that small changes in the values of the constants **c**, **G** (gravitational constant), **h**, **e**, and the masses of subatomic elementary particles cause large changes in the structure and lifespan of stars. In this case, the vast majority of universes would actually contain no stars at all. In those rare universes where stars would exist, the stars would generally be either too dim or so bright that their short lifespans would not suffice for biological evolution... Thus, our universe is finely tuned. We would not exist if the natural constants had values different from those they currently have.
> Edward Harrison
>
> — Cosmology (1981)[^2]

### The fundamental forces of physics
In physics, there are four fundamental interactions: gravity, the electromagnetic force, and the weak and strong nuclear forces. Their strengths are as follows when gravity is normalized to a strength of one:

*Image: The fundamental interactions of physics must be in just the right proportion to one another for, among other things, elements to be stable.*

* Gravity, strength 1
* Weak nuclear force, strength 10<sup>32</sup>
* Electromagnetic force, strength 10<sup>36</sup>
* Strong nuclear force (color force), strength 10<sup>38</sup>

(The situation is actually somewhat more complex because the interactions act on different things and the distance dependencies of the interactions are different. These details too are essential for life.)

Although the range of variation in the strengths of the interactions is enormous, their relative strengths must be like those of today for life to be possible. The relative strengths of the interactions hitting the right spot by chance is therefore extremely unlikely.

##### Gravity
The ratio of the strength of the electromagnetic force to the strength of gravity is 10<sup>36</sup> = 1 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000. If gravity were 3000 times stronger, stars would not be long-lived. If even stronger, there would be only mini-universes, short-lived stars, meter-scale planets, and only small organisms could withstand the gravity. If it were weaker, stars would not be hot enough for fusion to ignite. A 3000-fold change may seem large, but within the range of variation of the interaction strengths, this change is extremely small. The strength of gravity is thus finely tuned with an accuracy of 1:10<sup>36</sup>. This is like a slider as long as the known universe that could be moved only a couple of centimetres from its current setting.

##### Electromagnetic force
Due to the electromagnetic interaction, like charges repel each other and opposite charges attract. For example, the negatively charged electrons orbiting the atom remain around the positively charged atomic nucleus, making elements possible (this also requires extremely fine-tuned quantum physics, as a result of which electrons have stable states around the nucleus and elements have characteristic properties specific to each type.)

The positively charged protons in the atomic nucleus are close to each other and thus strongly repel one another. So the force that holds atoms together would blow the nucleus apart were it not for the glue between the protons, the strong nuclear force, which thus holds the atomic nucleus together. (The strong nuclear force is a residual force from the color interaction that holds protons and neutrons together.) The ratio of the strong nuclear force to the electromagnetic force is approximately 137, and for this reason, there are roughly this many different types of nuclei and thus elements. If the ratio were 60, the elements important for life would not be stable. A slightly larger change would eliminate all elements except hydrogen.

##### Strong nuclear force
Even a 0.5% change in the strength of the strong nuclear force would stop the production of carbon and oxygen in stars.

##### Weak nuclear force
* The weak interaction is sufficiently weak. If it were stronger, the proton-proton fusion using it would turn all hydrogen into helium, in which case there would be no life and no long-lived stars.
* On the other hand, if the weak interaction were weaker, there would be an equal number of neutrons and protons, and there would be only rapidly burning helium stars.

#### Other physical quantities
Electron mass. The electron is about a thousand times lighter than a proton. This enables the electron cloud to orbit a much heavier and thus sufficiently stationary nucleus, which in turn enables chemical bonds.

Proton and neutron masses. The neutron is just the right amount heavier than the proton:
* If the neutron were slightly (1/700th) heavier, the p + p → D reaction would not occur. The universe would consist only of protons and there would be no stars.
* If the neutron were slightly lighter, there would not be an excess of protons; there would be mostly helium. Helium burns too quickly.

(Most of the neutron's mass comes from the strength of the color interaction (field), and a smaller part from the masses of u and d quarks. If the situation is examined in terms of quark masses, even more precise fine-tuning is required.)

#### Several parameters changing at once
In a more detailed examination, the constraints imposed by life often concern two or more parameters. In such cases, the region of parameter space allowed by life can be represented as an area or volume in the space of those parameters. Only a very small region in this parameter space allows for life (see, for example, figures 4, 5 and 7 of Tegmark's article[^3])

Since the number of requirements imposed by life is far greater (140 [^4]) than the number of physical parameters (about a dozen), it is surprising that even one region meeting the conditions for life exists at all. In the vicinity of the finely tuned 'parameter island' we have observed, there may be a few life-permitting parameter combinations, 'islands', but their number is limited [^5] and, in any case, the volume covered by all life-permitting regions is astronomically small compared to the region of dead parameter space. Examining fine-tuning as a function of multiple parameters strengthens the fine-tuning argument.

#### Number of dimensions
We have 3 spatial dimensions (length, width, and height) and one time dimension. Life is possible only in this 3+1 combination. If there were fewer spatial dimensions, blood vessels, nerves or the gut would cut the organism in two. If there were more spatial dimensions, planetary orbits would not be stable. If, on the other hand, there were more time dimensions, causality would suffer. (String theories do have additional, extremely small 'compact' dimensions, but what matters here is the number of macroscopic dimensions.)

#### Parameters of the universe

*Image: To contain regions favorable for life, the expansion rate of the universe and its initial entropy must be finely tuned.*

For the universe to keep expanding for billions of years while its mass continually slows the expansion requires an extremely precise balance: on the one hand gravity must not pull the universe back together too early, and on the other hand the expansion must not be so fast that structures cannot form[^6]. Models of cosmic inflation explain the fine-tuning of the expansion rate, but the parameters of the models (type of inflation field, start and end times of inflation) and their structure must also be finely tuned for this to be possible. Thus, inflation models shift the fine-tuning into their own structure and constants. On the other hand, in this case the fine-tuning is no longer as extreme, at least as far as the model parameters are concerned.

According to current knowledge, normal matter in the universe is the result of a symmetry breaking between matter and antimatter that occurred at the very beginning of the Big Bang. Slightly more ordinary matter was created. There is no *a priori* reason why the symmetry breaking could not have occurred later or earlier. Fortunately, it happened at the right time; otherwise, we would not be here [^7].

The value of the 'force' accelerating the expansion of the universe (the cosmological constant) is extremely small. Fortunately, this cosmological constant is about 1/10<sup>120</sup> of what it would be expected to be (expected if particle masses were a matter of chance); otherwise, there would be no galaxies or stars. This 'force' would expand space so rapidly that no structure formation would have time to occur. The cosmological constant requires at least 1/10<sup>53</sup> (if fine-tuning is examined along the mass, not energy, axis) fine-tuning for life to be possible [^8]. This fine-tuning is more precise than a dart thrower on the moon hitting a specific atom on Earth's surface with their dart.

Mass is distributed in the universe with just the right uniformity: differences of 1 in 100,000. If mass were distributed too uniformly, gravity would not begin to clump stars together. If it were distributed too unevenly, there would be only black holes.

The entropy of the universe must be set to an extremely unusual value in order for a life-permitting universe to be possible. Entropy describes the probability of a system's state. The very large entropy of a black hole can be calculated. From this large entropy follows that an initial state composed of black holes is much more probable for the universe than the extremely ordered initial state that led to our current life-permitting universe. It has been calculated[^9] that the universe's initial state requires fine-tuning with an accuracy of <math>1/10^{10^{123}}</math>. This probability is so small that the number cannot even be written out using the atoms of the known universe.

### Elements and Compounds
#### Solvent
Any realistically imaginable form of life must consist of at least two or more states of matter ([phases](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/faasi)). For example, it is difficult to imagine how an organism could consist solely of gas (or liquid). In said phases, molecules are too loosely bound to each other and mix continuously, so they are unable to maintain structure. Thus, some solid-state matter is required that maintains the organism's shape and structure. Furthermore, it is realistic to expect that an organism needs metabolism, which, in order to be sufficiently fast, in turn requires a liquid or gaseous phase. Solid-based life therefore needs a solvent.

We will now examine why the chemical and physical properties of water make it an excellent solvent for carbon-based forms of life.

##### Water
Life needs a liquid. A liquid enables the building blocks of life to move, but also to touch each other frequently enough. Below are some of water's life-enabling properties. These and many other properties of water make water an extremely exceptional liquid.

* Water is a universal solvent: most substances dissolve in water. When life uses water, it can use most elements.
* Water is not too reactive; most substances do not burn or corrode too quickly under the influence of water.
* Water has a suitably low viscosity (liquid thickness), for example for blood circulation. Water's capillary phenomenon enables water to rise against gravity in narrow tubes, enabling the life of large plants.
* Water has a simple structure, so water is available

###### Water - remaining liquid
For life, it is therefore essential that water remains in a liquid state. Water's own properties help in this. If, then, water's properties were different, we would not be here.[^10][^11]:

* High heat capacity. Water has an exceptionally high heat capacity among substances, meaning that water can absorb a large amount of thermal energy without its own temperature changing significantly.
* High boiling point. Water has an exceptionally high boiling point; it vaporizes only at 100 degrees.
* Water expands when it freezes. This is extremely unusual among liquids and allows ice to float as it is lighter than liquid water. If ice were to form at the bottom of seas and lakes, every winter would add more ice to the bottom, and the ice would not melt during the summer. In that case, lakes would freeze solid all the way to the bottom, killing most of the life in the waters. Ice forms a protective layer against excessive freezing on lakes and seas because ice has low thermal conductivity and no convection occurs within it. Thus, most of the water does not freeze.
* High latent heat of freezing. When freezing, water releases a large amount of thermal energy into its surroundings, thereby slowing the freezing process at the bottom of the ice layer.
* Low thermal conductivity of ice and snow. Ice forms a protective layer against cold on the surface of lakes. Thus, freezing is slowed.
* Ice's suitably low viscosity. Ice is malleable under greater pressure, so ice does not remain at the Earth's poles or mountains; instead, large ice masses spread and their edges break down, so not all water freezes at the poles and mountain peaks.

###### Water - Temperature Regulation
The properties of water are just right for the temperature regulation of humans, animals, and the Earth (Temperature regulation and its favorable properties overlap to some extent with water's ability to remain liquid, but the goal is different.):
* High heat capacity. Water can absorb a large amount of heat energy without its own temperature changing significantly. Thus, when a human or organism performs work through muscle activity or metabolism, which always releases heat energy, the temperature of a water-using organism does not change significantly. For example, the temperature of a (water-using) human rises by 10 degrees as a result of a 10 km run (this too would be lethal, but the body uses water <!-- Mihin? -->. If we were made of salt, our body temperature would rise by 40 degrees; if made of lead, by 200 degrees. Similarly, the temperature fluctuations of the entire Earth are remarkably small because large water masses moderate daily and seasonal temperature variations.
* High boiling point. Water does not boil or evaporate too easily.
* Low viscosity of water. The fluidity of water enables blood circulation. Water is even a non-Newtonian fluid, which means that when pressure is increased, the viscosity of water decreases (i.e., fluidity increases).
* High specific heat. Water can carry away large amounts of heat energy from tissues. Water is capable of transferring a lot of heat energy and equalizing temperature differences on Earth (ocean currents, etc.).
* Thermal conductivity. Through its exceptionally high (four times higher than common liquids) thermal conductivity among liquids, heat is transferred from capillaries to surrounding tissue and vice versa. Thus, heat is transported to the skin.
* High latent heat of evaporation. When evaporating, water consumes a large amount of heat energy. This enables efficient cooling by sweating.

###### Water - Nutrients
The properties of water are also just right for the circulation of nutrients, which is also essential for life. Water expands when it freezes, breaking down rocks. Water dissolves nutrients and transports them to where they are needed. Its viscosity is suitable for rivers. The surface tension of water enables capillary action, by which plants transport nutrients and water from roots upwards against gravity.

In addition, many parts of proteins are water-repelling, i.e., hydrophobic, which enables, among other things, the proper folding of proteins.

#### Carbon
The properties of carbon make it just right for life's use[^10]:
* Carbon is capable of forming extremely long chains. For example, the long chains of carbon's often-cited substitute, silicon, are not sufficiently stable.
* Carbon's affinity for very different elements does not vary too much. This is particularly important in the cases of hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Thus, the energy content of the compounds of carbon with these elements does not vary much. They are stable, and many different reactions are available. Carbon's reactions are therefore generally mild, and the compounds are metastable.
* Carbon is capable of forming many types of bonds (strong covalent bond, weaker non-covalent bonds (e.g., protein's 3D shape)). For example, in the case of silicon, this is not the case, and thus silicon's chemistry is much poorer and lacks diversity.
* Carbon's bonds are usable in precisely the same temperature range in which water is liquid.

Similar 'happy coincidences' arise from many other elements that life can luckily use [^10].

#### DNA molecule

*Image: Degeneracy of the genetic code minimizes the harmful effects of mutations.*

DNA is the most suitable molecule for carrying the construction blueprints of living organisms[^10]:
* Chemically stable enough in water
* Its structure allows for precise and rapid copying
* It is flexible, which enables proteins to recognize the correct location in DNA and read the necessary instructions.
* It has extremely high compaction capability due to supercoiling (a one-meter strand can be coiled into a 0.005 mm ball).
* The best coding system:
  * The coding method (encoding 20 amino acids with 64 different codons) is optimal[^12]. The degeneracy of the genetic code (64 different codons encoding 20 amino acids) minimizes the harmful effects of mutations. If only 20 codons encoded amino acids and the remaining 44 caused interruption of protein synthesis, the probability of chain formation interruption caused by mutations would increase. Furthermore, the code is constructed such that changes in any of the three nucleotides of a codon lead to a synonym (the encoded amino acid does not change) or to an amino acid with similar chemical properties. Therefore, the degeneracy of the genetic code minimizes the harmful effects of mutations. [^13]
  * There are 10<sup>70</sup> possible codes. Searching this space is entirely impossible.
  * The code changing essentially even once during evolution is extremely improbable due to the vast number of correlations. (Changing the code would immediately affect the reading of the entire genome, resulting in countless errors.)
  * The current code is more error-tolerant than one million random coding systems, and likely the best among 10<sup>18</sup> codes[^14].
  * It is also the best coding system with respect to overlapping programs.
  * Since it is extremely improbable that the code could have evolved and yet it is optimal, this is strong evidence for design. A sufficiently intelligent designer is capable of arriving at an optimal solution without trial and error. Behind every other known code there has been a designer.

#### Light and Life
Visible light covers only a small portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. However, the region that our sun produces in abundance is energetically just right for any possible life to utilize[^10]. Life could not have evolved to utilize radio, gamma, or X-ray radiation. Astronomer and evolutionist George Greenstein clarifies why life can utilize only visible light:

> One might suppose that this is an adaptation: that plants have adapted to make use of sunlight. After all, if our Sun had a different temperature, wouldn't some other molecule be able to replace chlorophyll and absorb light of a different color? Startlingly, the answer is no, since all molecules absorb light of the same color range. The absorption of light results from the excitation of electrons to higher energy states within molecules. The energy scale in which this occurs is the same regardless of which molecule is involved. Furthermore, light consists of photons, packets of energy, and photons of the wrong energy simply cannot be absorbed.
> George Greenstein
>
> — The symbiotic universe (1988) [^15]

Life also requires an atmosphere through which visible light can pass. Our own atmosphere filters out the majority of the electromagnetic spectrum—such as the regions dangerous to life, including gamma rays, X-rays, and UV radiation—but still allows through a narrow band of the spectrum—the region of visible light essential for life.

* Water also absorbs radiation that is dangerous to us and allows through radiation that is beneficial. This enables, for example, the use of visual senses underwater as well as the significance of sunlight for both surface and bottom vegetation.
* The eyes of organisms are also capable of being precise exactly on this same wavelength range of light. Longer wavelengths would require eyes that are too large, and imaging errors would be hard to control. Shorter wavelengths are difficult to refract and they damage the eyes.

### Investigability

An additional boost to the design argument is that we are, in many ways, in the best possible position to make observations of the Earth, the solar system, the universe, and the laws of nature (Gonzales & Richards: [The Privileged Planet](../articles/the-privileged-planet-kirja.md)). The best place to live also appears to be the best place to study the cosmos. If the universe were merely the result of chance, there would be no reason why there should be a correlation between habitability and observability. A Designer would be expected to give the smartest conscious beings the opportunity to observe and appreciate the designed universe. Several things speak of the happy simultaneity between observability and habitability; let us take, for example, the Moon and our place in the galaxy:

* The Moon stabilizes Earth's axis, which allows for suitable seasonal temperature variations.
* The Moon's gravitational pull mixes the cold and warm water of the oceans and circulates nutrients.

* The Moon covers the Sun just right, enabling the understanding of the Sun's activity.
* The general theory of relativity was also confirmed by means of a solar eclipse.

The best place to live in the galaxy is also the best place to make observations:

* In the center of the galaxy, there is too much radiation. High-energy and abundant radiation would destroy life on a planet and blow the atmosphere into space. Additionally, the center of the galaxy has too many stars and thus too many collisions. On the outskirts of the galaxy, there is too little of heavier elements (for example, carbon, oxygen, phosphorus, and metals). The best place for life is where we are, that is, in the emptier area between the arms of the spiral galaxy, where there are enough different elements but not too much radiation or too many supernovae exploding nearby.
* The most habitable place in the galaxy (where we live, then) is also the best place for observing our universe, because in the center of the galaxy there is too much dust and other stars, so from there one cannot see other galaxies, background radiation, or large structures of the universe very well. From the very edge of the galaxy, one cannot see the center of our galaxy very well either. Our location between the arms of the spiral galaxy is also low-dust; from here, one can see well in every direction.

## Explanations for Fine-Tuning
* A more fundamental 'theory of everything': It does not appear to help; a more fundamental theory would shift the fine-tuning into its own constants or structure. For example, the mass of the neutron and proton naturally follows from the underlying quark masses and the structure of QCD theory, and the fine-tuning of the quark masses is even more precise in order to get the neutron and proton masses right. Thus, fine-tuning may become more difficult or easier, but the basic argument remains the same. Furthermore, the number of boundary conditions required by life remains the same in any case (from tens to hundreds), so with a smaller number of constants it is even harder to find a solution that satisfies them.
* [Multiverse theories](../articles/multiversumi-hypoteesi.md) and the selection effect (we could not observe anything else): This explanation does not improve the prediction of observability in the case of *this* universe. The multiverse explanation would raise the probability that *some* universe is fine-tuned, but it does not explain any better than a single universe does why *this* universe is fine-tuned. Thus, the multiverse explanation is not a credible explanation for fine-tuning. More on this topic in the [article dealing with the multiverse](../articles/multiversumi-hypoteesi.md) as well as, for example, in the article http://www.intelligentdesign.fi/2008/09/03/monimaailmankaikkeus-selityksen-monet-ongelmat/ and, for a technical treatment, in the article [arXiv:0802.4013](http://arxiv.org/abs/0802.4013) on the physicists' preprint server.
* Design: Out of fashion because of naturalistic philosophy of science, but it predicts observers best. There is also independent evidence for design.

Predictions of design can be justified at several levels, with examples below:
1. Predictions made on the basis of God's special revelation.
1. Predictions based on the similarities between God and man. (Analogy is one of the most common forms of scientific reasoning.)
1. Predictions based on the properties of conscious beings in general.
1. Predictions that are summed over all the motivations of a conscious being. Since in this case the dimension and type of probability space are different from the space of physical parameters, it maps to a non-uniform prediction in the space of physical parameters. For example, if we assign a 50% probability to the Designer (here, that is, God) creating a universe that allows conscious observers, calling this property of the universe O, then by summing over O and not-O, we still obtain a very large prediction for an observer-friendly universe in the space of physical parameters compared to a uniform distribution (or other non-informative distribution) in the space of physical parameters. For example, the strength of gravity is 1, when the strengths of all the forces of physics are in the range 1 - 10<sup>40</sup>. Gravity can be at most 3000 times stronger for life to be possible. Thus the probability that God creates a world where gravity is in the range 1-3000 is 0.5. Similarly, the probability of obtaining a gravity value in the life-permitting range purely by chance is approximately the length of the interval 1-3000 compared to the length of the entire scale, i.e., approximately 3000/10<sup>40</sup>=10<sup>-36</sup>. The God-explanation is thus approximately 0.5/10<sup>-36</sup> ~ 5*10<sup>35</sup>, i.e., almost a million million million million million million times more probable than pure chance (and thus theism is provisionally this many times more probable than atheism on the basis of this argument alone).

### Objections

##### Probabilities and time
Cosmologist K. Enqvist has made the following criticism of cosmic fine-tuning (Kanava 03):

> For example, creationists love to link probabilities to genes and their mutations. From all possible permutations of genes, one then obtains extremely small probabilities, which are meant to show that, for example, the origin of life by chance is "practically impossible". But these considerations are nonsensical unless seconds and volumes are attached to them: how many molecules inhabit a cubic centimeter and how often new permutations are formed per second.

ID is of course not [creationism](../articles/kreationismi.md), but in all the calculations I have read *[source missing]* where the number of molecules or cells is essential, it is of course taken into account.

##### Range of quantities
K. Enqvist:

> In addition, it must be known what is the possible range of variation of physical quantities.

Of course the range matters, but the calculations have been done with the smallest reasonable ranges of variation. In principle, the ranges of variation could be infinitely large, in which case the materialist would have to believe in p=0 probabilities.

##### All outcomes are improbable
K. Enqvist:

> The probabilities of occurrence for all complex phenomena are so infinitesimal that we could, if we wished, always call them miracles.

Enqvist has apparently not acquainted himself at all with [design theory](suunnitteluteoria.md), since the specification criterion settles exactly this issue. On the other hand, this is an almost trivial observation for [Bayesian hypothesis testing](../articles/bayesin-teoreema.md): the design prediction is better, and that settles it.

##### We should know the 'correct' probability distribution
K. Enqvist:

> it is nearly impossible to attach a clear number to the parameters of physical theories that would describe the probabilities of their observed values. At present we know neither the range nor the way in which the parameters can vary.

Here Enqvist has a wrong and selective concept of probability: we would have to know everything in order to be able to determine probabilities. But then probabilities would not be needed; we would know the result. For example, if we know everything about a dice throw, we know the result. Probability describes precisely our uncertainty. And it is used in cosmology precisely in this way. Uniform probability distributions, in particular, are generally in use.

##### Backward reasoning – a different kind of life
A common criticism of the fine-tuning argument is that if the laws of nature were different, a different kind of life would have evolved, so the precise tuning of constants should not be surprising. This criticism is based on a misunderstanding. First, most constraints concern such essential matters that it is difficult to imagine any kind of life if the conditions are not met. For example, if long-lived stars did not exist, life would not have sufficient free energy available. If there were not enough different elements, complex life would hardly be possible. If the universe expanded too quickly or if the universe consisted of one or more black holes, life would not be possible. And so on. So most of the 'happy accidents' in the laws of nature evidently concern all kinds of matter-based life. Second, water and carbon are the best building blocks for life that we know of. Water and carbon both satisfy many constraints, significantly more than the number of constants that determine their essential properties. Thus, different constants would probably not yield equally good elements. Third, we observe only carbon/water-based life. If a different type of life were vastly more probable (which is hard to imagine on the basis of our biochemical knowledge), why do we not observe it? It is probable that the life we observe is the most probable kind of life, or at least that other kinds of life are not vastly more probable. Thus, the conditions imposed on the laws of nature by carbon/hydrogen-based life can be considered to be of the right order of magnitude. And this order of magnitude points to the need for a very precise fine-tuning.

##### Stephen Hawking's claims
In his recent book The Grand Design, which Stephen Hawking has written together with science writer L. Mlodinow, Stephen Hawking claims that God is not needed to explain the universe, but that gravity is sufficient as an explanation. In the book by Hawking and Mlodinow, there is nothing essentially new from a scientific point of view regarding the origin of the universe.

The scientific content of the book:

* It tells of Hawking's model using imaginary time regarding the beginning of the universe. With this, the initial singularity can be circumvented or smoothed out. The universe has a beginning in this model too.
* Hawking admits to the wonder of the cosmic fine-tuning. He attempts to explain the fine-tuning with a multiverse explanation. More precisely, Hawking uses the many-worlds interpretation of quantum physics, in which all the terms of the path-integral sum are taken to be separate universes.
* Hawking does not, in his book, actually explain the origin of the universe based on the laws of physics. In the final chapter, Hawking quickly speculates that if the sum of the universe's positive and negative energy is zero, the universe could arise as a fluctuation from a vacuum. Hawking does not seem to notice that by assuming the vacuum, he already presupposes the existence of spacetime, the void, and thus vacuum energy. But it is precisely the existence of these that should have been explained. Furthermore, the total energy of the universe is not well-defined, so speculation with the sum of positive and negative energy is unscientific.

The philosophical content of the book:
* Hawking begins his book with the statement "philosophy is dead". However, most of his conclusions rely purely on amateurish philosophy, not on science.
* Hawking supports antirealism, according to which there is no real reality independent of models; there are only different models. Hawking states that big bang cosmology is just as valid as young-earth creationism; one model is just more comfortable/convenient for some.
* Hawking supports determinism, for which he, for some reason, wants to give the prefix "scientific" (again a symptom of a lack of precise philosophical thinking; science does not prove determinism; it is a philosophical presupposition). Hawking does not seem to understand that, according to his determinism, he did not actually write his book himself; rather, particles moving according to the laws of physics have determined what the book contains. We have no reason to assume that the book is true or well-founded. Why does Hawking believe in his thoughts and conceptions, which he believes are the consequence of the random motion of particles and fields?

Hawking refers to the discovery of a planet like Earth. So far, the over 500 discovered planets are not nearly as suitable as Earth for complex life. The distance from the sun is just one requirement [among dozens](../articles/kosminen-lähiömme.md) or actually even hundreds.

Hawking commits himself on faith to determinism, according to which everything follows from the motion of particles and fields. However, with the power of physics alone, no information arises that living beings are full of (DNA), and probably not personality or consciousness either. Due to his belief in determinism, Hawking is nevertheless forced to believe that the laws of physics could somehow produce such things and present the consequences of his belief with the authority of science.

### Conclusions

The properties of water, carbon, DNA, proteins, the band of light in the atmosphere, etc., are not self-evident; they are possible only with certain laws of physics and the values of their constants. These are finely tuned for life. So when you look at practically anything around you, it is good to understand that their existence is not a given, but that the laws of physics have been carefully adjusted so that life and humans are possible.
The probability that the laws would be correctly set through blind chance is extremely small. It is reasonable to think that a designer wanted to create life in his universe and designed the laws for life. In addition, nature contains much multi-level order, which consists of many parts and which is hard to evolve. More ID researchers (and funding) are needed. Although it has not been possible to do much research supporting design, the discovered facts together form a strong cumulative argument for design.
