# Cosmological argument

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md), [Arguments](../topics/argumentit.md), [Cosmology](../topics/kosmologia.md).*

According to Immanuel Kant's definition, the **cosmological argument** is an argument for the existence of God whose starting point is *the experience of existence in general*. More precisely, we can say that it is an argument from some finite object (anything other than God, who is an infinite object) to God[^1]. Often, however, this *finite object* is specified further, for example as the universe, the cosmos, in which case the cosmological argument is an argument from the existence of the cosmos to a **First Cause** or **Necessary Being** outside it, which is the ultimate explanation of the existence of the universe.

The cosmological argument has a long and distinguished history. In some form the argument has been defended by **[Plato](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platon)**, **[Aristotle](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristoteles)**, **[Maimonides](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides)**, **[Anselm](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_Canterburylainen)**, **[Thomas Aquinas](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuomas_Akvinolainen)**, **[Descartes](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes)**, **[Spinoza](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinoza)**, **[Leibniz](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz)**, **[Locke](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)** and, today, for example Alexander Pruss, Robert C. Koons, Timothy O´Connor, [Richard Swinburne](../pages/richard-swinburne.md) and **[William Lane Craig](../articles/william-lane-craig.md)**.

The cosmological argument is in fact a family of different arguments. Its subtypes are:

1. [Thomistic cosmological arguments](../pages/tomistiset-kosmologiset-argumentit.md), which seek, for the world or for particular features of it such as motion, causation or contingency, a ground of being that sustains it, or a **First Mover** that is **Pure Actuality**;

2. [Leibnizian cosmological arguments](../pages/leibnizilaiset-kosmologiset-argumentit.md), which, by way of the principle of sufficient reason, seek a sufficient reason why something exists rather than nothing; and

3. the [kalam argument](kalam-kosmologinen-argumentti-v3.md), which is an argument from the beginning of the universe to its **First Cause**.[^2]

The subtypes above are the best known, but in addition to them one can further distinguish

4. the [inductive cosmological argument](../pages/induktiivinen-kosmologinen-argumentti.md) (from Richard Swinburne's work The Existence of God), according to which the complexity of the universe gives inductive support for the existence of a simple God.
