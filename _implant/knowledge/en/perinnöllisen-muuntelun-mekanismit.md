# Mechanisms of hereditary variation

*Topics: [Theory of evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*


The mechanisms of hereditary variation include [natural selection](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/luonnonvalinta), [sexual selection](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/seksuaalivalinta), [mutations](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/mutaatio_(biologia)) and [migration](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geenivirta). Natural selection leads to a population that is better adapted to its current environment, because the most successful individuals are very likely to become, through more efficient reproduction, the prevailing type in the population in the end. In other words, genes determine an organism's traits, and so the organisms that fare better carry the genes that cause them to fare better. Individuals that fare better get to reproduce more often, and so the genes they carry, which bring about that greater success, become more common in the population. Evolution can also lead to specialisation, to simplification or to an increase in nature's [biodiversity](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/biodiversiteetti), its variety of life: a vacant niche in a habitat usually comes, at some point, to hold a species of its own that has specialised for it.

#### Mutations
A [mutation](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutaatio_(biologia)) is a hereditary change in an organism, an error that occurs when the genetic material, that is the [DNA](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), is copied. Mutations provide the "raw material" for natural selection, and they are the only source of new genetic material.

Most mutations are neutral, that is, they lead to no observable change in an organism's chances of survival or in its fit to its habitat. Of the mutations that are not neutral, most are damaging or harmful. Only a small share are [adaptive](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/adaptaatio), that is, such that they help the organism live and reproduce in its habitat, but this small number of beneficial mutations makes adaptation possible.

The DNA of many animals and plants contains a fairly large amount of material that does not code for proteins. Some of this material has no known genetic role, but part of it performs regulatory tasks. In humans this protein-non-coding "junk DNA", or [non-coding DNA](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/tilke-DNA), makes up as much as 97%. The probability of a beneficial mutation in an organism is small, and for such a beneficial mutation to be passed on to offspring, it must occur in the germ line, where it happens only in extremely rare cases. Even so, it can happen and does happen all the time.

For example, irradiation has produced changes in the germ lines of various animal and plant species that have been inherited by later generations. Some of these have been to the organism's advantage. Natural radiation is one cause of mutations, but because radiation in nature is considerably lower than in irradiation experiments, mutations occur much more rarely in nature. Nature, however, has considerably more time and more individuals "available" than irradiation experiments, so beneficial mutations almost inevitably arise over long periods, even though they are rare on a human timescale.

A beneficial mutation is recognised by the fact that the organisms that receive it succeed from generation to generation. Those that do not make the individual succeed are called harmful or neutral mutations. Even slightly beneficial mutations sometimes spread and become the prevailing type in the population. Then those that received the beneficial mutation are in the majority, and the next beneficial mutation will most likely be received by some individual that received the previous one. In this way beneficial mutations accumulate. What makes this accumulation even faster is so-called sexual reproduction, through which genes are "shuffled" like a deck of cards, and beneficial mutations that happened at the same time in different parts of the population can be combined in a single individual. In this way evolution becomes "parallel processing".

If a species benefits in its present environment from, for example, long legs, mutations that increase leg length, and through it speed, are beneficial mutations. Because faster individuals, for example, escape their predators better, they get to reproduce more often, and over many generations it is likely that the genes for longer legs become common traits in the population. These longer-legged individuals may also receive other beneficial mutations, and then the mutations really do accumulate. If the species is split over several isolated areas, these beneficial mutations or their accumulations naturally do not reach all members of the species. Soon so many mutations have accumulated that members of the different stocks can no longer interbreed. At that point [speciation](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/lajiutuminen) has taken place as a result of the accumulation of beneficial mutations.

#### There are too many mutations
* Harmful mutations are estimated to outnumber beneficial ones by 10<sup>4</sup>-10<sup>6</sup> times. The selective advantage of one beneficial mutation is typically on the order of one per mille.
* 100-600 harmful genetic mutations per person.
* The human species is not developing through this; the direction is downward.
* An organism's survival is affected far more by things other than its genome. For example, the food available, the environment it grows up in, or luck are usually statistically more significant factors than individual mutations.
* In nature, the genome's share in survival is typically 0.004.
* Because of "noise", natural selection does not even "see" slightly harmful mutations, which are the most common kind. They accumulate in the genome, which leads to the degeneration of the population.

#### Speciation
In any population of organisms, the traits of individuals vary somewhat because of both heredity and environment. So it inevitably happens that certain traits favour reproduction more than others, and for this reason these traits become more common in the population. When living conditions change (climate, geography, chance, other organisms), it may well be that some other traits promote reproduction more than the rest, and these in turn begin to increase in the population. From this, speciation gradually arises, especially if the population splits into two or more parts, for example for geographical reasons, so that hardly any gene exchange takes place between the populations. In this way the lines of development slowly diverge from each other, and in the end one ancestral form has speciated into different species that cannot interbreed.

Speciation is the splitting of one species into several. Three mechanisms of speciation are distinguished:

* Allopatry: the parts of the population live geographically apart.
* Sympatry: the parts of the population live in the same geographical area, but in different ecological niches.
* Parapatry: the parts of the population live geographically side by side.

In every case the genetic traits of the subpopulations drift further and further apart as a result of randomly accumulating mutations. In the end the different populations are genetically so far from each other that they can no longer produce fertile offspring together. In this way two species have arisen.

A special case of parapatry is the so-called [ring species](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/rengaslaji), which consists of many adjacent populations that all interbreed with their neighbours, while the ones at the two ends no longer interbreed with each other.

Perhaps the best-known example of speciation is the specialisation of [Darwin's finches](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwininsirkut) into 13 different species on the [Galápagos Islands](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galápagossaaret) from a single ancestral species that reached the islands. Their beaks have specialised to fill different feeding niches.

#### Migration
The role of migration in evolution is that a new, beneficial hereditary trait that arose in one place becomes common throughout the whole population. The larger a contiguous population, the larger also the number of mutations. Then the number of beneficial mutations is larger too. So the larger the population in number of individuals, the faster more variation takes place.

A population should, however, be required to be such that an individual can physically travel from one place to another within it. For organisms living on dry land, for example, this means there must be a contiguous area of dry land. The most striking example of this is [Australia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia).

According to the common view, 45 million years ago the Australian [continental plate](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/mannerlaatta) finally separated from Antarctica and sailed away, so that the organisms living in Australia developed differently from organisms elsewhere. Australia is still home to marsupials, which have adapted to the same various ecological niches as placental mammals in the rest of the world. For example, where the rest of the world has wolves, mice and moles, Australia has the marsupial wolf, the marsupial mouse and the marsupial mole. This is also an example of [convergence](konvergenssi.md).
