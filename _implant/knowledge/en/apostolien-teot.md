# Acts of the Apostles

*Biblical backbone: [Acts of the Apostles](../books/act.md), [New Testament](../books/nt.md).*

*Topics: [History](../topics/historia.md), [Bible](../topics/raamattu.md).*

One of the first aims of naturalistic New Testament scholarship has been to call into question the historicity of the Acts of the Apostles, because Acts provides an account of the first years of Christian history. In order to justify their own narrative of the origin and development of Christianity, naturalistic scholars must cast doubt on the Acts of the Apostles. It would be very problematic to admit that Acts was written by a close friend and colleague of Paul a few years after Paul's death, since then it would carry too much historical weight. So Acts is readily explained away as a legendary account from beginning to end. Once the historical frame of reference provided by Acts is cleared away, it becomes possible to construct narratives of the early stages of Christianity that fit the naturalistic world picture.

According to Luke Timothy Johnson[^1], such "histories of Christianity" share the common characteristic that they are not history in any ordinary sense of the word.

> The lack of genuine factual material is compensated by making generous use of the principle of analogy in the form of developmental models. – – The model uses only part of the factual material, and the part used is forced to fit into the model. Scholars of early Christian history begin to look like puzzle builders who are given 27 pieces from a thousand-piece puzzle and discover that only six or seven pieces fit together. A reasonable procedure would be to put those pieces together, guess what part of the puzzle they belong to, and then modestly refuse to make guesses about those pieces that do not fit together. These puzzle builders, however, throw away the central piece, Acts, which would generally make it possible to build connections between the pieces. Then they claim the right to make use of pieces from other puzzles. Finally, they take this jumble of pieces, construct a general picture of what history should look like (based on some general puzzle pattern), and begin to modify these pieces until they fit together with each other.

Besides trying, at a general level, to set aside the New Testament's account of the early stages of Christianity altogether, naturalistic scholars have sought to demonstrate detailed historical errors in the writings of the New Testament. For example, naturalistic scholars long regarded the mention of Lysanias, tetrarch of Abilene, in [Luke 3:1](../books/luk.md) as evidence of Luke's historical unreliability. Skeptics never tired of bringing up this detail. Eventually, however, it was revealed that the unreliability belonged not to Luke but to the naturalistic scholars. The historical evidence indicates that, in addition to the earlier Lysanias, who died in 36 BC, another Lysanias ruled during the reign of Emperor Tiberius. Even Josephus refers to Lysanias in some passages in a way that should have made clear that this was a contemporary of Luke, not his earlier namesake (Antiquities 19.5.1 § 275; 20.7.1. § 138). Eventually, inscriptions were found that placed Lysanias precisely in Luke's time period. So this textbook example of the New Testament's historical unreliability vanished into the thin air it had come from.[^2]

Other similar examples have met the same fate. The historical reliability of the New Testament writers is astonishing. It is confirmed with every new discovery. Luke was long ago shown to be historically accurate in calling the ruler of Cyprus a proconsul. An inscription was also found mentioning the proconsul Sergius Paulus, about whom Luke writes in the Acts of the Apostles. Luke's historical reliability is underlined by the fact that the political history of that time is very complex. The status of provinces changes frequently, borders shift, and so do relations with the Roman central administration. A later historical forger would find it very difficult to describe this world reliably. It is entirely impossible to imagine that Luke's Gospel is the work of a later forger, considering how reliably and in what detail the historical circumstances of the events are described in it. Luke has succeeded in navigating this labyrinth like a true expert. A forger living a hundred years later would have found it impossible to describe the historical circumstances so accurately. Roman historians who are considered historically reliable have made mistakes. Even Tacitus errs in some details. A forger living a hundred years later would have found it impossible to avoid all the numerous historical pitfalls without errors. The New Testament proves to be a work built on eyewitness testimony in the light of historical evidence.[^3]

There is no doubt that Luke was an eyewitness to these events or at least could rely on eyewitness testimony. A. N. Sherwin-White[^5], an expert in Roman history, states:

> The evidence supporting the historicity of the Acts of the Apostles is vast – – Any attempt to reject its historicity appears absurd. Scholars of Roman history have long regarded this as self-evident.

For naturalists, it is unpleasant that Luke reports a total of 35 supernatural miracles in the same book in which he reports all these 84 details confirmed by historical research. Luke reports the miracles as part of his historical account in the same straightforward manner as other historical details. It is unlikely that Luke would be precise when describing relatively insignificant details such as the direction of the wind, the depth of the sea, and peculiar city names, yet imprecise when describing important events such as miracles. Since Luke has proven to be a reliable historian in every respect, it would be prejudiced to consider him unreliable when he describes miracles. Luke's reliability as a historian has been confirmed in so many details that there is good reason to trust him in those areas as well where, due to the nature of the matter, we cannot obtain external corroboration.[^6]
