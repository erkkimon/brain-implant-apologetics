# Moral argument

*Topics: [Arguments](../topics/argumentit.md), [Ethics](../topics/etiikka.md).*


The **moral argument** is one form of the *axiological argument*. *Axiology* means the study of values, i.e. the theory of what is valuable and how valuations can be justified. The axiological arguments for [the existence of God](jumalan-olemassaolo.md) that prevail today generally appeal to moral values, and so this article concentrates on the moral argument for the existence of God.

The moral argument starts from the premise that moral life has an order that is just as subtle and complex as the order of nature. The *direct moral argument* takes the moral order as its starting point and concludes that the existence of an intelligent, almighty Creator is the best explanation of this fact. The *indirect moral argument* approaches the same subject from the opposite direction: without God there would be no moral order either, and life would be meaningless; but since life is not in fact meaningless, God must exist.

### Objective morality is a precondition of human life

Modern versions of the moral argument thus start from the premise that morality is objective.[^1] No society can allow its members to harm one another arbitrarily. Morality is a precondition of close and lasting friendships, and life without friends is poor.

There are, of course, many philosophers who are moral antirealists, i.e. who claim that moral values and norms have no objective basis in reality. Moral antirealism, however, seems to be little more than "armchair philosophy": one can hold to it only in theory and on paper. In practice it seems to be almost impossible for people to live consistently without assuming that at least some of the most fundamental moral principles are objective and binding on all people. In practice human life runs on this assumption.

### Objective morality is best explained by the existence of God

Once grounds have been given for regarding moral values and norms as objective, the objectivity of morality is best explained by assuming that a perfectly good and just [God](jumala.md) is the origin of all reality. The moral order is easier to explain from a [theistic](teismi.md) than from an [atheistic](ateismi.md) starting point. From an atheistic starting point one can appeal to [evolutionary mechanisms](perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md) and to the requirements of the survival of the human species to explain how the moral order arose. Morality, however, is a subtle and many-sided system that contains much that is not necessary for the survival of the species.

### Three criteria of a hypothesis's explanatory power

[Paul Copan](../articles/paul-copan.md)[^2] proposes three criteria by which a hypothesis's ability to explain a given fact can be assessed. We should choose the theory that a) offers a more natural transition from the general theory to the fact in question, and that is b) more unified and c) more fundamental.

#### A natural fit between the explanation and what it explains

Copan argues that theism fits objective morality better than atheism does. If a perfectly good God exists, it is natural that his nature defines what is good and valuable. If, on the other hand, the origin of life is assumed to be explained by a theory of evolution that proceeds by natural selection and chance alone, we are dealing with a process that takes place in a state of nature without values, meaning or purpose. It is only about survival and natural selection. From there it is hard to ground objective morality and the [intrinsic value](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itseisarvo) of human beings. On the basis of the theory of evolution it also becomes hard to assume that our moral intuitions give us reliable knowledge of what is morally right or wrong, good or evil, because these intuitions have presumably developed only because they have helped the human species survive. Acting in a way that serves survival is a different thing from acting morally.

#### The unity of the whole formed by the explanation and what it explains

In terms of unity, too, the theistic theory fares better than the atheistic one. The idea that a perfectly good God creates man in his own image, and that man therefore has intrinsic value, fits well with the theistic theory as a whole. By contrast, the idea that material reality first comes into being out of nothing in the Big Bang, that this material reality gradually develops into living organisms which finally become conscious, and that the moral intuitions of these conscious beings could give true knowledge, seems to fit poorly with that theory as a whole.

#### How fundamental the thing explained is in the explanation's view of reality

The third criterion is fundamentality. A hypothesis must be considered better than its rivals if it can give the important features of reality a more fundamental place. Does a worldview leave us with a set of isolated facts that simply have to be accepted, or does it offer a good starting point for explaining why they exist? Atheistic materialism has great difficulty explaining human consciousness, which is the foundation of morality. The materialist philosopher Colin McGinn writes:

> How is it possible for conscious states to depend upon brain states? How can technicolour phenomenology arise from soggy grey matter? - - How does the aggregation of millions of individually insentient neurons generate subjective awareness? We know that brains are the de facto causal basis of consciousness, but we have, it seems, no understanding whatever of how this can be so. It strikes us as miraculous, eerie, even faintly comic.
>
> — Colin McGinn[^3]

Naturalist moral realists such as David Brink hold that morality is built on consciousness. Brink argues for a materialist view on which states of consciousness depend on physical states. He assumes that few people regard mental states as metaphysically strange.[^4]. It seems, however, that philosophers of mind do not regard this as being as unproblematic as Brink assumes. Colin McGinn writes:

> Think of the universe before conscious beings appeared: the chances did not look good that such beings would arise. The world consisted of mere physical objects and physical forces, without life – – We have a good idea of how the Big Bang led to the formation of stars and solar systems, mainly through the action of gravity. But we know of no comparable force that could explain how ever-expanding material bodies would develop into conscious life.
>
> — Colin McGinn[^5]

The problem that holds for consciousness holds for moral values and norms as well. It is hard to explain how objective moral values and norms could develop out of mere lifeless matter. From the point of view of theism, the origin of both consciousness and morality becomes understandable, because the origin of reality is a perfectly good and omniscient person. Man is created in the image of God, so that the foundation of morality lies in God's nature.

Even many naturalists acknowledge that moral values are something strange in a naturalistically understood universe. J. L. Mackie writes:

> "If – – there are -– – moral values, they make the existence of a god more probable than it would have been without them. Thus we have – – a defensible argument from morality to the existence of a god."
>
> — J. L. Mackie[^6]

Paul Draper agrees with Mackie and states: "A moral world – – is very probable on theism."[^7].

From the point of view of morality, the concept of the person is of central importance. Self-awareness, value, free will, the intrinsic value of human beings, rationality and deep human relationships are all bound up with the concept of the person. The concept of the person, in turn, is hard to explain if the origin of everything is understood to be impersonal. If instead the origin of reality is a personal God, the existence of personal beings becomes understandable. The idea of man as a being created in the image of God explains the intrinsic value of human beings and gives morality its foundation.

The moral argument thus seeks to offer a rational reason to believe in the existence of God by showing that without a perfectly good and intelligent person the whole of moral reality would rest on nothing. If the origin of the universe is an entirely impersonal and random process, morality cannot have the significance that we as human beings give it.

### The indirect moral argument

#### The moral argument as the basis of an indirect proof of God

The moral argument can be used as the basis of an indirect proof of God as follows:

1. If God did not exist, life would be ultimately meaningless.
1. Life cannot be ultimately meaningless.
1. **Therefore:** God must exist.

The second premise of this argument is either felt to be true or not, and the argument can work precisely for those people who grant that it is true, either immediately or after reflection.[^8] For the argument to work, the first premise must of course also be accepted, and that is what the following sections set out to justify.

#### The meaninglessness of life without God

So this approach does not try to prove the existence of God directly; it first concentrates on examining the meaninglessness of life without God.[^9]

##### Dostoevsky and the problem of suffering
The famous Russian writer **Fyodor Dostoevsky** reflected on the problem of suffering in his books. Dostoevsky was tormented by the question why God allowed so much evil and suffering in the world. Dostoevsky saw two factors that he considered significant for the problem of suffering and the existence of God.[^10]

1. Innocent suffering may ennoble a person's character and draw them closer to God.
1. On the other hand, he concluded that if there is no God, we drift into moral relativism, in which no act can be condemned.

Dostoevsky's second view is interesting because it goes to the heart of the whole question "why is there so much evil in the world if God exists?". Without absolute truth it is hard to define the difference between evil and good. Absolute truth means that there is a real "right" and a real "wrong". These are universally and unchangeably true whether or not anyone believes in them.[^11] The opposite of this view is moral relativism, which stresses a relative understanding of right and wrong. In practice this shows in statements that stress everyone's right to do whatever feels good. Eastern religions among others largely teach moral relativism and base their ethics on it. This in turn leads almost inevitably to detachment from everyday reality, which cannot function on the basis of moral relativism. If we try to apply the Hindu guru Paramahansa Yogananda's remark "I don't take life seriously at all...it is all a dream"[^12] to everyday life, we immediately run into insurmountable problems. A society cannot function without taking life seriously.

In practice we constantly make decisions and choices within the framework of certain moral views. We do not regard slight speeding as being as bad as poisoning a city's drinking water. Such things seem self-evident, but in fact they are not. We have learned certain patterns of behaviour that rest on the existence of right and wrong. In everyday life an emotion-based way of choosing works, but when we come to the logical coherence of our worldview, we have to look beneath the surface.

##### It feels wrong?
When Frederick Copleston debated the famous atheist Bertrand Russell, he asked him: "Mr Russell, you believe in good and bad, don't you?" Russell said yes. "How do you distinguish between good and bad?" Copleston went on. "In the same way as between yellow and blue," Russell replies. Copleston pressed further: "But Mr Russell, you distinguish blue from yellow by seeing them, don't you? How do you distinguish good from bad?" Russell's answer sums up the moral problem of atheism: "On the basis of feelings - how else?"[^13]

If feelings alone are the means of telling good from evil, we are thrown out of the ditch of relativism into the surf of chaos. It felt right to Hitler to kill six million Jews in concentration camps. It felt right to Dr Mengele to advance medicine by performing unimaginably cruel experiments on people shut up in concentration camps. We can also ask: what is the feeling that something is right? An electrochemical reaction in the brain? What makes it a more right electrochemical reaction than the electrochemical reaction that judges something to be evil but still urges one to do it?

##### Is a human being worth more than the HI virus?
In practice most people would answer yes. We rank different forms of life largely by how far we can identify with their suffering. An animal may be killed if it does considerable harm to human life. Although many animal-rights activists protest against this, that is nevertheless the general practice. The fight against the HI virus, which causes AIDS, is fierce all over the world. Efforts are made to restrict the virus's living space and freedom of movement, dozens of laboratories and postgraduate students sweat to find the chemicals that will wipe out the species for good, and the marketing managers of pharmaceutical companies drum their fingers waiting for the first drugs, so that they can spread substances aimed at destroying the HI virus to the very last Salvation Army night shelter.

Are the Serbs' genocides of Albanians not justified, if a human being is just an animal? What, in the end, is the difference between an Albanian and the HI virus, if both are products of a Big Bang that happened by chance, and both appear for only a vanishingly brief moment in a history of a few billion years?

##### May you steal if you are not caught?
If we define as right whatever furthers the survival of our species (we do not even raise the question why the survival of the species is more right than its extinction), we are professing a kind of selfish moral principle. If the survival of the species is the criterion of right and wrong, there is presumably nothing wrong in stealing from a stranger. The words of the philosopher Michael Ruse echo the atheistic theory of evolution: "Morality is just an aid to survival and reproduction...and any deeper meaning is illusory."[^14] When you steal your sausage from the Esso station in Siilinjärvi, you further your own survival, and so your species benefits too (unless you are from Savo). Hitler decided that his species was the Aryans and that the Jews were bacteria, i.e. a different species. Did this entitle Hitler to act as he did?

#### Who are you, Absolute?

The moral argument for the existence of God in its indirect form does not prove the existence of God directly; it concentrates on showing that life would be meaningless in the (hypothetical) case that there were no God. Within the moral argument God is approached as the highest moral absolute, who ultimately determines what is good and what is not, and so both gives morality an objective foundation and thereby makes a meaningful life possible.[^15]

Ray Cotton says this of God as absolute truth:

> According to some philosophers, defining right and wrong is arbitrary for God. God is not arbitrary. He is the source of all life and so the source of truth. We would have no basis on which even to define the concept "arbitrary" if we could not refer to an unchanging God.[^16]

God's unchangeability is in the final analysis a fact that is not relative.

According to the Christian view, man is the image of God and in an entirely different position from the animals. This view starts from the basic premise that God is absolute truth and therefore ultimately decides what is right and what is wrong. Christianity thus acknowledges and recognises absolute truth. Since the very basic thesis of relativism, "there are no absolutes", is itself an absolute claim, relativism is illogical. In Christianity the Bible is understood as the revelation of absolute truth to humankind, and the view it represents is given priority when ethical or moral-philosophical questions are dealt with.
