# Anselm's proof of God

*Topics: [God](../topics/jumala.md).*

This page focuses on aspects of [Anselm of Canterbury's](../articles/anselm-canterburylainen.md) original argument for [the existence of God](jumalan-olemassaolo.md). The argument's significance in the history of philosophy and its variants are treated elsewhere, under the heading [ontological argument](ontologinen-argumentti.md).

### Starting point of this treatment

The main source for this page is the [article](http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anselm/) written by Thomas Williams and published as part of the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, especially its section **2.3 The argument of the *Proslogion***.

At this point, however, Williams does not base his account directly on Anselm's work *Proslogion* (lit. 'address') but on how Anselm answered the criticism that Gaunilo, a monk of his own time, directed against the *Proslogion* argument. Williams holds that this reply shows best what Anselm had meant to say from the start.

### On the naming of the argument

"Ontological argument" is a name introduced only by [Immanuel Kant](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant), who lived some seven hundred years after Anselm (and who opposed the argument itself). In the Middle Ages the name used was "Anselm's argument" (Latin *argumentum Anselmi*).

In choosing the title of this article, it was taken into account that Anselm also put forward other well-known and apologetically significant arguments, which may later be presented in articles of their own.

### Anselm's argument

#### Form of the argument

The logical form of Anselm's argument is [*modus ponens*](../articles/argumentaatioanalyysi.md):

1. That than which nothing greater can be thought can be thought.
1. If that than which nothing greater can be thought can be thought, it really exists.
1. **Therefore:** That than which nothing greater can be thought really exists.

*Modus ponens* is indisputably a logically valid and binding form of inference, so the only remaining question is how well Anselm succeeds in justifying the first two premises.

#### Justification of the argument

To justify the first premise, Anselm shows how the concept of that than which nothing greater can be thought can be formed from the experience and understanding we have of things than which something greater **can** be thought:

> [W]e are quite able to form a thought of that than which nothing greater can be thought, starting from that than which something greater can be thought. Who, for example, could not think – – that if something that has both a beginning and an end is good, then something that has a beginning but never ceases to exist is much better – – likewise, something that has neither a beginning nor an end is better still, even though it is constantly moving from the past through the present into the future – – [a]nd something that has no need or compulsion whatever to change or move is much better even than that, whether or not anything of the kind really exists? Can such a thing not be thought? Can anything greater than this be thought? Is this not rather an example of a thought of that than which nothing greater can be thought, formed from the things than which something greater can be thought? So there certainly is a way to form a thought of that than which nothing greater can be thought.
> Anselm's reply to Gaunilo
>
> — 8

Once we have formed this thought of that than which nothing greater can be thought, Anselm says, we can see that the being in question has properties that cannot belong to anything whose existence is possible but not actual – in other words, we can establish that the second premise of the inference is true as well.

For example, a being that could fail to exist is less great than one that exists necessarily. Now if that than which nothing greater can be thought does not exist, then it is certainly possible for it not to exist; in that case, even if it did exist, it would after all not be that than which nothing greater can be thought. It is therefore logically impossible to deny that if that than which nothing greater can be thought can be thought – that is, if it is a possible being – then it also really exists.

#### The argument from the standpoint of alethic modal logic

Alethic [modal logic](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/modaalilogiikka) studies the logical relations between the degrees of certainty of propositions. From the standpoint of its questions, Anselm's argument can be interpreted as follows:

* Existence is either impossible, [contingent](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kontingentti) or necessary. (a universally valid axiom of alethic modal logic)
* **Therefore:** God's existence is either impossible, contingent or necessary. (a direct consequence of the preceding)
* God's existence is not impossible. (follows from the first premise of Anselm's argument)
* God's existence is not contingent. (follows from the second premise of Anselm's argument)
* **Therefore:** God's existence is necessary. (follows from the three preceding statements by the logically indisputable disjunctive syllogism, i.e. *modus tollendo ponens*)
* **Therefore:** God really exists. (a direct consequence of the preceding)

It also follows that every claim whose force depends, directly or indirectly, on assuming that God's existence is (at most) contingent is so glaringly wrong that it must be regarded as downright absurd (which is a different thing from meaningless). It is impossible that God could exist or not exist independently of whether the rest of reality exists. Whoever speaks of something whose existence is presupposed to be contingent is not speaking of God (at least not rationally).

### Aspects of the argument

#### The character of Anselm's theology

At the core of Anselm's theology is the idea of "faith seeking understanding" (Latin *fides quaerens intellectum*). This idea holds a great deal:
1. Christian faith does not arise from human reasoning but as a gift of God, from revelation and by God's working (through the means of grace);
1. faith, once born, shows itself as love for God and seeks to know him more deeply, with the understanding as well;
1. in this way faith challenges the understanding to give an account of its object and to look for logically valid grounds for it;
1. this search of the understanding leads to discoveries that do not replace faith resting on revelation but on the one hand confirm it and on the other always remain subordinate to it;
1. although the understanding can open to the knowledge of God only after faith has been born, it can express its new discoveries in a way that even unbelievers can understand;
1. having looked into the matter, the understanding of unbelievers can see, and is forced to admit, that the content of the understanding opened by faith is irrefutable;
1. this in itself does not yet turn unbelievers into believers;
1. even unbelievers, however, can now understand something of the content of faith's understanding (or of understanding faith) and realize that they cannot refute it;
1. by God's grace those humbled in this way can receive the gift of faith from God, which would have been impossible had they remained in foolish pride;
1. in any case, unbelief in this way loses its reputation for wisdom, and the understanding opened by faith serves to make the truth known among people.

Although Anselm's theological thought contains much more than apologetics proper, what has been said already shows that it also has a clear apologetic dimension.

#### Background of the argument

The background of the argument is the statement of the Bible that those who think there is no God are fools (Psalm 14:1; 53:1). With a single, unifying argument Anselm wanted to show how the very idea of God's non-existence shows all its adherents to be fools, and so to open the understanding to grasp this truth revealed in the Bible better.

#### Topicality of the argument

The comparison of God's existence with the existence of mythological beings of antiquity such as fauns or unicorns, which has come up especially in connection with [New Atheism](../articles/uusateismi.md), is a current example of exactly the kind of foolishness that Anselm's argument addresses; the same can be said of the bus campaign whose slogan was "There's probably no God".

The argument is also topical in relation to the religious pluralism common today, according to which all religions are at bottom "equally good" or even the same: all thinking that does not see the foundation of reality rightly is built on an untenable base. So it is not only atheists who are wrong but equally all others whose religious views contradict indisputable facts.
