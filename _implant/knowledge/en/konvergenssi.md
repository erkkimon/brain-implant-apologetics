# Convergence

*Topics: [Design theory](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md), [Theory of evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*

Convergence means a structural similarity that is explained by the similar function of the structure, in a situation where the presumed common ancestor of the organisms did not have that structure. In such a situation, according to the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md), the structure must have arisen several times independently of one another, so the structure has to be optimal enough to some degree, and on the other hand simple enough, for it to make sense that it arose more than once.

### Convergences in design theory

From the point of view of [design theory](suunnitteluteoria.md), similar structures are explained by a common designer. The same designer would have created different animals, making use of the same working structural solutions as needed. The explanation is not credible, however, if matching structures occur **only** where they should be according to [common descent](polveutumisoppi.md). Instead it would be natural to assume that in most animals that need a similar function, similar structures would have been used.[^1] So design theory expects matching structures to occur in particular also where they should not be on account of common descent, whereas the theory of evolution has to explain the occurrence of the structures by a similar evolutionary pathway being repeated.

### Convergences in the theory of evolution

Convergent evolution can be justified by the shared selection pressure that a shared ecological niche brings, so the idea of convergence as such is in no way contrary to the theory of evolution. The idea of convergent evolution works for simple traits such as speed. Strong selection pressure can easily optimize an organism's build to be more streamlined, its lungs and heart larger and its legs longer. Such convergence is easy to see, for example, between the cheetah and the greyhound.
However, convergences run into problems as soon as the structure in question is even slightly more complex, because evolution is not a goal-directed process. Convergent evolution is quite enough to explain how organisms equipped with similar structural characters thrive in the same ecological niches, but it cannot explain how these similar structural characters would have developed independently of one another. The explanations are at best just stories, and their plausibility is usually not examined by doing real scientific analyses. It is unclear how strongly a similar ecological niche can influence the origin of similar structural characters in such different organisms. For example, the eye or some other comparable photoreceptor would have had to arise through convergent evolution at least 40 times in all, independently of one another. The origin of such complex structural systems even once is remarkably improbable, so the problems of the theory of evolution are only accentuated when convergences require these structures to have developed several times independently of one another. In principle the theory of evolution can be reconciled with every kind of similarity, but since it predicts nothing very precise about where similarities occur, it predicts nothing well. In this sense, similarities in outward form combined with different genetic make-up point more closely to a common designer than to a common evolutionary origin. Convergences make the testability of the theory of evolution highly questionable, when it is claimed to be able to explain anything and it makes no proper predictions about anything.

#### Convergences and homologies

The possibility of convergent evolution also makes taxonomy based on the theory of evolution very challenging, since one cannot be sure whether a shared trait is a sign of relatedness or a case of convergence. In some cases convergences are so similar that biologists cannot tell whether they are looking at [homology](../glossary/sanasto.md#homologia) <!-- red link: homologia --> or convergence until the organisms' other characters are examined. Homology, after all, is a shared structure obtained through common descent. According to the homology argument the common ancestor already had the structure in question, so it would not have had to develop separately in both organisms. Similar structures observed in different species have then been interpreted as evidence of common descent. Case by case, however, one must already "know" the evolutionary relationship between the species before one can know whether it is homology or convergence. So the possibility of convergent evolution in particular cuts the ground from under the whole homology argument.
