# History of the theory of evolution

*Topics: [Theory of evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*


Evolutionary ideas were already put forward by the materialists of ancient Greece, especially Lucretius, whose views required some kind of arising from matter by itself. Lucretius proposed that heads, legs and arms of living creatures arise by themselves in marshes, and sometimes they happen to fit together suitably.

Before [Blaise Pascal's](../articles/blaise-pascal.md) sterile experiments it was commonly believed that rats, for example, arise by themselves if you leave a sack of grain lying at the back of a room. Speculations about the spontaneous origin of life are still common today, and they suffer from the same lack of evidence and from the fact that the community does not want to put its speculations under hard and public self-criticism.

Charles Darwin is generally regarded as the father of the theory of evolution; he presented his theory of development, known as Darwinism, in 1859 in his book The Origin of Species. According to Darwin's idea, the changes that the common descent of biodiversity requires would have come about through small changes and natural selection.

#### The birth of Darwinism

By 1831 Charles Darwin had already studied at university, but he did not yet have any training in natural science. Even so, he joined the *HMS Beagle*, a ship sailing around the world, as its naturalist. For the theory of evolution, the most important part of the whole voyage was a month on the [Galápagos Islands](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galapagossaaret), a remote archipelago west of Ecuador. There he noticed that every island seemed to have a finch type of its very own. Even on a single island, different finch species lived in different niches. Yet they clearly came from the same stock.

Darwin writes in The Origin of Species that what he saw convinced him that species do not stay unchanged but are able to adapt to new conditions. All that remained was the question of how the change happens.

Darwin tells how, on 3 October 1838, he had a sudden insight while reading Thomas Malthus's work on the growth of populations. Malthus's work claimed that a population grows explosively unless something checks it. Darwin thought that out of the resulting surplus, those that had changes favorable to the conditions succeeded, and those that had unfavourable changes disappeared. This principle is called natural selection. The conditions, that is the surrounding nature, select which individuals survive. When the conditions change, the species too develops to fit the new conditions.

Just as Darwin was about to finish his main work, it turned out that Alfred Russell Wallace, in the Malay Archipelago, had reached the same conclusions as Darwin.

Darwin's main work *[The Origin of Species](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lajien_synty) <!-- red link: Lajien synty -->* appeared on 24 November 1859. The edition of 1,250 copies sold out the next day.

Charles Darwin's theories met with a great deal of opposition as soon as they were published, because they dropped man from his place as the crown of creation into the ranks of the other species. The idea of man as a relative of the animals aroused disgust, and newspapers printed cartoons of Darwin with his ape cousins. Another thought was that people would become cruel and immoral, because they are descended from animals.

#### Alfred Russell Wallace

*Picture: Alfred Russell Wallace (1823-1913) developed a theory of selection almost identical to Darwin's. Wallace did not, however, consider that the variation of domestic animals showed that species had arisen as the result of purposeless processes*

Before Darwin's *Origin of Species*, A. R. Wallace published an article on evolution through natural selection in 1858.

Wallace believed that evolution explains several things in the living world, but he considered the mechanism Darwin put forward insufficient to explain everything in biology. He also considered several of the pieces of evidence Darwin gave for the powers of mutation and natural selection insufficient, for example the guided variation of dog breeds. Wallace was an early supporter of design theory (ID, intelligent design).[^1][^2]

#### Charles Darwin

*Picture: Charles Darwin's grandfather Erasmus Darwin set out the essential claims of the theory of evolution in his work Zoonomia (1794).*

Darwin is often claimed to have invented the theory of evolution, and even, especially in the popular media[^3], to have believed in creation before his voyage on the Beagle. An important part in the birth of Darwinism was played by Charles Darwin's grandfather [Erasmus Darwin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Darwin), who came to the conclusion that all living things are descendants of a common ancestor. He placed man as a relative of the apes. [Jean-Baptiste Lamarck](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Lamarck) built on Erasmus Darwin and tried to explain that, for example, the giraffe's long neck would have arisen from stretching the neck over successive generations. Lamarck held that acquired characteristics are inherited. This claim was later shown to be untrue. So in fact the theory of evolution was, in its essentials, already complete in Darwin's surroundings.

Charles Darwin was a freethinker and materialist from several generations of them[^4]. Materialism was one of the fashionable views of the social elite in Charles Darwin's time. Charles Darwin's grandfather Erasmus Darwin had set out the essential claims of the theory of evolution more than five decades earlier in his influential book Zoonomia. The book contained the ideas of common descent with modification, of sexual selection and of the survival of the strongest. Darwin's father Robert Darwin hid his radical atheism for political motives, and even suggested that Charles train for the clergy because of the comfortable life clergymen led at the time. Before leaving on the Beagle, Darwin worked under Robert Grant, who told Charles his views on the theory of evolution and hired Charles as his research assistant. Evidence was now sought for the grandfather's ideas and for the family's pet theory (the Darwin family's coat of arms bore the motto *"All things out of shells"*). So it was hardly a surprise that Charles interpreted the small variation he saw on the Galapagos Islands in line with his family's theory of evolution.

Darwin's career in natural science was inspired and greatly helped by John Henslow, a professor of botany and a confessing Christian, and by Adam Sedgwick, one of the fathers of modern geology and an Anglican clergyman.

It is interesting that Darwin seemed to suffer from the same inner contradiction as most materialists today: in his heart he wanted man to be able to be good and to take others into account, but at the same time his thinking made morality arbitrary. According to Darwinism, whatever survives better is good.
Darwin opposed slavery, but refused to consider, for example, Bishop Wilberforce's warning that Darwinism would give the defenders of slavery a strong weapon: if one race survives better by enslaving another, what can be wrong with that?
Darwin thought that the nice, civilized British race was the finest product of evolution, which would displace the lower races and the savage native peoples, who were closer to the apes. It is interesting what kind of inner contradiction a shallow faith in science drove Darwin into.

Darwin's theory of evolution differed from his contemporaries' theory in that it was entirely materialistic/atheistic. The theory suited the materialistic intellectual climate and became very popular. Darwin opposed slavery, but on the other hand he regarded dark-skinned people as a lower species that the more civilized human species would soon replace. Darwin also believed in the neuroscience of the time, phrenology, the reading of human traits from the shape of the head. Darwin thought, for example, that his head changed shape because he became more intelligent after he became a scientist.

#### Early criticism
The arguments against Darwinism came from the eminent scientists Charles Lyell and Alfred Wallace, and above all from the former Darwinist and professor of zoology G. Mivart (in his book *On the Genesis of Species*), who largely concentrated their attention on two weak points: (1) On what grounds is it claimed with certainty that evolution is not guided, although the mechanism does not seem sufficient to explain the observations? (2) Why are the considerable differences between man and other organisms played down? Darwin himself found Mivart's arguments frighteningly strong and mostly contented himself with dodging them rhetorically. According to Wiker, the critics typically believed in evolution<!--Mitä evoluutiota tarkoitetaan? Muuntelua? Polveutumisoppia? Standardina pidetään kyllä muuntelua, jos ei toisin mainita, mutta kontekstin perusteella en olis ihan varma.-->, but not in Darwinism, that is, in Darwin's favorite idea that development was unguided, non-teleological, in other words Godless.

Because of his God-denying philosophy, Darwin built a theory that was limited to mutation and natural selection alone, but this mechanism had no scientific evidence. Mivart argued that the incipient stages of biological structures would give no selective advantage, that similar biological structures are seen to develop from different starting points, and that evolution (in whose theistic version Mivart believed) would be expected to proceed in jumps, which the fossil record also supports better.

Lyell's *Principles of Geology* criticized the evolutionary idea of Lamarck and Erasmus Darwin in 1832 on the following grounds:
* Lamarck confuses variation within [species](../glossary/sanasto.md#laji), which does happen, with unlimited variation that would make new species, for which there is no evidence.
* According to observation, species of which we have detailed knowledge over a long period have not changed considerably, but have only varied within the species, if even that.
* Animal breeders have found that however hard one tries, a species cannot be changed beyond certain limits. Moreover, the changes achieved revert if the species is left to itself. So variation is something that already exists in the species' potential.
Several other eminent scientists also put forward criticism of Darwin's theory, but the materialist fashion won.
