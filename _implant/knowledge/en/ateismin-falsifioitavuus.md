# Falsifiability of atheism

*Topics: [Atheism](../topics/ateismi.md).*


Some atheists claim that the existence of God[^1] is not [falsifiable](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilismi) <!-- red link: fallibilismi -->, so there is no reason to believe in it. The question can, however, also be turned around: how, then, might atheism itself be falsifiable?

Many rational grounds have been put forward for [the existence of God](jumalan-olemassaolo.md). Atheists have responded to them either by trying to get around them with [*ad hoc* explanations](../articles/naturalistien-paniikkiratkaisut.md) or by [labeling](../articles/aukkojen-jumala-v2.md) them [appeals to ignorance](../articles/argumentum-ad-ignorantiam.md). At the same time, not a single piece of positive evidence has been offered for atheism itself. This gives reason to ask:
* Does atheism depend on any rational arguments at all?
* What kind of conceivable evidence or argument might be enough, even in the atheists' own view, to refute atheism, that is, to prove the existence of God[^1]?

### Evidence on the table?

Couldn't atheism then be falsified easily, simply by proving the existence of some personal god? After all, falsifying atheism only requires objective, verifiable and repeatable evidence, or a watertight logical argument, for any theistic god whatsoever. Such evidence just has not been published yet. If God existed, convincing an individual atheist of theism ought to be easy, right?

This view, however, does not do justice to the question. The repeatability mentioned above is a very useful criterion when we study the regularities that hold in the world, that is, [the laws of nature](../articles/luonnonlaki.md). If someone claims that whenever two bodies collide, their combined momentum at the start equals their combined momentum at the end: m<sub>1</sub>v<sub>1a</sub> + m<sub>2</sub>v<sub>2a</sub> = m<sub>1</sub>v<sub>1l</sub> + m<sub>2</sub>v<sub>2l</sub>, anyone can verify experimentally with a suitable setup whether the rule holds this time too. Ideally, a single deviation from the rule would show the rule to be false. If the rule has nevertheless proved valid enough times, it is not abandoned altogether; it is usually modified by restricting its validity with certain additional conditions. The criterion of repeatability is useful in practice, but it is not logically valid, because it rests on [induction](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/induktiivinen_päättely), that is, on generalization. Usually, however, it is considered reliable enough that scientific theories and laws of nature are built on it, and it is sometimes even believed that our understanding of these laws is the most reliable knowledge we can have.

The usefulness of the criterion of repeatability is essentially limited by the fact that it can verify only laws and regularities. Using the criterion of repeatability presupposes believing that such laws exist at all, that is, that the regularities we observe are not mere chance which we now call a law of nature but would not wonder at in the least had things turned out otherwise. Furthermore, if a person wants all knowledge to be based on repeatability, he believes, in other words, that everything that can possibly be known is caused solely by laws of nature. Such a belief is not derived from scientific observational data or from the logical reasoning of pure reason; it is a naturalistic position. Supernatural things are by definition irregular: "An entity or phenomenon independent of the laws continuously prevailing in nature and of the effects of closed causal events, which nevertheless itself has causal effects." Denying the existence of supernatural phenomena on the grounds that they are not repeatable is then circular reasoning.

If an atheist demanded that the existence of God be provable by repeatable experiments that can be repeated anywhere, he would in other words be claiming that God should be some law of nature. For if repeated experiments looked only for God's action, so that only such action of God as was fully repeatable counted as reliably verified, the atheist could always say that such regular action is just one law of nature among others and needs no gods to explain it.

### God cannot be seen

On the other hand, an atheist could suggest that even if God were not a law of nature measurable everywhere, God should nevertheless be objectively observable, so that everyone could observe him and critically evaluate and compare other people's accounts of what they saw. God should therefore be observable and measurable like people and physical objects, and since no such opportunity has been arranged, believing in God is not meaningful either. The atheist would not accept experiences of God's presence as observations, because not everyone experiences the presence of any god, and such an observation does not allow verifiable measurements. In practice all determinable measurements in physics are based on visual observation, so the proposal could also be put like this: "believing in God would be meaningful if God could be seen."

Such a criterion might be meaningful when speaking of gods claimed to be beings within the universe, such as the Hindu gods. According to the Judaeo-Christian view, however, God is a being outside the universe who himself created the whole universe. Visual observation is based on electromagnetic radiation, emitted or reflected by bodies with mass, reaching our eyes. God, however, has no properties internal to the universe, such as mass, so to begin with there is no reason why he should be visible. It is of course another matter if he decides to be visible, for example by creating out of nothing the photons that reach the observer's eyes.

A visible appearance of God and a permanent presence that no one could escape would certainly be a very impressive revelation, but even from this one could not yet conclude that God exists. It could be some kind of trick or mass delusion. According to the Bible, however, God because of his holiness cannot let anything sinful come near him. Moreover, what matters to God is not so much that a person merely believes in his existence under compulsion, but rather whether the person wants to love God or to reject him.

### A watertight logical argument?

If God is a being outside the universe, and even if his existence cannot be established by repeated experiments, shouldn't it still be possible to present some purely logical proof, so that there would be reason to believe in God's existence?

As an idea this may sound good, but the problem is that [no proof](../articles/todistus.md) is completely watertight in the sense that it could not be disputed from some philosophical point of view. Instead, the traditional proofs of God aim to show that believing in God is reasonable and has good grounds.

### God would surely know what would be sufficient evidence

*Main article: ["Why doesn't God reveal himself clearly?"](../articles/miksei-jumala-ilmoita-itseään-selvästi-v2.md)*

Some atheists say that if God existed, he would surely know what evidence would suffice to convert an atheist. Surely an [omnipotent](jumalan-kaikkivoipuus.md) and omniscient being can come up with sufficient evidence, and if it is a loving God, he also wants to show the atheist that he exists.

This thought, reasonable enough in itself, does not stand up to closer scrutiny. The omnipotence of the Christian God works within the bounds of logic[^2], so we should not assume he would come up with anything logically contradictory. God could, for example, force the atheist to believe in him, but then God would have to interfere with human [free will](../articles/vapaa-tahto.md). So if God wanted to give people irrefutable knowledge of himself, he would have to take away people's ability to question, or alternatively plant an irrefutable proof of his existence in people's minds. It is questionable, however, whether a completely irrefutable proof can even exist.

What can be seen instead is that the world offers sufficient grounds for belief in God, if a person only wants to believe. A person can also believe in atheism if he wants to.

### An illustrative example

1. Assume that God exists.
1. Assume that God is omnipotent.
1. Assume that God also uses his omnipotence and acts on causal processes within history.
1. Assume the following fictional course of events:

#### The case of Mauri

Mauri belongs to the local Orthodox-Catholic-Lutheran-Pentecostal congregation and has just arrived at church for a prayer meeting. Unexpectedly, a local animist whose hobbies are live-action role-playing, martial arts and Swedish milk has a fit of madness, shouts curses in Norwegian and punches Mauri in the face. Mauri's head comes off. Oh well, these things happen.

Mighty brothers and sisters lay their hands just above the midpoint of Mauri's shoulders, anoint his neck with oil and pray powerfully for God to heal Mauri. Someone even gets round to calling a hearse. God answers this prayer and creates a new head, completely identical to the one that came off, but attached to Mauri's body so that Mauri's vital functions return and he feels shower-fresh. On waking, Mauri says he went through [frightening experiences](../articles/kuolemanrajakokemukset.md) and saw fire and brimstone, and that Jesus told Mauri to punch himself in the face because his time had not yet come. After waking, Mauri punches himself in the face, that is, repents, and spends the rest of his life preaching to theology students about the coming judgment. A Christian apologist who was present collects the severed head with silk gloves and preserves it for further study. He writes a story about it in the latest issue of Uusi tie. In his view, this is exceptionally irrefutable evidence of God's existence and action.

#### The case of Erkki

Assume further that Erkki was present, a caricature of an intellectually dishonest form of atheism in which belief in the truth of atheism cannot be shaken by any facts. Nothing can convince Erkki of his own fallibility, yet he considers himself the very model of rationality, because he imagines that all the reasonable people he knows think as he does.

Erkki watches the situation for a moment in astonishment and soon reaches a conclusion: the observation does not fit very nicely with the prediction of any natural phenomenon he knows, but the case can nevertheless be explained rationally: just the right atoms happened to tunnel quantum-mechanically out of the ground to where Mauri's head was. This is of course astronomically improbable, but it is simply in line with the anthropic principle, considering that the history of the universe contains an estimated 10^150 physical events and that there may be any number of universes, so somewhere this is bound to happen sooner or later. "I wouldn't be here pondering this if it hadn't happened by chance just now." And if there are infinitely many universes, this same phenomenon recurs infinitely many times anyway. So nothing new in the multiverse. As knowledge accumulates, some law of nature may of course be found that explains the observation even better than tunnelling, but even this is a better explanation than appealing to [a God of the gaps](../articles/aukkojen-jumala-v2.md), who would vanish anyway as knowledge grows.

Thinking over what happened later on, Erkki concludes that it was, almost beyond doubt, Mauri's identical twin brother, who had been missing. This, after all, is the only reasonable explanation for Mauri's DNA samples matching those of the severed head, and for the heads looking alike. The [mass suggestion](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/suggestio) produced by the religious occasion, together with the [mass psychosis](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/psykoosi) produced by the suddenness and traumatic nature of the event, caused people to perceive and remember what happened wrongly. In reality the rampaging man had apparently punched both Mauri and Mauri's brother in the face, but only in the brother's case did it make the head come off and cause death. The rampager apparently took Mauri's body with him and left, or forgot, the head at the scene. The severed head, the amount of blood and Mauri's coming round during the prayer were thus construed in the minds of those present as one person dying and coming back to life. Not every detail can of course be confirmed beyond doubt, but the miraculous healing has nevertheless been shown to be a false explanation. Erkki congratulates himself on having managed to stay coldly, rationally scientific about the matter, and on agreeing with the scientific world.
