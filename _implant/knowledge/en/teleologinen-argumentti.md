# Teleological argument

*Topics: [Philosophy](../topics/filosofia.md), [Design theory](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md), [Arguments](../topics/argumentit.md).*

The starting point of the teleological argument is the purposiveness (*telos* = end) and order manifest in nature. The best explanation for purposiveness is that behind everything lies an intelligent designer.

The variants of the argument developed before the 1700s were based on the idea that the universe appeared to be ordered for a specific purpose. In India, this argument was used by the so-called Nyaya school (100-1000 AD), which justified the existence of God by appealing to the order present in the world.[^1] According to the variant presented by Plato, there are two teachings that lead humans to believe in gods. The first of these is the teaching about the soul. "The second is our teaching about the ordered motion followed by the stars and other bodies, which are governed by reason, the creator of the order of the universe." (Laws 966E)

[Thomas Aquinas's](tuomas-akvinolainen.md) fifth way of proving the existence of God draws attention to the fact that many beings without intelligence act in a purposive manner.

> We see, then, that certain things without knowledge, namely natural bodies, act for an end. This is evident in that they always or for the most part act in the same way to achieve what is best. Therefore, it is clear that they do not reach the end by chance but by design. But things without knowledge do not direct themselves to an end unless some knowing and understanding being directs them, as an archer directs an arrow to the target. Therefore, there is some understanding being that directs all natural bodies to an end, and we call him God.
>
> — Aquinas[^2]

For example, biological systems function purposively and as if according to an intelligent plan.

With the development of modern natural science thinking, in newer variants of the teleological argument one no longer appeals merely to the order prevailing in the universe, but rather more attention is paid to the complexity of the world's order. [William Paley](../pages/william-paley.md) (1743-1805) takes, for example, the situation where we find a [pocket watch in a meadow](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelloseppävertaus) <!-- red link: Kelloseppävertaus -->. Since the structure of the watch is complex and intricately built for a specific purpose, we assume that it has an intelligent designer. According to Paley, we can meaningfully apply the same reasoning to plants and animals, because their structure manifests traces of a complex and advanced intelligent plan.

Generally, it is assumed that [David Hume](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume) <!-- red link: David Hume --> (1711–1776) and [Charles Darwin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin) <!-- red link: Charles Darwin --> (1809-1882) refuted the teleological argument. Hume argued against Paley by stating that, based on our experience, we know that a clock is the result of an intelligent design, but we lack corresponding experience regarding plants and animals. In his work *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Hume did not reject the argument entirely, but in his view, it justifies only a weak conclusion: that the first cause of the order in the universe probably possesses properties that are, in a loose sense, analogous to human intelligence.

Modern variants of the teleological argument have been developed, appealing to the increasingly wondrous [cosmic fine-tuning](kosminen-hienosäätö.md) revealed through the progress of science. The scientific community has been amazed to discover how complex and sensitive a combination of conditions is required for the [origin of intelligent life](../articles/kosminen-lähiömme.md) and its development. The revelation of [cosmic fine-tuning](kosminen-hienosäätö.md) has led many scientists to conclude that such a delicate balance cannot be regarded as mere chance but requires an explanation. This fine-tuning of the universe seems to manifest the presence of [designing intelligence](suunnitteluteoria.md). Assuming an intelligent design based on the fine-tuning of the universe is not analogical reasoning but inference to the best explanation.

John Leslie[^3] speaks of the need to adopt a "natural explanation," which not only explains the matter in question but also reveals why it needs an explanation. For example, suppose that Jari was given a new car as a birthday gift. There are millions of different license plates, so it is very unlikely that his license plate would be CHT 4271. However, such a license plate in a gift car would not arouse any special interest. But suppose that Jari was born on August 8, 1949, and his birthday gift car's license plate was JAR 8849. It would be odd if he merely shrugged his shoulders and said: "Well, the car had to have some license plate, and all numbers are equally unlikely ..."

[William Dembski](william-dembski.md)[^4] has presented a comprehensive theory of the [idea of intelligent design](suunnitteluteoria.md). He has presented a ten-point argument that allows distinguishing random phenomena from designed ones. According to Dembski, the decisive factor is not only that the thing to be explained is an improbable event, but that the event fits some independently given pattern, so that it is an example of "specified complexity." The prevalence of specified complexity in the universe leads us to seek an explanation based on intelligent design rather than appealing to blind chance.

Robin Collins[^5] has presented an argument based on the fine-tuning of the universe using the principle that when comparing two hypotheses, an observation is evidence for the hypothesis in the light of which it is more probable (or less improbable).

> 1. The fine-tuning of the universe is not very improbable on theism.
> 2. The fine-tuning of the universe is very improbable on the atheistic single-universe hypothesis.[^6]

In light of the principle stated above and these two premises, Collins concludes that the theistic idea of intelligent design receives support from the fine-tuning of the world compared to the atheistic single-universe hypothesis.

An atheist may adopt the multiverse hypothesis, according to which our own universe is just one of many and by chance the one in which life could have arisen. In this case, the problem of cosmic fine-tuning is merely shifted to another frame of reference, and the argument presented above can be used in this new frame of reference.

Modern defenders of the teleological argument also appeal to the fact that the laws of the universe embody beauty, elegance, and harmony. Thus, scientists may also rely on aesthetic values in their research. Aesthetic aspects can serve as criteria for distinguishing theories and as guidelines for theory formation. Judith Wechsler draws attention to the fact that "when speaking of their own and other researchers' work, scientists often use terms such as 'beauty' and 'economy' with a kind of laudatory delight that is more characteristic of painting, music, and poetry."[^7]

Aesthetic aspects sometimes affect the formation of scientific concepts and the construction of models, as well as the evaluation of scientific theories. Physicist Werner Heisenberg said to Einstein:

> You may think that, in speaking of simplicity and beauty, I apply aesthetic criteria to truth, and I openly admit that I am strongly affected by the simplicity and beauty of the mathematical models that nature offers us. You too must have felt the almost terrifying simplicity and harmony of those relations that nature suddenly spreads before us – –.
>
> — Wechsler[^7]

The well-known English theoretical physicist Paul Dirac expresses a similar view.

> It is more important to get beauty into your equations than to make them agree with experiments – – If one strives to get beauty into one's equations and has a truly healthy outlook, one can be sure of progress.
>
> — Davies[^8]

Timothy Ferris comments on Dirac's statement:

> Dirac of course did not mean that empirical results should be completely ignored, but that a beautiful theory does not need to be discarded merely because it fails in some preliminary experiment. He had in mind Erwin Schrödinger's reluctance to publish his wave mechanics equations solely because they were at odds with the experimental body of facts. "It is very important for a theory to be beautiful," Dirac told science writer Horace Freeland Judson. "And if the observations do not support it, do not be too worried, but wait a while until it becomes clear whether the observations might possibly be erroneous."
>
> — Ferris[^9]

Such an assumption of the beauty, elegance, or harmony of the universe's structure is sensible if the universe is the result of God's intelligent design. Robert Collins[^10], however, argues that it is not sensible to assume that the laws of the universe are beautiful or elegant if they are not the result of intelligent design. Thus, theism provides a more sensible background for this fact than atheism.

Is the teleological argument valid and convincing? To identify intelligent design, it is essential to be able to eliminate its two competitors, chance and physical necessity. Physical necessity could explain the specified complexity manifesting in the world only if there existed, as yet unknown, a set of laws that could be condensed into a theory of the universe explaining what the universe is like. According to this possibility, the universe must be as it is, and there is little chance that the universe would not be life-permitting. This possibility is entirely speculative and would merely shift the fine-tuning argument one step back. Explaining an ordered universe based solely on chance also encounters serious problems.

The philosopher [Antony Flew](antony-flew.md), who became known as a champion of atheism, considers specifically the modern variants of the teleological argument sufficiently convincing intellectually for him to deem it justified to abandon atheism and adopt theism instead. Flew notes that Darwin did not even claim in his [theory of evolution](../articles/evoluutioteoria.md) to explain the [origin of life](../articles/abiogeneesi.md), but only the mechanisms that guide the development of organisms.

> It seems to me that [Richard Dawkins](../articles/richard-dawkins.md) constantly overlooks what Darwin himself says in the fourteenth chapter of his work *Origin of Species*. There he notes that his entire argument starts from an organism that is capable of reproduction. This is the product of evolution that a truly comprehensive theory of evolution should explain. Darwin himself was very aware that he had not provided such an explanation. It now seems to me that over fifty years of DNA research have produced material for a new and overwhelmingly convincing argument in favor of [intelligent design](suunnitteluteoria.md).
> Flew
>
> — quoted by Habermas[^11]

Since Flew believes in the existence of God on the basis of the teleological argument, he considers his work *God and Philosophy*, built on the foundation of atheistic thinking, to be outdated and regards it as suitable only as a historical document without direct relevance to current discussions.

The teleological argument does not prove the existence of God, but it shows that the fine-tuning of the universe provides grounds for considering theism as a more epistemically justified view than atheism.
