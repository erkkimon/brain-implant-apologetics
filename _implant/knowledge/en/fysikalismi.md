# Physicalism

*Topics: [Worldview](../topics/maailmankatsomus.md), [Ideologies](../topics/aatteet.md).*


**Physicalism**, or **philosophical materialism**, is an [ontological](../glossary/sanasto.md#ontologia) position according to which everything that exists is [physical](../glossary/sanasto.md#fysikaalinen); that is, ultimately nothing exists other than
* *matter*, which has either always existed or somehow appeared by itself, and
* the *laws of nature* that govern matter and somehow exist by themselves.
Proponents of physicalism have included, for example, **[Lucretius](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius)** in the first century and later **[Paul d'Holbach](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_d'Holbach)**,  **[Ludwig Büchner](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Buchner)** and **[Richard Vitzthum](http://www.infidels.org/library/modern/richard_vitzthum/vitzthum-bio.html)**. In Finland this position is represented, for example, by the physicist and anti-philosopher **[Kari Enqvist](../articles/kari-enqvist.md)**.

The consistent physicalist's conception of reality is necessarily narrow, since physicalism, following the objects that modern physics studies, recognizes only the existence of [particles](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/alkeishiukkanen) and [fields](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kenttäteoria). The things that matter most to human beings, such as [love](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakkaus) <!-- red link: rakkaus -->, [freedom of the will](../articles/vapaa-tahto.md) and the reliability of reason <!-- red link: järjen luotettavuus -->, beauty, goodness, values, [morality](moraalinen-argumentti.md), truth and meaning, then have to be explained by the purposeless and aimless motion of particles, without any ultimate meaning. On this view, reality dies at the latest in the [heat death of the universe](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maailmankaikkeuden_lopullinen_kohtalo), and human endeavors, science included, are in the end entirely temporary.

### Problems of definition

Physicalism, like naturalism, is strictly speaking hard to define. What really are "matter" and "materiality"? Modern cosmology postulates[^1] unknown forms of matter such as [dark matter](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/pimeä_aine) and [dark energy](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/pimeä_energia) in order to explain observations better. In principle, any entity a physicalist wants to believe in can be defined as an unknown form of matter, if the concept of matter is flexible enough. So far there is no clear way to define matter. Usually, however, matter is defined by the fundamental constituents of matter in the physics of the day; in practice, then, physicalism claims that nothing exists except things made of the kinds of matter that physics has found so far.

### The inconsistency of ordinary physicalism

Many physicalists even consider physicalism an intellectually courageous position. Perhaps for this reason it is a fairly common belief, although apparently no proper arguments have been offered for it, while there are several rational arguments against it. The intellectual courage of this view thus seems to be a modern myth.

Probably the best argument offered for physicalism is the following: we observe many physical things, and if we do not observe anything else, there is no reason to believe in anything else. This way of thinking is often called [scientism](../articles/tieteisusko.md). This argument has problems, however:
1. Typically the physicalist restricts observations so that only observations referring to the material level count as credible or "scientific." The physicalist is therefore not open to evidence against their position, and the whole position is mere circular reasoning. In reality, observations that count against physicalism include, for example, the historical evidence for [the resurrection of Jesus](jeesuksen-ylösnousemus.md), experiences of miraculous healing, experiences of God's presence, evidence from consciousness research for a non-material level, and [near-death experiences](../articles/kuolemanrajakokemukset.md).
1. Even if we had no evidence of non-material things, it would of course not follow that they do not exist. Even if some primitive tribe in Africa had no observations of the existence of cars, they would not be justified in claiming to *know* that cars do not exist.[^2] A question that cannot be settled with good reason on the information available should be left open.

Moreover, physicalism appears to be self-contradictory. By the physicalist's own belief, their thoughts result from the motion of particles and fields. So the motion of particles and fields has produced the physicalist's belief in physicalism and keeps it up. There is no reason to assume that the motion of particles would produce true thoughts in particular, so the physicalist should not trust their own thinking.[^3] In fact, the very fact that our thinking nevertheless seems quite reliable is evidence against physicalism and for theism: a rational God has given us the ability to understand a rational creation. This was also the foundation on which modern natural science arose. Unless the physicalist can offer a justification[^4] for why the contents of their beliefs would probably be reliable, it is not rational[^5] for them to believe in physicalism.

### Physicalism and morality

Physicalism also seems to make a consistently grounded morality impossible. A physicalist can of course believe that moral views and beliefs exist[^6] as a phenomenon caused by the structure of the brain, ultimately a physical one. But it is hard, if not impossible, for them to justify the rationality of moral views, for example for the following reasons:
1. It makes no sense to hold anyone responsible for anything they could not have done or refrained from doing. According to physicalism all human actions are physically compelled, so holding a person responsible for them makes about as much sense as holding a person responsible for the expansion of the universe or the value of the gravitational constant. Physicalism thus removes moral responsibility,[^7] and a moral view that no one could be held responsible for neglecting does not seem meaningful.
1. Nor does it make sense to obligate anyone to do something they are physically prevented from doing, or to refrain from something they are physically compelled to do. Physicalism therefore removes moral obligations as well. Without obligations, moral claims can have hardly any meaningful content. Yet in practice it is impossible for people to live without acting on their beliefs about obligations and without holding on to those beliefs.[^8]
1. By [Hume's guillotine](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humen_giljotiini), how things ought to be cannot be inferred from how things are. Moral views therefore cannot be derived from any non-moral belief. If a physicalist has moral beliefs, then, they cannot have been inferred from any non-moral beliefs about physical states of affairs. The only possible sources of these beliefs would seem to be[^9] (1) a person's own intuition, (2) some authority outside the person, or (3) groundless belief. The validity or reliability of moral beliefs would seem impossible to justify within physicalism, because the physicalist typically believes in no possible moral authorities other than other people, and other people have the same epistemic limitations on moral questions. If such an authority does not justify its moral beliefs by appealing to another authority, the only justifiable option left would seem to be human intuitions. Yet the typical physicalist believes that the human mind and cognitive faculties developed in the way described by the Darwinian [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md). Then intuition, too, is merely a product of aimless natural selection. An intuition developed by natural selection may perhaps tell us how to have the most offspring capable of reproducing[^10]. But it would seem impossible for an intuition that arose under the guidance of natural selection alone to give knowledge of what kind of action is truly good and right. Even if the physicalist did not believe that intuition arose under the guidance of natural selection, it is probably impossible to think of a way in which a purely physical ethical intuition could be epistemically reliable.
1. The motion of particles knows nothing of right and wrong. It would seem impossible for the physicalist to justify why one arrangement of particles would be good and another evil.

Under physicalism all morality seems to be entirely arbitrary and in the end even in conflict with physicalism. On a personal level, any given physicalist can of course have an internally coherent system of moral rules and can in principle live quite consistently even by Christian values. The physicalist worldview thus does not necessarily prevent its adherents from acting morally. In practice, however, it is impossible for the physicalist to justify right and wrong. Even though a physicalist may consider their own moral views enormously important to themselves and feel obligated to act on them, it is hard for them to give a purely rational justification for why a person should not just as well adopt a moral view that leads to atrocities such as headhunting or mass murder. The justification usually remains at the level of feeling, or else it may be rational relative to some goal. Physicalists, however, do not seem to have[^11] any justification for why these goals would be good or evil.

### Critique of physicalism (from the article by Leila Haaparanta and Heikki Koskinen)

The physicalist has to step clearly outside physics when claiming that all the phenomena we experience ultimately reduce to physics.

> So if an adherent of the scientific worldview wants to claim, for example, that all the phenomena occurring within our everyday experience and studied by the special sciences in their own fields ultimately reduce to physics, in making such a claim they have to step clearly outside physics itself. The situation is similar if biology or the theory of evolution is put forward as the foundation.
>
> — Haaparanta & Koskinen[^12]

Physicalist conviction is not a conclusion of scientific reasoning but one possible starting point for it. Interpreting scientific facts requires a frame of reference that includes assumptions about the origin of the universe and its ultimate nature.

Physicalists often give the impression that their materialism and physicalism is a scientific worldview, whereas the view of those who believe in God would be unscientific. Haaparanta, however, draws attention to the faith-based character of such a view:

> When a person who accepts the scientific worldview decides to rely on the scientific picture of the world and to commit to unbelief or atheism, they make, in a philosophical sense, a choice similar to that of a religious person.
>
> — Haaparanta & Koskinen[^12]

Justifying the descriptions that science offers requires stepping outside science, into the domain of philosophy.

> The so-called scientific worldview is a kind of choice of faith. Its adherent expresses a will to believe and makes a fundamental choice about what to build their trust on. Reducing all of reality to its material components is by nature unbelief toward God. It rejects the reality of God at the level of worldview faith. "Unbelief is understood as reluctance toward God or as detaching oneself from God." The atheist, too, makes a kind of "leap of faith"
>
> — Haaparanta & Koskinen[^13]

Atheists often try to conceal their own leap of faith by equating it with impartial scientific objectivity.

#### The place of doubt in the believer's life

Faith in God does not exclude doubt. On the contrary, faith in God frees one to doubt. Haaparanta & Koskinen[^14] describe this state of affairs aptly:

> Even if religious faith is understood as trust or certainty, this does not mean that doubt could not also be meaningful and genuine as an "internal attitude" of faith (a state that recurs from time to time). – – Doubt is a particular state of mind of the believer, perhaps an occasional state of distrust, which is significant for the believer's whole life. Doubt has a conceptual content that can usually be expressed as propositions.
>
> — Haaparanta & Koskinen[^14]

Haaparanta and Koskinen illuminate the significance of doubt in the believer's life by taking the example of Mother Teresa, according to whom doubt means an even greater closeness to God, because doubt makes concrete what it is to lose God.

> Although faith does not arise from the force of rational argument, rationality has its place in the believer's life and relationship with God through the believer's wish to understand their life and to see it as meaningful.
>
> — Haaparanta & Koskinen[^15]

### Physicalist heads of state

The heads of state who have most clearly represented physicalism are **[Joseph Stalin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josif_Stalin)**, **[Mao Zedong](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)** and **[Kim Il-sung](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Il-sung)**. Physicalism seems to make totalitarianism possible because:
1. those in power do not necessarily believe in any objective moral obligations that would limit the exercise of power,
1. the people share no common moral tune by which they would act on the wishes of those in power out of desire or a sense of duty, so in practice the people must be forced and/or brainwashed into line, and
1. within physicalism, in turn, it is hard to justify why brainwashing, lying or coercion would really be wrong (or why the whole question of right and wrong deserves any attention at all).
