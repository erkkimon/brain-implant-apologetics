# The logic of the design inference

*Topics: [Design theory](../topics/suunnitteluteoria.md).*

The **design inference** is a [design-theoretic](../articles/suunnitteluteoria.md) inference that leads to confirming or rejecting a design hypothesis about the object under study. This article deals mainly with the logic of the design inference as presented by [William Dembski](william-dembski.md) and assesses the criticism directed at it. Design inferences can also be made by other means than Dembski's method (at least [in a Bayesian way](../articles/bayesin-teoreema.md)), and despite the focus on Dembski's method, some of the points made here concern design inferences in general.

### Extending methodological naturalism

According to *methodological naturalism*, scientific explanation may appeal only to natural causes, ultimately to (unguided) chance and regularity (laws of nature, "necessity"). Design-theoretic methodology relaxes this restriction by admitting a third explanatory factor: intentional causation (intelligent, that is, genuine design). In design theory, intention is therefore an explanatory factor on the same fundamental level as chance or regularity[^1]. The core of the logic of the design inference is thus to take seriously the possibility that the object under study was intentionally caused, and to find out whether the object could be explained without this hypothesis. The design hypothesis is rejected whenever it can at all reasonably be rejected – but no more often. Criticism of design theory that neither understands nor takes this into account is therefore necessarily misguided.

Why must so simple a point be stressed separately? Because the criticism of the logic of the design inference comes mainly from the naturalist camp, whose attitude [Richard Lewontin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lewontin) has perhaps [pictured](http://www.drjbloom.com/Public%20files/Lewontin_Review.htm) most clearly[^2]. According to Lewontin, [science](../articles/tiede.md) is built so that only materialist explanations are allowed in it, because otherwise one would have to accept that "miracles may happen"; so, without any investigation, it is absolutely ruled out that "miracles" could happen, and only work that starts from this premise is accepted as "scientific research". On the other hand, even according to Lewontin it follows that, when needed[^3], the whole research community is fully prepared to accept uncritically materialist attempts at explanation however implausible and fanciful. A "miracle" is thus by definition something whose most natural explanation is not materialist, and by sticking to materialist explanations alone, "science" can as it were "prevent miracles from happening" by repeating the slogan that "everything has a natural explanation" – even if the plausibility of the explanations were, in every practical respect, a round zero[^4]! This is of course not objective[^5] natural-scientific research at all, but *applied materialist natural philosophy*.

The design-theoretic design inference sets a cautious, reasonable limit precisely on the boundless arbitrariness of materialist imagination in "scientific explanation", which the monopoly in the philosophy of science demanded for [naturalism](../glossary/sanasto.md#naturalismi), as Lewontin approvingly describes it, makes possible. Thinking that has deliberately hanged itself on its own materialism often has not even understood the logic of the design inference that it claims to have refuted. This should become quite clear in the other parts of this series of articles, where some of Dembski's critics are discussed.

### How intention, chance and regularity relate in design theory

From the point of view of design theory, the objects under study fall into three classes:
1. regular,
1. random, and
1. intentionally caused (intelligently designed).

These classes must be understood as *nested* in the sense that random objects may contain regularities besides randomness, and intentionally caused objects may in turn contain the effects of randomness and regularities[^6]; they are *alternatives* in the sense that objects classed as random can as a whole be explained with tolerable plausibility without appeal to intentional causes, and objects classed as regular without appeal to either intentional or random factors.

Besides this threefold division, a twofold one can also be used:
1. objects that arose unintentionally (through causes lacking intelligence or at least intent), and
1. objects that arose intentionally (through the deliberate action of intelligent causes).

Here the classes of regular and random objects from the threefold division are merged into the class of unintentionally arisen objects. Given the nesting explained above, this is unproblematic, because regular objects are a special case of random ones and as such their subclass (regularity can be understood as a [degenerate case](../glossary/sanasto.md#degeneroitua) of randomness, in which the probability of a necessary event is exactly 1).

Could all cases, following the same idea of nesting, finally be collapsed into one alternative, the widest class (the one that allows the most possibilities), that is, the intentional (which also leaves room for the effects of randomness and regularity)? In principle, yes[^7], but since the aim of the design inference is precisely to distinguish *evident intentional* causation from *possible unintentional* causation, this makes no sense within design theory. The design-theoretic design inference succeeds precisely in this, its proper task of distinguishing[^8].

### In what sense is Dembski's method reliable?

No scientific method can be reliable in the sense that using it could never lead to wrong results. To err is human, and anything can be used wrongly (intentionally or unintentionally). Nor can any method be reliable in the sense that it could be applied successfully in every situation: if essential information is missing, no reliable conclusions can be reached – "nothing comes of nothing". These general limitations of course apply to the logic of Dembski's design inference just as they apply to all other scientific methods.

Dembski's method is nevertheless reliable in the essential sense that, when it can be used at all and is also used correctly, it produces no "false positives": it does not classify an object that arose unintentionally as intentionally caused.

### The logic and arithmetic of reliability

The reliability of the design inference can be assessed on the one hand by weighing whether its theoretical grounds make sense, and on the other by judging intuitively whether the **universal probability bound** it uses is sufficient. The following tries to do both on a small scale.

#### An illustration

Suppose you have decided to play the following game of chance with a friend: you each put a one-euro coin on the table, then he tosses his own coin, and if it comes up heads he wins both euros, if tails, you get them. Since he gets to toss all the time, you in turn get to decide when the game ends. You know that if this is a genuine game of equal chances, situations where you are ahead will almost certainly come up while it lasts, and since you decide when to stop, you are almost sure that, unless you lose your nerve and stop while behind, the game will turn out profitable for you: you will get ahead now and then, and by stopping at such a point you will manage to get at least a little richer.

So you start playing, and he wins; you put another euro on the table, the result is the same, and this happens again and again. How many euros would you be willing to spend to keep playing such a game?

#### A short justification of the design inference's reliability

In a finite world everything has its limits. Among other things, there is only a finite number of possible objects of study (be they physical things or beings, events or something else). So when the object under study meets a specification whose probability of being met by chance is so small that it is more likely that no object so improbable arises unintentionally in the whole history of the world than that even one such object arises unintentionally (by unguided chance) even once, it is reasonable to assume that this object did not arise by chance. This is reasonable because, even if all objects this improbable were observed and classified as intentionally (non-randomly) arisen, it is more likely that all such conclusions are right than that even one of them is wrong. So since they are probably all right, however many of them are made (and in a finite world, of course, not infinitely many can be made), it is very, very improbable that one particular one of them (that is, the inference about the very object in question on a particular occasion) is wrong.

Dembski's method is of course more complex than this description, but the core of the justification of its reliability lies here. The rest is a matter of refining the details of the inference and of identifying and estimating the relevant quantitative properties of our observable universe, which makes it possible to fix the numerical value of the improbability required (the universal probability bound, Finnish *yleispätevä satunnaisuskottavuusraja*). Going through these details does not, however, affect the logic by which the reliability of the criterion is justified: in a world that is finite and can be estimated quantitatively, it is in any case possible to fix some concrete universal probability bound with the property described above. This means that the plausibility of methodological naturalism has the same limit: if objects are observed (in principle even one would suffice, but to exclude the possibility of error more than one of course adds to the persuasiveness of the result) that meet some specification whose probability of being met at random falls (on careful examination) below the universal probability bound, and which methodological naturalism nevertheless requires to be explained as somehow unintentionally caused, then one must give up either methodological naturalism – or else reasonable thinking.

#### Back to the illustration

The question, then, is this: if the situation is what it should be, heads and tails are equally probable outcomes and your opponent's winning streak cannot go on forever. If the game goes on long enough, he should therefore start losing at some point; even if you are now evidently losing this time, since you had such "bad luck" as to get so miserable a start, continuing the game should at some point at least reduce your current loss. If, on the other hand, it is a swindle (he has, say, got hold of a coin with heads on both sides), the longer you keep playing, the more foolish you are. At what point should you decide not to continue (and never again agree to play this game, at least not with the same friend)?

Classical statistics uses the following probability bounds: a probability of 5 % is almost significant grounds for rejecting the chance hypothesis (that is, the null hypothesis, here the assumption of a fair game), 1 % is significant and 1 per mille highly significant grounds. Turned into runs of heads, they would correspond to a run of 5, 7 and 10 tosses lost by you. Perhaps you would not have agreed to go on playing after the fourth or even the third loss. But if you decided to apply the criterion of Dembski's universal probability bound and stop only when this bound too is broken, that is, when your friend can by no reasonable thinking be playing fairly any more, you would get to go on playing much longer. 10 losses in a row would not be enough, nor 15... nor 20... nor 25... nor 30... nor 40... nor 50... nor 60; 75 would not be enough... nor 100... nor 125... nor 150... nor 175... nor 200. 240 would not be enough... nor 280... nor even 320. You would have to break a second and a third piggy bank... in fact you would be allowed to stop only after losing a full **500 €** in a row.

As was said, the design inference is ready to reject the design hypothesis whenever that can at all be reasonably possible, but no longer when it would quite unconditionally be absurd. That does not happen in a hurry.

### Criticism of the design inference's reliability

Has Dembski's pioneering work, then, made the naturalist camp concede the general principle that whoever makes a claim must prove it? Has anyone seriously set about examining how plausible the unintentional causes are, given their monopoly in research programmes drawn up on the terms of (methodological) naturalism? Quite the contrary: the naturalist camp has – as such quite as expected, as if reading its position of power in the sociology of science as its own collective infallibility, and appealing to that authority – mostly refused even the smallest revisions of its position, and has instead taken to presenting venomous criticism of Dembski's design inference. The quality, content and substantive weight of this criticism are sorted out [in an article of its own](../articles/tarkoituksellisuuspäättelyn-kritiikki.md).
