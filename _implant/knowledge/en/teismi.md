# Theism

*Topics: [Theism](../topics/teismi.md).*

**Theism** (from the [Greek](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kreikan_kieli) word *theos*, god), or **belief in God**,[^1] is the view that there exists a personal [god](jumala.md)[^2], distinct from the world, who has created the world and sustains it,[^3] has intervened and still intervenes in its events, revealing himself to people in words[^4] and deeds[^5], and at the end of time gathers people[^6] to be judged according to their deeds[^7].

### The prevalence of theism

#### Theism is humanity's innate conception of reality

According to the consensus of several recent studies, the theistic conception is natural to human beings.[^8] Consequently, the (new) atheist idea that "all children are born atheists, but most of them soon after birth become victims of religious brainwashing" has been refuted by empirical research.

#### The situation in Finland

In an international survey conducted in 1998, 81% of Finns said they believe in God.[^9] Considering the strongly anti-Christian character of our popular culture[^10] and the reputation for wisdom that active antitheists enjoy,[^11] this can be regarded as a surprisingly strong demonstration of how tenacious innate theistic thinking is, even in a hostile cultural environment.

### Theism as a bone of contention

Although theism is a starting point of thought given to human beings, it is also possible to rebel against it. Indeed, many religions, cultures and subcultures, at all times and all over the world, have rejected theism and tried to build a non-theistic model of thought and action. Regardless of which way of thinking is natural to human beings and which is not, every possible worldview can be both attacked and defended by presenting grounds and arguments that are meant to be, and are presented as, reasonable. So debate of this kind has been, and still is, conducted around theism too.

#### Arguments for theistic belief

Theistic belief can be argued for in many different ways. The most common arguments are the [cosmological argument](kosmologinen-argumentti.md), the [teleological argument](teleologinen-argumentti.md), the [moral argument](moraalinen-argumentti.md) and the [argument from abiogenesis](../articles/abiogeneesi.md).

#### The falsifiability of theism

<!--=== Teismi on periaatteessa falsifioitavissa ===
-->
According to the philosopher **[William Lane Craig](../articles/william-lane-craig.md)**, theism is by nature a position that is in principle falsifiable: it could be refuted by proving true, for example, one of the following claims:

* "Theism is logically incoherent." – One may try to show this, for example, in the following ways:
  * ["Can God create a stone so big that he himself could not lift it?"](jumalan-kaikkivoipuus.md)
  * [The problem of evil](../articles/pahan-ongelma.md): "If God existed, nothing evil would ever happen, but evil does happen."
* "The existence of the universe (without God) is metaphysically necessary."

The crux of the matter, then, is that theism is a position with real content which, if it were not true, could not only be rejected on faith but could first be shown to be false, and be rejected only once such a demonstration of falsity, i.e. falsification, had actually succeeded. Therefore the atheists' practice of rejecting theism even though they have not been able to show validly that it is false is intellectually untenable.[^12]

### Concepts related to theism

#### The atheists' "straw-man theism"

Atheists quarrel among themselves even about what [atheism itself is](ateismin-määritelmä.md), so they hardly agree fully on what "theism" should mean either. Nevertheless, one can observe an atheistic tendency to define "theism" on atheism's terms: as the logical complement of atheism, i.e. the set of all non-atheistic beliefs. Since atheists do not say they believe in "a god or gods", and since from their point of view "theism" is whatever "atheism" is not (the prefix *a-* expresses negation), it comes naturally to atheists to define theism as "belief in a god or gods".

Thus, according to the definition of "theism" favoured by atheists, one that concerns [existence](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/olemassaolo), i.e. an [ontological](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/ontologia) one, "theism is the [proposition](../glossary/sanasto.md#propositio) 'a god or gods exist'".[^13] If such a definition is accepted as the basis of the discussion, it gives atheists several advantages:

1. The way is now open for rhetoric according to which it is precisely the "theists" who have "claimed something" here, whereas the atheists have simply "just refrained from assenting to this claim of the theists", so that "atheism in itself claims nothing and therefore need not be proved in any way (nor can it even have any logical or practical consequences); rather, the whole [burden of proof](../articles/todistuksen-taakka.md) rests on the theists, so the atheists can concentrate on mere indifference towards theism or, alternatively, on mere criticism of theistic arguments".
1. If someone actually declares himself an "'I believe in a god or gods' theist", he has at the same time declared himself a person who apparently does not really know himself what he believes in (a god or gods – or perhaps, to be on the safe side, both a god and gods?). So he has lost the game from the outset, before the discussion has even begun: "If you can believe in gods just as well as in a god, why not believe in bogeymen and goblins too while you are at it?" – On this basis, substantive discussion is impossible, which in turn typically works in the atheists' favour, because substantive discussion is exactly where they are not strong.
1. By accepting the atheistic definition of "theism", one loses the possibility of holding to the meaning of "theism" in Christian theology and in precise philosophy of religion. One then cannot even name precisely and intelligibly the [ontological](../glossary/sanasto.md#ontologia) position one was supposed to defend as a Christian, and so cannot properly argue for it either; the whole discussion remains muddled.
1. The lower the level to which the atheists manage to drag these discussions, by this conceptual muddle among other means, the more credibility they can hope to win for the propagandistic position that "religiosity" as such is in any case mere conceptual muddle that makes no sense, at least from the point of view of "the ordinary person", and that "atheism, by contrast, is therefore little more than the natural effort of every sensible and moderate person to keep out of such a muddle".

#### The precise definition of theism, its subordinate and superordinate concepts, and its logical alternatives

**[Seppo A. Teinonen's](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seppo_A._Teinonen)** *Teologian sanakirja*[^14] defines it as follows:
* "**theism** (Gr. theos, god), philos., theol., relig. stud.: the view that there exists a personal and –> transcendent God who has created the world and sustains it. The term was first used by R. Cudworth in 1678. *Theist*, an adherent of theism; *theistic*, in accordance with theism."
* "**transcendence** (Lat. transcendere, to step over, to rise above), being on the other side, being beyond, the beyond, (God's) being beyond the world (and not in it, as in –> immanence), (God's) being outside the world. ..."

Also quite good[^15] is the definition given in the book *Ateismi*[^16] (p. 16):

* **Theism:** belief in an omnipotent, omniscient, all-good, personal God who created the universe, takes an interest in the events of the world and has given a special revelation to human beings.

To understand the precise definition of theism properly, one also needs to know what theism so defined *is* – a subspecies of [supernaturalism](../glossary/sanasto.md#supranaturalismi) and, somewhat surprisingly, specifically also of [monotheism](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoteismi) (and not the other way round), and the common superordinate concept of [unitarianism](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarismi) and [trinitarianism](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolminaisuusoppi) – and what it *is not*, i.e. which positions differ from it. For the logical complement of theism so defined, i.e. the set of all positions outside it, includes much more besides [naturalism](../glossary/sanasto.md#naturalismi), [materialism](../glossary/sanasto.md#materialismi) and [atheism](../glossary/sanasto.md#ateismi), for example [deism](../glossary/sanasto.md#deismi)[^17] (itself also a subspecies of monotheism), [dualism](../glossary/sanasto.md#dualismi), [polytheism](../glossary/sanasto.md#polyteismi) and [pantheism](../glossary/sanasto.md#panteismi).

### Theism, Christianity and Christian apologetics

Christianity falls within theism, but not all theism (for example unitarianism, such as Islam and the doctrine of Jehovah's Witnesses) is Christianity.

From the standpoint of Christian apologetics, the concept of theism is especially significant in that, in practice, it divides non-Christian views fairly precisely[^18] into "pagan" ones (based on sources outside God's special revelation), which typically *are not* theistic, and "heretical" ones (based on a misuse of God's special revelation), which typically *are* theistic but antitrinitarian.

Because of this division, against views outside theism Christian apologetics can typically appeal to many arguments from outside the Bible,[^19] whereas when it meets heretical views that fall within theism, the point at issue usually becomes the true content of God's revelation, and then valid and convincing biblical arguments, among other things, are needed. – Somewhat paradoxically, this state of affairs also means that many arguments for theism, even central ones, of Christian apologetics can serve as they stand in the apologia of heretical views as well.[^20] Christian apologetics should therefore see clearly the whole range of non-Christian views, so that it can help people from every non-Christian background, as convincingly as possible, "into their joy", that is, into the full light of the gospel of Christ.
