# Common descent

*Topics: [Theory of evolution](../topics/evoluutioteoria.md).*


*The tree of life*

|  |
|---|
| *Image: The kinship of organisms is usually compared to a tree in which new species branch off from the main limbs as they develop. When the kinship of groups of organisms is compared on the basis of several genes, however, representing the differences with a tree model turns out to be difficult, and one has to resort to a web-like representation. (Image: Doolittle 2000)* |

According to **common descent**, all organisms have descended from a common ancestral form, either guided or unguided. In practice, common descent means that all living things are related to one another. Common descent is often confused with the [theory of evolution](evoluutioteoria.md), because it is a necessary part of the theory of evolution. Common descent by itself, however, takes no position on the mechanism by which organisms have changed from the common ancestral form into their present diversity, whereas according to the theory of evolution the mechanism is the known [mechanisms of variation](perinnöllisen-muuntelun-mekanismit.md).

### Evidence for common descent

*Image: In the popular media the tree of life is usually presented in a heavily simplified form (Image: HS 2008). The unambiguous classification and kinship of organisms shown in the image is explained by the fact that the comparison was made on the basis of only one gene. A reader unfamiliar with the subject, however, gets a misleading impression of how clear the "tree of life" is (compare the image at the beginning, which is from 2000).*

#### Homologies
By comparing the [anatomy](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/anatomia) of organisms, one observes similarities in their structures. For example, the body of an insect always has a head, an abdomen and a thorax; insects have three pairs of legs and usually two pairs of wings. Likewise, in mammals the human arm, the foreleg of a cat or a horse, the wing of a bat and the flipper of a seal follow the same basic plan, and their bones can be identified as the same almost bone for bone. Common descent is defended by arguing that these traits must be due to their having been inherited from common ancestral forms, since the similarities are far too great to be accidental. This fact, however, does not support common descent alone; a common designer is also a reasonable explanation for similar structures that perform a similar task. Homologies can thus be used as an argument both for descent and for a common designer.

##### Similarities in embryonic development

*Main article: [Recapitulation theory](../articles/rekapitulaatioteoria.md)*

Organisms also show similarities at the various stages of individual development, but these are not substantially greater than one would expect from the finished structures. For example, in all vertebrates the central nervous system that develops at an early stage has roughly the same shape, and for that reason they all look somewhat alike, resembling to some extent a [cashew nut.](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/cashewpähkinä)

The similarity could be explained by the different groups of vertebrates having inherited from common ancestors a way of developing that still repeats itself in them. On the other hand, the similarities may also point to a common designer and common functional requirements. The [exaggerated similarities of embryos](../articles/rekapitulaatioteoria.md) used to prove the theory of evolution are one of the greatest admitted frauds in science.

##### Homologies and convergences

*Main article: [Convergence](konvergenssi.md)*

To prove the homology argument, it is usually shown that similar structures recur in the tree of descent with logical regularity. There are, however, also many similarities that do not fit the tree; in their case one speaks of analogous structures, which according to the theory of evolution arose independently of one another through so-called convergent evolution. In these cases the similarity is thus not due to the structure's history of descent after all, but must be explained by the structure's similar function, in which case it would have developed through natural selection by adapting to a similar [ecological niche](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/ekologinen_lokero). The possibility of convergent evolution also makes taxonomy based on common descent very challenging, since one cannot be sure whether a shared trait is a sign of kinship or a case of convergence. In some cases convergences are so similar that biologists cannot tell whether it is a homology or a convergence until the organisms' other characteristics are examined. In each case, however, one must already "know" the evolutionary relationship between the species before one can know whether it is a homology or a convergence. The possibility of convergent evolution therefore cuts the ground from under the homology argument for functional structures.

[Ethology](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etologia) is the branch of biology that studies animal behavior. It reveals similarities in the nest-building methods of different forms, such as [social insects](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/aitososiaalisuus), ants, wasps and bees.
Yet functional similarities, still less behavioral ones, do not prove common descent; after all, not all people who slurp coffee from the saucer are closely related to one another either.

##### Imperfect homologous structures

*Main article: [Imperfect design](../articles/epätäydellinen-suunnittelu-argumentti.md)*

[Vestigial organs](../glossary/sanasto.md#surkastuma) are organs that have lost their original function during their history of descent and have no other function, or whose function is too slight for the structure. Vestigial organs have degenerated because they are unnecessary and even harmful. Examples of vestigial organs are the eyes of blind cave fish and the wings of insects on windy islands. Vestigial structures put forward as evidence of common descent include the abdominal bones of whales, which resemble the pelvis and leg bones of land mammals and reptiles. Likewise the tailbone of humans and chimpanzees, since the ancestor of humans and chimpanzees can be assumed to have once had a tail but to have lost it as unnecessary. Other examples are the remnants of hind-leg bones found in snakes and the remnants of teeth in the jaws of [baleen whales](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/hetulavalaat).

The human tailbone, for example, is a vital attachment point for the pelvic muscles. The phrase "vestigial organ" often merely covers up our ignorance of the organ's purpose. The human appendix, which was earlier thought to be a useless vestigial remnant, is now known to be a functioning component of the immune system.<!--lähteitä!-->

##### Molecular biological homologies

*Image: Proteins such as cytochrome c can be used to build protein trees. Building such trees, however, becomes more difficult and more contradictory the more organisms and proteins are compared.*

[Molecular biology](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/molekyylibiologia) is a field of biology that seeks, for example, to infer the kinship and developmental history of different organisms by comparing [homologous](../glossary/sanasto.md#homologia) genes or homologous molecules between organisms. The similarities of living beings extend all the way to the molecular level. All organisms use the same basic chemical structures in their metabolism. This makes it possible, for example, to analyze protein and DNA/RNA sequences, from which molecular trees can be built. It has been noticed, however, that the more different protein and gene sequences are analyzed, the more difficult it becomes to build consistent molecular trees. Comparing different molecules leads to different conclusions. Moreover, as the differences between the compared molecules grow, the room for interpretation grows rapidly.[^1]

When molecular biological homologies are used to prove common descent, the appeal is most often to so-called pseudogenes, that is, copies of actual genes that seem to have no functional effect. For example, the [design theorist](suunnitteluteoria.md) [Michael Behe](../articles/michael-behe.md) presents pseudogene homologies as evidence for common descent in his book [The Edge of Evolution](the-edge-of-evolution-kirja.md):

> When two lineages share a common, apparently arbitrary genetic error, the evidence for the common descent of these lineages is compelling, just as the evidence for plagiarism becomes overwhelming when one notices that one writer has made the same unusual spelling mistakes as another, using exactly the same words. Evidence of this kind can be seen in the genomes of humans and chimpanzees. For example, both humans and chimpanzees have a broken copy of a gene that in other mammals takes part in producing vitamin C. Because of this, humans and chimpanzees cannot produce vitamin C themselves. If this mutation became fixed in the genome of the ancestor of these two species and was then passed on to both descendant species, that would neatly explain the situation.

> More compelling evidence for a common ancestor of humans and other primates is provided by their hemoglobin – not only working hemoglobin, but also broken hemoglobin genes.[^2] In one part of the human genome there are five different genes for different proteins that serve, at different stages of development (from embryo to adult), as the second (beta-type) chain of hemoglobin. These include the gene that produces the beta chain itself, two almost identical copies of the gamma chain (which appears in fetal hemoglobin), and some others. Chimpanzees have exactly the same genes in exactly the same order. In the region between the two gamma genes and a gene that works after birth, human DNA has a broken gene (a so-called "pseudogene") that closely resembles a working beta-chain gene but has features in its base sequence that prevent it from coding successfully for a protein.

> The chimpanzee has a very similar pseudogene at the same place in its DNA. At the beginning of the human pseudogene there are two particular changes in two nucleotide letters that seem to switch the gene off. The chimpanzee gene has exactly the same changes. Reading a little further along the gene, the human pseudogene has a deletion mutation in which one particular letter is missing. For technical reasons the deletion irreversibly scrambles the gene's coding. The same letter is missing from the chimpanzee gene. Toward the end of the human pseudogene another letter is missing. It is missing from the chimpanzee pseudogene too.

> The same errors, in the same gene and at the same place, in the DNA of both humans and chimpanzees. If these mutational errors were retained in a common ancestor whose descendants later gave rise to these two modern species, that would very smoothly explain why they are found in both species. It is hard to imagine how there could be stronger evidence for a common ancestor of chimpanzees and humans.

> The strong evidence provided by pseudogenes points much further than just to human origins. Despite the remaining puzzles,[^3] there is no reason to doubt that Darwin was right on this point: all organisms on earth are biologically related to one another.
>
> — Michael J. Behe, The Edge of Evolution[^4]

In the light of newer knowledge, however, arguments based on pseudogenes are questionable. According to a review article published in the journal RNA Biology in 2012, pseudogenes have been found to have numerous functions, particularly in regulating the production of their protein-coding counterparts.[^5] When one further considers how drastically hemoglobin production depends on cell type, it is clear that strong regulation is involved.[^6]

> The molecular clock method is not without problems: calibrating the clocks can be difficult, and the "ticking rate" of the clocks depends, among other things, on whether a particular gene sequence is under so-called selective pressure. The best molecular clocks are so-called [junk DNA](../articles/tilke-dna.md) regions, which do not code for any protein and thus are not expressed in the [phenotype](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/fenotyyppi). DNA regions that code for a working protein (that is, *genes*) generally change much more slowly, because mutations in a working gene are for the most part harmful, and natural selection therefore removes them from the population.

<!--lähde?-->

> Another method for working out these kinship relations from genetic sequences is based on so-called *plagiarised errors*. Junk DNA regions contain copies of many working genes that have been damaged so that they no longer work. From such errors shared by species one can infer that the errors already existed before the species diverged. An example of such a case is a mutation shared by primates because of which they cannot make vitamin C, unlike almost all other mammals. (Guinea pigs cannot make vitamin C themselves either, but in them the mutation is different.)

<!--lähde?-->

It has since been noticed that several alleged "shared errors" are due to correlated mutations that commonly occur often (so-called *hotspots*), or that the alleged errors have a useful function.[^7][^8]

> With molecular biology it was noticed that molecules, too, evolve in the same way as somatic structures. The closer two species are to each other, the more similar their molecules are as well. In many cases the ambiguity of the morphological data has raised doubts, but comparing molecules has revealed the true relationship of the species. Molecular biology is today one of the most important sources of information in the study of [phylogenetic](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/fylogenia) relationships.

In reality, trees built with molecules differ significantly from those built with morphology.[^9][^10][^11][^12][^13]

#### Fossil evidence
[Paleontology](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/paleontologia) studies fossils. The remains of animals and plants that lived in a given period are found petrified in geological strata dated as very old indeed. According to common descent, an earlier stratum should contain, in a continuous way, simpler and even "half-finished" forms of the fossils found in the next stratum.

For example, birds and mammals are assumed to have evolved from reptiles. In 1861 a fossil of *Archaeopteryx* (a feathered "bird-lizard") was found, which is presented, especially in popularizations of the theory of evolution, as an intermediate form in the evolution of birds. On critical examination, however, it can be stated that Archaeopteryx cannot clearly be seen as an intermediate form between, for example, dinosaurs and birds, since Archaeopteryx has a mosaic of features from several different groups of organisms, so it is a [mosaic form](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/mosaiikkimuoto). Archaeopteryx has characteristics of four different reptile groups ([ichthyosaurs](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kalaliskot), [Crocodilia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/crocodilia), [Saurischia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/saurischia), [Ornithischia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linnunlantioiset)), which is why it cannot be considered the ancestor of modern birds.[^14]

Fossils that fill an empty gap are called missing links. The most complete [fossil lineages](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/fossiililinja) are considered to be the line from mammal-like reptiles to mammals, the evolution of whales, and the evolution of the horse family from the ancestral species (*Eohippus*) to the modern horse (*Equus*). Several different developmental series have been proposed for human evolution.[^14]

### Individual development and the origin of organs

When a multicellular organism develops from an [egg cell](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/munasolu), different types of tissue sooner or later begin to develop and organs form. The process has many stages, and the successive stages are directed by genes.

Different animals can have very similar-looking organs. For example, [insects](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/hyönteiset) and [birds](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/linnut) have wings, [dolphins](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/delfiini), [penguins](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/pingviini) and [fish](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kala) have fins, and so on. On the other hand, different animal species can have very different-looking organs, such as [fins](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalojen_anatomia), [wings](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/siipi) and [hands](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/käsi), which despite their differences can have a common origin.

> Similar appearance and the same function do not necessarily mean the same evolutionary history. If animals with similar organs that perform the same function do not share the same developmental (embryological) background, the organs are **analogous**. The wings of insects and birds, or the eyes of mammals and octopuses, are analogous organs.

Perhaps the most popular, grossly simplified claim in favor of the theory of evolution is that a species' old developmental path shows, at least in part, in the embryonic development of every individual (see the article [recapitulation theory](../articles/rekapitulaatioteoria.md)).

On the basis of the theory of evolution it was once assumed that, for example, homology in an organism's [phenotype](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/fenotyyppi) also implies homology in individual development and at the gene level. In some cases, however, similar organs have a different genetic code behind them. The situation can be compared to the outward and functional similarity of OpenOffice and Microsoft Office, although their code is very different.

Correspondingly, in the case of analogy, homology was not assumed to appear in individual development or at the gene level. Both claims have since been called into question. A closer analysis reveals that, for example, homologous organs arise in individual development by different routes in different organisms. In the case of analogous organs, on the other hand, homology has been found at the gene level. For example, the eyes of different organisms have been regarded as a classic example of analogous organs. Later, however, it was found that the development of these analogous organs is directed by the same regulatory genes.

> And this is what has generally been observed: the more evidence that species are close to each other, the more homologies of organs are found as well. The kinship required by the theory of evolution, that is, "common descent", is the only theory that **requires** this, and its absence would be a fatal piece of counter-evidence to the theory of evolution.

> Also interesting are the so-called rudimentary or vestigial organs: in humans, among others, the wisdom tooth and the appendix. Darwin himself mentioned the vestigial hind legs of boa snakes. In other snakes these are found only at the embryonic stage and no longer in the adult. Birds, too, have tooth buds at the embryonic stage (compare [Archaeopteryx](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeopteryx) <!-- red link: Archaeopteryx -->), which then disappear.

### Common ancestor

*Image: The universality of the genetic code was long regarded as the strongest evidence for a common ancestral form of organisms. Since then, contrary to expectations, deviations were found in the code assumed to be universal. Today dozens of different codes are known.[^15*

]

In the light of astronomical and [geophysical](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/geofysiikka) evidence, the earth is estimated to have formed about 4.57 billion years ago. According to estimates, the earth became habitable about 3.8 billion years ago, which is also when the first organisms are thought to have arisen. We do not know what the first organisms looked like. Life is believed to have begun with the emergence of the first replicating molecules, such as [RNA ribozymes](../articles/rna-maailma.md), which were able to take matter and energy from the surrounding non-living molecules and from the sun's radiation. Many competing hypotheses have been put forward about how this happened, but despite decades of effort the spontaneous origin of life, [abiogenesis](../articles/abiogeneesi.md), is still a mystery. The biological theory of evolution proper enters the picture only when a self-replicating primordial organism exists.

Darwin proposed that all species of organisms have a common ancestor; every organism has evolved from the same primordial organism, which lived at least 3.5 billion years ago. In support of this hypothesis, popularizations of the theory of evolution usually point to the similar basic chemical structures of the metabolism of organisms, such as nucleotides ([DNA](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA)/[RNA](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA)), [amino acids](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/aminohappo), [nucleotides](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/nukleotidi), [sugars](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/sokerit) and [fatty acids](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/rasvahapot). The universality of the genetic code was also regarded as strong evidence for a common ancestor. For example, [Petter Portin](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petter_Portin), emeritus professor of genetics at the University of Turku, makes this claim in his book celebrating Charles Darwin's double anniversary year:

> The basic metabolism and structure of cells are alike in all organisms, and indeed one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the theory of evolution is that the genetic code is universal, that is, the same in all organisms, whether the organism in question is a virus, a bacterium, a yeast, a chanterelle, a birch, a blue whale or a human. The phenomenon is scientifically very hard to understand other than in the light of the theory of evolution.
> Petter Portin and Timo Vuorisalo
>
> — Evoluutio NYT! (2008)[^16]

Evolutionary researchers indeed long regarded a change in the code as impossible. Since then, contrary to expectations, significant exceptions to the code have been found (already several years ago)<!--Milloin?--> (for example, in [mitochondria](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/mitokondrio) a change in a *Stop* [codon](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/kodoni)), and several genetic codes are now known.[^15]

> An easy misunderstanding is that this "common forefather/foremother" was alone in the world. More probably it was just one individual in an ocean already full of life. All the others, however, failed to continue their line to the present day. Another misunderstanding is that it was the *first* organism on earth. A more realistic picture is that it was the end result of a long process of development whose earliest stages were not necessarily even unambiguously "alive". In the early stages life may have arisen again and again, but with present knowledge we do not know very much about that, because, for example, it is almost impossible to find undisturbed layered rock from 3.8–3.5 billion years ago, from the earth's earliest times (none is known today), and an oxygen-free atmosphere would be needed in addition to other unusual conditions. Humans today have no knowledge at all of the first 300 million years of life, and laboratories have not yet been able to produce life, but it is probably not entirely impossible as technology and knowledge develop. Rich bacterial communities have been found already in rocks 3.5 billion years old.

> All the first pioneers of life, however, had to solve many problems, the greatest being how to obtain energy and how to copy themselves. DNA molecules are regarded as essential for copying, but there is at present no adequate theory of how DNA was selected for that task. Its role is, however, believed to have been secondary at first, preceded by an [RNA world](../articles/rna-maailma.md) in which protein synthesis took place and which lacked the efficiency of DNA-mediated protein synthesis.
